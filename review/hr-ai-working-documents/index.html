AI in HR: a decision test and a first-90-days sequence

Three short working documents: how I decide whether a piece of work should get AI at all, how I would sequence the first ninety days, and where I make the machine stop and hand back to a person.

1. The four-question test 2. The test, applied 3. The first ninety days 4. Where the machine stops

What these are

Four short working documents on how I decide where AI belongs in HR work, how I would sequence a first ninety days, and where I make a system stop and hand back to a person. Written from what I have built and run, not from a framework.

Document oneShould this work get AI at all?

Most of the value in this job is in the things you decide not to automate. Every function I have seen start this work begins with a list of use cases. The list is never the hard part. The hard part is having a test you will actually apply when a use case is attractive and wrong.

These are the four questions I run, in this order. The order matters: the first two decide whether it is worth doing, and the second two decide whether it is safe to do.

1. Is the work high in volume and regular in shape?

Not just frequent. Regular. The same inputs arriving in roughly the same form, over and over. If every instance is genuinely different, a person is doing judgment work and the volume is an illusion.

If no, stop here. The effort will exceed the saving and you will have spent political capital on a small win.

2. If it gets it wrong, does somebody catch it?

A mistake that surfaces in the next step of the process is cheap. A mistake that leaves the building, reaches a candidate or an employee, or quietly settles into a record nobody re-reads is not. This is a question about the process around the tool, not about the tool's accuracy.

If errors are not caught downstream, you need a checking step designed in before go-live, not added after the first incident.

3. Is there one named person who owns the output?

Not a team. A person, who would notice if the quality drifted and has the standing to stop it. Automated work with diffuse ownership degrades slowly and invisibly, because everybody assumes somebody else is watching.

If you cannot name the person, you are not ready to build it. Finding that person is the first task, not a later one.

4. Does somebody's livelihood ride on the decision?

Hiring, promotion, pay, performance rating, discipline, exit. If the answer is yes, the machine assists and a person decides. Always. The machine can gather, summarise, surface, compare and prepare. It does not choose, and it does not rank people into an order that a human then rubber-stamps, because a ranking nobody overturns is a decision wearing a recommendation's clothes.

This one is not a scoring question. It is a line. In the EU it is also a regulatory line, since AI used in recruitment, selection and promotion decisions is treated as high-risk, with obligations that attach before a system goes live.

Why this order

Questions one and two are about whether the work is worth doing. Questions three and four are about whether it is safe to do. Running them in that order means you stop cheap: most candidates fail question one, and you never spend a governance conversation on something that was never worth building.

Document twoThe test, applied to the usual candidates

The same four questions, run across the work that comes up first in every HR function considering this. The point is not that the answers are universal. The point is that the test produces different answers for work that looks superficially similar, which is what a test is for.

The work Volume and shape Errors caught Named owner Livelihood Verdict
Answering policy questions for employees and managers High, and the questions repeat Yes, the asker knows when an answer is wrong Straightforward, the policy owner No Start here
Scheduling and coordination across interview panels and time zones High, and highly regular Yes, immediately and visibly Recruiting operations No Start here
Drafting job descriptions and internal communications Moderate, shape is regular Yes, a human sends it The hiring manager or the writer No Start here
Summarising engagement and exit feedback into themes Periodic but regular Partly, a wrong theme is hard to spot Needs naming, often nobody owns it No, but it shapes decisions that do Worth doing, with a check
Workforce reporting that leaders make decisions from High, and very regular Often no, a wrong number looks like a right one Must be named explicitly Indirectly High value, needs the check
Screening and ranking candidates High and regular Rarely, a rejected candidate never comes back to tell you Recruiting, but the decision is diffuse Yes Assists only, never decides
Performance calibration and promotion readiness Periodic, shape varies a lot No Diffuse by design Yes Assists only, never decides
The pattern worth noticing

The work that is safest to automate is mostly not the work people get excited about. Screening is the first thing every function reaches for and it is the one place the test says slow down. Meanwhile answering the same policy question four hundred times a month is unglamorous, entirely safe, and returns real hours to the team immediately. Starting with the boring row is how you earn the right to attempt the interesting one.

Document threeThe first ninety days, as a sequence

Not a task list. Each step exists to produce the thing the next step needs, which is why the order cannot be shuffled. A plan you can reorder freely is a list of activities, and it will not survive contact with a real organisation.

  1. Find where the hours actually go

    Ask the people doing the work what takes their week, and read what the systems already record about volume and cycle time. Four weeks, overlapping with everything else.

    Which produces A ranked list of where HR's time actually sits. This is the only honest source of candidate use cases. A list assembled any other way is a list of what is fashionable.

  2. Run that list through the four-question test

    Publicly, with the reasoning visible, including for the ones that fail.

    Which produces A much shorter list, and a written reason for every no. The reasons are the more valuable half: they are what tells a worried team that this is being done carefully, and they are what stops the same rejected idea returning every quarter.

  3. Take the survivors to the people who would be affected, before anything is built

    Including whoever formally represents them. Not a communications plan after the decision, a conversation before it.

    Which produces Either agreement to pilot one, or the specific objection that has to be designed around. Both are useful. Only one of them is a surprise six months later, and the surprise version costs far more than the conversation would have.

  4. Pilot exactly one, with the oversight built in from day one

    One use case, one team, a defined period. Human review, record-keeping and the stopping rule designed in at the start rather than added in the week before launch.

    Which produces Adoption evidence from a real rollout, and a governance pattern that came from practice instead of a template. A governance document written before anyone has used anything is a guess.

  5. Use that evidence to choose the next two

    The pilot's own numbers, not a vendor's.

    Which produces A roadmap the business believes, because it is built on its own data, and the first real piece of a single front door for employees and managers rather than a scatter of disconnected tools.

What I would expect to be wrong

A good third of this will change once I see the actual data and meet the actual constraints. The shape is what I would hold to: find the hours before choosing the tools, decide what not to do before deciding what to do, talk to the affected people before building rather than after, and let one real pilot rather than a slide deck choose what comes next.

One sequencing question sits underneath all of it and has to be answered early rather than discovered late. If a major platform programme is already running, this work either waits for it, runs alongside it on today's systems, or builds on what lands. Those are three different ninety-day plans. Choosing the wrong one does not slow the work down, it strands it.

Document fourWhere the machine stops and a person decides

This is the list I wrote for my own systems before they went into daily use, rather than after something went wrong. It is the part I would bring to an employer unchanged, because the categories do not change when the organisation gets bigger. Only the volume does.

Anything falling into one of these categories stops and waits for a person. Everything else runs.

The categoryWhy it is on the list
It reaches a real person outside the system
An email, a message, a scheduled meeting, anything that arrives in somebody's day		 It cannot be recalled. The cost of a wrong one is paid by a person who did not choose to be part of the experiment, and it is paid in trust, which is the slowest thing to rebuild
It is published under a name
Anything that carries a person's or the organisation's name in public		 Wrong content is seen before it can be pulled back, and the correction never travels as far as the original
It touches money
Pay, benefits elections, invoices, anything with a number attached to a person's income		 Errors here are felt personally and immediately, and they are the fastest way to lose a function's credibility
It destroys or moves data irreversibly
Deletions, bulk changes, migrations, anything without a straightforward undo		 The class of mistake where the recovery is harder than the original task, and where you often discover the problem months later
It changes who can access what
Permissions, credentials, system access, data-sharing arrangements		 An access mistake is silent by nature. Nothing breaks, so nothing alerts you, and the exposure runs until somebody happens to look
Somebody's livelihood rides on it
Hiring, promotion, rating, discipline, exit		 Question four of the test above. This is a line rather than a risk calculation, and in the EU it is also a regulatory one
It would weaken one of these rules
Any change that removes, narrows or carves an exception into the list itself		 The one that people forget, and the one that matters most over time. A system permitted to relax its own limits will eventually do so, quietly, for a good reason, on a busy day
The two things this list teaches that a policy document does not

First, adding a category is cheap and removing one is expensive, so the honest version is written early, while nothing is inconvenient yet. Every category above was easy to accept before it ever cost anything, and at least one of them has been inconvenient since.

Second, a stopping rule only works if stopping is easy. If the review step is slow or awkward, people route around it and the list becomes decoration. The design question is not what should require review, it is how to make the review take seconds.
