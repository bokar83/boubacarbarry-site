2. The skill — what it actually is (I downloaded and opened it)

The download link runs through their email tracker to a Google Drive file called desktop-organizer.skill — a 3 KB zip containing a single file, SKILL.md. There are no scripts, no code, no dependencies. It is a Claude skill in the plainest form: a Markdown instruction sheet that tells Claude how to do a job.

What it does

It is a computer-use skill. It drives Claude (in the desktop app's "cowork" / computer-control mode) to: request Finder access, screenshot the desktop, group loose files into ALL-CAPS folders (CONTENT, BUSINESS, SOCIAL PROOF, OPERATIONS, PROJECTS), present the plan and wait for a yes, then create folders and drag files in one at a time, clean up the layout, and brief the user on how to maintain it. Seven numbered steps, an error-handling section, and one worked example.

How it is packaged as a lead magnet (the reusable UX lesson)

  • One file, tiny. No install, no repo, no account. Download, drag into Claude, run. The whole product is a 3 KB text file — the value is in the instructions, not code.
  • The email carries the install UX, not the file. The "open Claude desktop > new cowork task > drop it in > run now" line is the actual onboarding. The file assumes zero setup knowledge.
  • Trigger-primed for onboarding. The skill description literally says "Trigger proactively at the start of any AI onboarding session" — they built the free gift so it also seeds their own paid onboarding flow. The lead magnet and the product are the same asset.
Two things leaked that you should learn from. Their example plan lists a folder named Magnetic-Agents (an internal codename) and a folder Testimonials for Rob (a real client's first name). Both are baked into the free file everyone downloads. Lesson: when you package your skill, scrub every internal codename and client name first — this is your existing hard rule, and here is a live example of a competitor breaking it in public.
Honest weakness in their choice: it is a Mac-only computer-control skill. To run it, a non-technical person needs the Claude desktop app, cowork enabled, and to grant Finder / screen-control access, then watch Claude drag files one at a time. That is more friction than "runs in ten minutes" implies, and it is fragile (their own error-handling section admits drags fail). You can beat this with a lower-friction format — see section 6.
3. The Anthropic stat — verified, and corrected

Their claim: "The newest data from Anthropic shows more than 90% of AI agent use is not coding at all."

What Anthropic actually publishes (Anthropic Economic Index, March 2026 report): "Coding remains the most common use on our platforms, with tasks associated with Computer and Mathematical occupations accounting for 35% of conversations on Claude.ai." That means about 65% of usage is non-coding — roughly two-thirds, not 90%+. In less mature markets coding is even higher (over half of usage in India). Anthropic does not publish a clean "90% of agent use is non-coding" figure anywhere I can find.

Verdict: the DIRECTION is true and it is genuinely Anthropic-sourced — most AI use is not coding, and that is a legitimate, powerful reframe for a non-technical audience. But the specific 90% number is inflated and not defensible. If you use this move (you should), cite the real one: "Anthropic's own usage data shows coding is only about a third of how people use Claude — the rest is the everyday business work that eats your week." That respects your no-unverified-numbers rule and is still just as disarming.

Source: Anthropic Economic Index report, March 2026 (anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report).

4. The free→paid bridge — how they connect the gift to the sale

The bridge is one sentence and it is worth studying because it does not feel like a pitch:

"And when you are ready to build the rest of the team, that is what we do together. Earlier this month we ran a full workshop on hiring your first AI employee and building a workforce around it."  →  Watch the workshop

Why the bridge works

  • It rides the momentum of the free win. The reader is told to run the skill and feel the leverage. The bridge catches them at the exact moment that feeling lands: "when you are ready to build the rest of the team."
  • The free gift and the paid offer are the same shape. Free = your first AI employee (desktop). Paid = the rest of the AI team (workshop). The gift is a sample of the product, not a random freebie. That continuity is the whole trick.
  • Reader-paced, not seller-paced. "When you are ready" hands control to the reader. No deadline, no scarcity in this email — the scarcity/urgency lives elsewhere in the sequence, so this email stays low-pressure.
Your version already has this shape and it is stronger: your free gift → your July 30 workshop ("hire AI without getting burned") → consulting. What you are missing is the felt first win at the top. Give them that, and this bridge sentence does the rest.

6. THE BIG QUESTION — should your free gift become a runnable skill?

Short answer: yes. Upgrade the checklist to one real, runnable skill. But do NOT copy their format. Build a lower-friction one that sits inside your moat.

Why upgrade

  • The felt first win is the entire reason their funnel outperforms a checklist. You cannot get that from a PDF.
  • It is a differentiator and it is on-brand: an operator who builds real tools in the open, handing you a real tool.
  • It ties directly to your workshop promise ("hire your first AI employee"). The gift becomes a sample of the workshop, exactly like theirs.

Why NOT copy their exact skill

Their skill is Mac-only and needs computer-control access, cowork, and screen permissions — too much friction for a non-technical HR person, and fragile. If your free gift fails to run for half the people who download it, it does the opposite of building belief. Go text-in / text-out instead: a skill that takes something the user pastes in and hands back something useful, running anywhere Claude runs, in two minutes, no permissions.

Recommended skill (best fit for your ICP + your proof)

"Your first AI hire: the recruiting coordinator"

The user pastes a rough role description (or even a job title and a few bullets). The skill returns: a cleaned-up, bias-checked job description; five structured behavioral interview questions; and a simple 1–5 scoring rubric. One paste in, a hiring kit out. That is a person's first taste of handing real work to an AI and getting back something they would have paid a coordinator to do.

Why this one wins over the alternatives:

  • It sits on your verifiable proof. You ran a hiring Kaizen that cut time-to-fill from ~60 days to under 30. A hiring skill is your home turf, not a generic desktop cleaner.
  • It IS an AI employee doing an HR job — the gift and the workshop promise are literally the same thing, which is what makes their bridge sentence work.
  • Near-zero friction. Paste text, get text. No Mac, no permissions, no computer-control fragility. Anyone on your list can run it.
  • Reuses what you already have. It is a packaged version of copy/rewrite tooling you already run internally — a SKILL.md wrapper, not a new build.

Strong alternates (same format, if you want options)

Meeting notes → owner-tagged action list

Paste messy notes or a transcript, get a clean action list with owners and dates. Universal operator pain, instant win.

Dense HR policy → plain language

Paste a jargon-heavy policy, get a version a human actually understands. HR-native, obviously useful.

Package it like they did, minus their mistakes: one SKILL.md in a small zip; the install UX ("open Claude > new task > drop it in > run") carried in the email, not the file; scrub every internal codename and client name before it ships; and route the final gift email + the skill's user-facing text through your voice + lead-gen gates before it goes out. This ties to your open workshop-gift decision — recommend deciding the gift IS this skill.
7. What to skip — honest, not everything is worth copying
The 90% stat as written. SKIP Inflated and indefensible. Would break your no-unverified-numbers rule. Use the real ~65% / "about a third is coding" framing instead.
The Mac computer-control skill format. SKIP Too much friction and too fragile for a non-technical ICP. Text-in / text-out beats it as a lead magnet.
The faceless "Jamie & Steve" duo voice. SKIP Generic. Your single-operator personal brand and earned voice is a stronger asset than an anonymous two-founder "we." Do not flatten yourself into their template — the moves are stealable, the voice is not.
The "we let go of our $88K team" flex tone (elsewhere in their sequence). SKIP Reads as hype-flex. Your voice is earned and humble; the same point lands harder in your register without the boast.

Net: steal the structure and the felt-leverage mechanic, keep your own voice, tools, numbers, and domain. The parts of their funnel worth copying are the parts that are audience psychology, not the parts that are their brand.
