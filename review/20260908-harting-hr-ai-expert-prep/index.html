The two constraints almost nobody else interviewing for this will know, and why they are your opening

A global HR AI rollout at a German family-owned group is not primarily a technology problem, and both constraints below turn a demo into a deployment plan if you name them.

1. The EU AI Act classifies this exact work as high-risk

Annex III of the EU AI Act puts AI used in recruitment, candidate selection, targeted job advertising, and certain performance, promotion and termination-related decisions in the high-risk category. That carries real obligations before a system goes live: a risk-management process, data governance, human oversight, transparency to the workers affected, logging, and a conformity assessment. Recruiting and talent management are both named in this posting's own bullet list, so this is not a hypothetical for the seat.

The timeline moved, and it is worth knowing which version is current. The obligations were originally due 2 August 2026. Under the EU's Digital Omnibus agreement, the application date for these Annex III employment obligations was pushed to 2 December 2027, and a system already in genuine use before that date is grandfathered unless it goes through a significant redesign. That is a real, live regulatory fact, not a hedge on your part, and knowing the corrected date rather than the old headline one is itself a small signal.

2. German works councils have a real, legally protected say, not an advisory one

The Works Constitution Act (Betriebsverfassungsgesetz), sharpened by the 2021 Betriebsrätemodernisierungsgesetz (Works Council Modernisation Act), gives the works council concrete rights over exactly this kind of rollout: Section 90 requires the employer to inform the works council in good time before introducing an AI system, Section 80(3) gives the works council an explicit statutory right to bring in an external expert to evaluate it, at the employer's cost, and Section 95(2a) extends co-determination to personnel-selection guidelines even where AI supports them, which covers the recruiting and talent-management use cases this role is meant to scale. On top of that sits the older Section 87 co-determination over any system capable of monitoring employee performance or behaviour.

The practical consequence is the whole point: at a German-headquartered company, an HR AI rollout does not ship because it works technically, it ships because the works council agreed to it, and that agreement has to be a designed-in step of the rollout, not paperwork chased after a pilot is already built.

Whether HARTING itself currently has an active works council, and how it is structured, is not confirmed anywhere I found. These are the general provisions of German law that would apply to a company of this shape, not a claim about HARTING's own house. Say it as "at a German employer, in my understanding," and let them correct you if the specifics differ. That is a good conversation to have, not a risk.

How to actually say this, in your own words, not as a compliance lecture:

I've spent my career deploying HR systems and process changes into environments where the people affected had to be brought along before anything shipped, not after. That is exactly what a German-headquartered company doing this well requires. The EU treats AI used in hiring and talent decisions as high risk, and German works councils have a real, legally protected say over introducing a system like this, including the right to bring in their own expert at the company's expense. I don't read that as a delay to plan around. I read it as the shape the rollout has to take from day one. A pilot that skips that step doesn't fail on the technology, it fails on adoption, and by the time that happens you've spent the trust you needed for the next one. I'd rather build the change-management plan and the technical plan together than hand HR a tool it can't actually ship.

Why this matters more than it looks. Every other candidate will pitch use cases. If you are the one who says the constraint out loud and treats it as a design input rather than an obstacle, you arrive with a deployment plan instead of a demo, and that is the difference between someone who has actually shipped something inside a company and someone who has only presented on one.
