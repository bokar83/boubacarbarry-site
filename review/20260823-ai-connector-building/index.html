TL;DR — the four things that came out of this

The technique is real, it has a name, and it takes an afternoon. The part that is worth money is not the technique. Sell the curation, keep the capture as a rare last resort, and practise on Skool this week.

1. The real technique: capture-to-connector

Almost no software has "no API." It has no published API. Every modern web and mobile app is already talking to its own private data service in the background, using your own logged-in credentials. You record that conversation, turn the recording into a written map of what the app can do, throw away 95 percent of it, and hand the remaining ten or fifteen business verbs to an agent as tools.

It has a named public implementation: Printing Press (printingpress.dev, cli-printing-press), taught by Jay at RoboNuggets and verified first-hand from the paid Skool access, not from a marketing page. It sniffs a site's private traffic and generates a command-line tool, a plug-in server and docs from one run. Its public library ships tools built purely from sniffed traffic for services with no official interface at all.

2. The ranking correction: CLI > API > MCP

An earlier internal assumption ranked the plug-in style connector as the preferred shape on plumbing-convenience grounds. That is backwards. A plug-in server loads every tool description into context on every session; a command-line tool does not. The same physics was measured independently from the other end in this system's own token work (~63,900 tokens for a restricted agent against ~101,100 for one inheriting everything, and ~39,900 for a read-only search dispatch — a 61 percent cut). Shape also sets the ceiling, not just the price: the Google Workspace command-line tool can mark a Gmail message read, the Gmail plug-in cannot.

Ship this one separately and now. The ranking flip costs one commit, is correct on this system's own measured numbers, and should not wait on whether any of the rest gets built.

3. The service-line correction: sell the safest authorised path, not the hack

I make the software you already own legible to AI agents, by the safest authorised path that exists.

The wrong version is "I build custom connectors for tools with no API." It commits to the hardest, riskiest, least supportable path as a specialty, sells strongest exactly where the money is not, and turns one person into a pager rotation against a dozen vendors. Three tiers instead, ordered by how much of the revenue they should be: (A) agent-readiness curation — the base, the largest share, zero legal risk, and it applies to companies that already have an API and have simply made it unusable by an agent; (B) authorised-path integration through what the client already runs; (C) last-resort capture, rare, premium, legally gated, never led with.

Synthesis — the four lenses pulled into one answer
Synthesis TL;DR

Yes, there is a fast way, it is real, and it is a five-step pipeline: capture the app's own private traffic, turn the capture into an OpenAPI spec, curate it down to 8-15 business verbs, and generate a connector server from it. Two tools do most of the work (mitmproxy2swagger, FastMCP.from_openapi()), and one thing nobody in the four lenses noticed: the capture step is already automated in this system today via the browser-control read_network_requests, which removes the single manual bottleneck the leading tool admits to. Build the pipeline on FastMCP; adopt Jay's cli-printing-press as an accelerator after a security-gate pass, not as the core.

1. The real answer, separated from hype

Real: almost no tool has "no API." It has no documented API. Every single-page app, mobile app, and modern web tool is already calling a private JSON API from its own front end, with its own auth, right now. Capturing that traffic and wrapping it is a solved, tooled, hours-not-weeks workflow. The chain developer-tools capture → mitmproxy2swagger → FastMCP.from_openapi() → curated connector server is the fastest legitimate path in existence today and it is the thing most people do not know is possible.

Also real: Jay's material exists and was verified first-hand from Boubacar's own paid access. His curriculum (Claude Living Masterclass, Volume E) teaches connection-method ranking CLI > API > MCP on token-cost and capability grounds, and the bonus lesson hands over cli-printing-press, which sniffs a site's traffic and generates a Go command-line tool plus a connector server plus docs from one run. Its public library ships command-line tools built purely from sniffed traffic for services with no official API (ESPN is labelled exactly that). That is the technique running in public, not a claim.

Hype, name it plainly: the "agentic operating system" framing around it is packaging. The engineering underneath the packaging is checkable and good; the framework language is not the asset. Also hype-adjacent: auto-generation as a finished product. FastMCP's own docs say language models perform "significantly better" against curated servers than auto-converted ones, and that auto-generation is for bootstrapping, not mirroring. Dumping a 400-endpoint spec into an agent's tool list is a token disaster and a reliability disaster.

The generation is the commodity. The curation is the billable expertise. That distinction is the whole business model.

The one thing that is genuinely hard, and it is not technical: contract law and silent schema drift. Neither is a blocker. Both have concrete answers, below.

2. The four techniques, ranked strongest and fastest first

Rung 1 — Private-API capture to spec to connector server. Hours. The default.

What it is: act in the app's interface while capturing network traffic (developer-tools export, or mitmproxy for desktop and mobile). Run mitmproxy2swagger (9.6k stars, MIT) over the capture: pass one emits candidate path templates all prefixed ignore:, you strip the prefix from the endpoints you actually want, pass two generates full request and response schemas. Feed that spec to FastMCP.from_openapi() (27.3k stars, Apache-2.0) with an authenticated httpx client and you have a working connector server. RouteMap filters endpoints into Tool / Resource / Exclude by method, path regex, or tag.

When: the default for any web or mobile software the client already pays for and logs into. Covers the large majority of enterprise "we can't automate this" cases.

Security tradeoff: the capture file is a live-credential artifact. It contains bearer tokens, session cookies, and often personal data in response bodies. mitmproxy2swagger's own docs warn its output "may add sensitive data (tokens, passwords, personal information)" into the generated spec. Never commit it, never email it, scrub and destroy on a clock. Credentials live on the HTTP client from a secret manager, never baked into the generated surface.

Reliability tradeoff: an undocumented endpoint has no compatibility contract, no changelog, no deprecation window. nba_api (3.7k stars) is the honest public precedent: its contributing guide states outright that the vendor "does not provide information regarding new, changed, or removed endpoints" and asks users to report breakage. Mitigation is a schema-validating client plus a scheduled canary, not hope.

Rung 2 — Skip the build: bridge through a platform that already owns the connector. Minutes to an hour.

What it is: n8n's MCP Server Trigger turns existing n8n workflows into agent tools over SSE or Streamable HTTP (stdio not supported). Make exposes scenarios as tools with hard run caps of 25s OAuth / 40s token, so anything slow must be built async-first against executionId. UiPath's uipath-mcp-python hosts servers inside their platform, inheriting the orchestrator's vault and audit trail. Pipedream Connect and Composio solve the per-user auth problem (external_user_id binds a request to that user's connected accounts) which is the thing that actually kills enterprise rollouts.

When: first check, always, before writing anything. If the client already runs n8n or UiPath, this is both faster and a better security story, because credentials stay in a vault the client already owns and already audits.

Tradeoff: you inherit the platform's failure modes and timeouts, and the client inherits a vendor dependency. Not a weakness for enterprise; usually a selling point.

Rung 3 — Native and mobile: defeat certificate pinning, then fall back to Rung 1. Hours to a day.

What it is: since Android 7, apps ignore the user trust store, so a proxy alone fails. Three real answers, all MIT: apk-mitm (static patching, honest about failing on Flutter and complex packages), android-unpinner from the mitmproxy organisation (runtime, no root, injects Frida Gadget over ADB and touches only the manifest), and Frida directly for hooking crypto calls above the wire when packet capture fails entirely. Once decrypted, you are back on Rung 1. If the replayed API then gets blocked by a web application firewall despite correct auth, curl_cffi (6.4k stars) impersonates browser TLS and HTTP/2 fingerprints across 37 profiles. That last piece is the difference between "works on my laptop" and "works in production" and almost nobody knows it exists.

Tradeoff: highest terms-of-service exposure of any rung, and the tooling itself runs third-party code against real credentials, so it must pass a security gate before it ever runs.

Rung 4 — No HTTP layer at all: drive the interface. Days. Last resort, and call it what it is.

What it is: Citrix-delivered desktop apps, mainframe green screens, air-gapped internal tools. Stagehand (MIT, Playwright API plus act / observe / extract with typed extraction, accessibility-tree trimming, self-healing) is the enterprise default. Skyvern is more capable out of the box but AGPL-3.0, which is a real procurement conversation, not a footnote. Microsoft's playwright-mcp says outright that it "is not a security boundary."

Security tradeoff, and this is the sharpest one in the report: a browser agent holds a live authenticated session with everything the human user can do, and a language model decides what to click. Anthropic's own adversarial testing puts browser-agent prompt-injection success at 23.6% unmitigated, 11.2% autonomous-with-defences. Page content is an attack surface. Scope these to a dedicated least-privilege service account, always, and never grant one write access it does not need.

Precise limit, honestly stated: this rung is not a connector. It is a robot with a mouse. It fails in ways that look like success, which is worse than failing loudly. Price it accordingly and prefer Rung 1 whenever an HTTP layer exists at all.

3. Four adjacencies nobody proposed

A. The manual-capture limitation is already solved in this system, today. cli-printing-press states its own hard limit plainly: "Browser-sniff requires manual capture. You point a browser at the site (or import a HAR); the press doesn't crawl autonomously." But the browser-control tooling is already connected here and exposes read_network_requests, and Playwright exposes browser_network_requests. That means an agent in this system can already drive the interface and read the resulting network traffic in the same session. The capture step that every external tool treats as the human-in-the-loop bottleneck is scriptable here right now. Nobody has connected those two facts. That is the single biggest speed advantage available, and it is sitting unused.

B. The security-scan checkpoint covers the wrong artifact class, and the gap is exactly where the danger is. The existing gate does static analysis before external code runs, which correctly covers apk-mitm, Frida scripts, and cli-printing-press itself. It does not cover capture artifacts, which are a new artifact class this work introduces and which are live secrets by definition. A capture file is more dangerous than a script: a script is inspectable, a capture silently carries a client's session token and their customers' personal data. This needs a sibling Capture Hygiene Gate, not an amendment to the existing one, with three mechanical rules: captures never enter a repository, captures are scrubbed before spec generation, captures are destroyed on a fixed clock. cli-printing-press's auth doctor fingerprints tokens to four characters and never prints full values, which is the right instinct applied to the wrong file, and it is the control most likely to be missing in a first client engagement.

C. This system already has independent empirical proof of the CLI-first ranking, from a completely different domain. Jay ranks the command-line tool above the plug-in server because a plug-in server loads every tool description into context on every session. This system measured the same effect from the other end: the token-efficiency work found a restricted-tool agent costs ~63,900 tokens against ~101,100 for one inheriting all tools, and a read-only search dispatch ~39,900 against ~101,100, a 61% cut. Same physics, arrived at from dispatch economics rather than connector design. That means the existing discovery draft, which ranks the plug-in server as the preferred shape on plumbing-convenience grounds, should be flipped to CLI > API > MCP and justified with this system's own measured numbers, which are stronger evidence than Jay's assertion. Jay's E3 example makes the capability half of the argument too: the Google Workspace command-line tool can mark a Gmail message read; the Gmail plug-in cannot. Shape determines ceiling, not just price.

D. The second stakeholder is the retainer, and the maintenance burden is the product, not the tax. Every reverse-engineered connector is durable liability, because the vendor can break it silently. Priced as a one-time build that is a trap. Priced as build plus drift monitoring it is exactly the recurring-revenue shape the fractional-engagement north star needs. And the monitoring is not a new build here: a scheduled synthetic canary that exercises each endpoint against a fixture and alerts on schema or status drift is the same pattern as the existing audit crons and watchers. The deliverable to an enterprise is therefore three line items, not one: the connector, the curation of it down to real business verbs, and the canary that tells them before their users find out.

E. The legal posture is the differentiator, not the disclaimer. Van Buren gave the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act a narrow "gates-up-or-down" test and hiQ applied it, but hiQ still lost on breach of contract, and the EFF summary is explicit that protection for password-protected content remains undefined. Most connector work is authenticated, so hiQ shelters none of it. The statute is not the risk; the terms of service are. Which flips the whole thing: sniffing a client's own tenant, on their own account, with written authorisation, is a fundamentally different posture from sniffing a third-party consumer site, and it is why this skill sells to enterprises specifically. Being the person who raises the vendor terms clause to the client's legal team in writing before building is the consultant move. Hiding it is the scraper move. Non-US regimes were not researched and should not be assumed.

Build a Connector Foundry: a five-stage pipeline, with FastMCP as the generation core and the existing browser tools as the capture head.

Stage 0  DISCOVER   Phase -1, reranked CLI > API > MCP. Check printing-press-library

How it actually works

Three input modes, and the third is the one that answers "tools with no plug-in or API":

  1. --spec — an existing OpenAPI spec
  2. --har ./capture.har — a browser developer-tools export
  3. a bare URL — it launches a browser, captures network traffic, and reverse-engineers the private API the web app is already calling

Then a five-phase pipeline: research and resolution → ecosystem absorption (catalogs what competing wrappers already do) → code generation (Go command-line tool plus a connector server plus docs, so you get both shapes from one build) → verification → publish.

Agent-native design choices that matter for token economics: typed exit codes so the agent can self-correct, auto-JSON when piped, --compact claiming 60-80% token reduction, --dry-run, local SQLite persistence with full-text search and incremental sync, and compound commands that a stateless HTTP wrapper cannot express.

Auth: infers the scheme from the spec (Bot / Bearer / Basic), supports OAuth flows, and ships cli-printing-press auth doctor, which reports which environment-variable tokens are set, unset or suspicious using four-character fingerprints only, never full token values.

Verification, four gates: a 100-point two-tier scorecard (50 infrastructure / 50 domain correctness), a "dogfood" structural check for dead flags and auth mismatches, a proof-of-behaviour data-flow check, and an optional read-only live smoke test — GET only, never mutates, 10-second timeout, halts on 401.

3. The honest tradeoffs

Legal and terms of service — the biggest one for enterprise work. The project's own readme carries this warning: "Before generating a CLI for any service, review its Terms of Service. Many services explicitly prohibit automated access. Using this tool against such services may violate their terms or applicable law. You are responsible for ensuring your use is authorized." In an enterprise engagement this flips from a risk to an advantage: sniffing a client's own internal tool, on their own account, with their written authorisation, is a completely different legal posture than sniffing a third-party consumer site. That is the version to sell.

Reliability. A reverse-engineered private endpoint carries no compatibility contract. The vendor can change it in any release with no deprecation notice and no changelog. Mitigation is structural, not hopeful: printing-press-reprint exists precisely because regeneration is expected to be routine, and the scorecard plus live smoke test give you a mechanical canary. Budget for breakage; do not promise a service level on a sniffed connector without a monitoring line item.

Not autonomous. Stated limit: "Browser-sniff requires manual capture. You point a browser at the site (or import a HAR); the press doesn't crawl autonomously." A human drives the session that produces the capture. For enterprise that is a feature — the capture is scoped, consented, and auditable.

Security — a capture file is a live-credential artifact. A developer-tools capture contains session cookies, bearer tokens, and often personal data in response bodies. It must be handled as a secret from the moment it is written: never committed, never emailed, never left in a scratch directory. The auth doctor fingerprinting discipline is the right instinct but it protects the generated tool's environment variables, not the capture that produced it. This is the single control most likely to be missing in a first engagement.

Supply chain. cli-printing-press generates code that will run with real credentials. It is a third-party MIT repository. I did not independently verify its star count, last-commit date, or maintainer activity — the fetch returned a summarised readme, not raw metadata, and my web-search budget was exhausted this session. Anything installed here goes through the existing security-scan checkpoint first, no exception.

Maintenance burden is the real business-model risk. Every sniffed connector is a small piece of durable liability. Priced as a one-time build it is a trap; priced as build-plus-monitoring it is a recurring-revenue line that fits the fractional-engagement north star exactly.

Lens 3 — Private-API reverse engineering: the named technique end to end
Lens 3 TL;DR

The technique is real, named, and mature: intercept the app's own private API with a man-in-the-middle proxy, convert the captured traffic into an OpenAPI spec, wrap that spec as a connector server. Two tools do most of the work (mitmproxy2swagger, FastMCP.from_openapi()). The hard part is not technical — it is contract law and silent schema drift, and both have real engineering answers.

Honest scope caveat: this session's web-search budget was already spent (200/200) before this lens started. Everything below is from direct fetch of primary sources, so it is depth-verified but not breadth-exhaustive. No open-ended discovery searches were run.

1. The technique, named properly

There is no single name; practitioners call it private API reverse engineering, API discovery via traffic interception, or in security terms dynamic analysis / man-in-the-middle interception. The pipeline is five layers, and you go down the stack only as far as you have to. Speed comes from stopping early.

Layer 1 — browser developer tools network tab / capture export. For any web app, the front end is already calling a private JSON API. Filter to XHR and fetch, act in the interface, read the calls, export the capture. This is minutes of work and covers a surprising share of enterprise software. No proxy, no certificates, no legal grey area beyond the terms of service.

Layer 2 — man-in-the-middle proxy for desktop, mobile and native traffic. mitmproxy (44.8k stars, MIT, Python) is the reference tool, with three interfaces: mitmproxy (console), mitmweb (browser UI), and mitmdump ("tcpdump for HTTP"). It generates a per-host interception certificate signed by its own authority; you install that authority in the client's trust store and it decrypts TLS transparently. Commercial equivalents: Charles Proxy, Proxyman, Burp Suite, Fiddler, HTTP Toolkit.

Layer 3 — defeating certificate pinning. This is where most people quit and where the real skill lives. Since Android 7 (API 24), apps ignore the user trust store by default and only honour the system store, per HTTP Toolkit's guide. Three real answers:

  • apk-mitm (5.1k stars, MIT) — static patching. Decodes the package with Apktool, rewrites the network security config to trust user certificates, patches known pinning implementations, re-signs. Its stated limits are honest: Apktool struggles on complex apps, and it cannot defeat pinning inside native binaries (Flutter).
  • android-unpinner from the mitmproxy organisation (1k+ stars, MIT) — runtime patching, no root. Only modifies the manifest to mark the app debuggable, injects Frida Gadget over ADB via a custom JDWP implementation, then runs HTTP Toolkit's unpinning script. Leaves the compiled classes untouched.
  • Frida directly — a dynamic instrumentation toolkit ("Greasemonkey for native apps") that injects QuickJS into a running process and hooks arbitrary native functions across Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android, FreeBSD and QNX. Its own docs cite encrypted-protocol analysis as a primary use case: when packet sniffing fails, you hook the crypto calls above the wire.

Layer 4 — client fingerprint impersonation. Once you replay the private API from your own code, web application firewalls and bot detection often block you even with correct auth, because your TLS handshake does not look like a browser. curl_cffi (6.4k stars, MIT) is the Python binding to the curl-impersonate fork; it mimics TLS and HTTP/2 fingerprints across 37 preset browser profiles (Chrome, Safari, Firefox), and supports HTTP/2 and HTTP/3. This is the piece almost nobody knows about and it is the difference between "works on my machine" and "works in production."

Layer 5 — browser and interface automation as the honest fallback. When there is genuinely no HTTP API to find (a Citrix-delivered desktop app, a mainframe green screen), you drive the interface: Playwright, or computer use. Slower, more fragile, but sometimes the only path. Do not pretend this is a connector; it is a robot with a mouse.

2. The speed play: capture → OpenAPI → connector server

This is the answer to "there's got to be a fast way."

Step A — mitmproxy2swagger (9.6k stars, 372 forks, MIT, Python). It converts captured traffic into an OpenAPI 3.0 spec automatically. The workflow is two-pass by design: capture to a flow file (or export a capture straight from developer tools — that input format is supported), run it once to emit candidate path templates prefixed ignore:, hand-remove the ignore: on the endpoints you actually want, re-run to generate full schemas from observed request and response bodies. The two-pass design is a feature: the human curation step is what keeps you from shipping a 400-endpoint mess.

Its docs carry a warning you must operationalise: including example data and headers "may add sensitive data (tokens, passwords, personal information etc.)" into the generated spec. Scrub before that file touches a repository.

Step B — FastMCP.from_openapi() (27.3k stars, Apache-2.0). Feeds the spec straight into a working connector server:

mcp = FastMCP.from_openapi(
    openapi_spec=openapi_spec,
    client=httpx2.AsyncClient(
        base_url="https://api.example.com",
        headers={"Authorization": "Bearer YOUR_TOKEN"},
    ),
    name="My API Server",
)

Auth is configured on the HTTP client, not in the spec. RouteMap objects control which endpoints become TOOL / RESOURCE / RESOURCE_TEMPLATE / EXCLUDE, filtered by method, path regex, and tags; the default is that every endpoint becomes a Tool. FastMCP also ships real auth infrastructure — OAuth proxy, OIDC proxy, token verification, multi-auth sources, and provider integrations for Auth0, Azure, Cognito, Google, Keycloak, Okta-likes, WorkOS.

FastMCP's own stated limitation is the most important line in this whole report: language models perform "significantly better" against curated servers than auto-converted OpenAPI ones, and auto-generation is recommended "for bootstrapping and prototyping, not for mirroring" a full API. So the real deliverable is: auto-generate in an hour, then hand-curate down to the 5-15 tools that map to actual business verbs. That curation is the billable expertise.

Elapsed time for a cooperative web app: a single afternoon. That is the "speed way" he is looking for, and it is not hype.

3. What makes it safe and enterprise-acceptable

This is what separates a consultant from a scraper, and it is the part he can sell.

Auth: never bake a session. The captured traffic will contain a bearer token or session cookie. Do not hardcode it. Three tiers, in order of preference: (1) if the tool has an official token mechanism, use that even if the endpoints are undocumented — you are then authenticated as intended and only the routes are unofficial; (2) a service account whose credentials live in a secret manager, with the connector performing a real login flow and refreshing; (3) headless browser session capture as last resort, isolated, with cookies never written to disk in cleartext. The connector process should hold the narrowest credential that works, not the admin one that was easiest to grab.

Scope: read-only by default. Ship discovery and read endpoints first. Write endpoints get an explicit allowlist and human approval, because an undocumented POST has undocumented side effects.

Rate limiting: budget below the observed human ceiling. You now know the app's real limits because you watched it. Set a client-side token bucket well under that, add jitter, honour Retry-After, and back off exponentially on 429 and 5xx. Getting a client's corporate IP blocked is a fireable outcome.

Blast radius: run it isolated. Dedicated egress IP, its own container, structured logging of every outbound call so there is an audit trail the client's security team can read. Enterprise security will ask "what did it do at 3am" and you need a real answer.

Secrets and captures are toxic waste. Flow files and capture exports contain live credentials by definition. Treat capture artifacts as secrets: scrub, short retention, never committed.

4. Legal and terms of service — the real constraint

Two decisions define the US landscape, both verified via the EFF:

Van Buren v. United States (Supreme Court) adopted a "gates-up-or-down" test for "exceeds authorized access" under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. hiQ v. LinkedIn (9th Circuit) applied it: scraping publicly available data likely does not violate that statute, because public profiles "erected no gates to lift or lower." Authorisation gates exist only where a login is required.

What that means for connector work, stated as precisely as the courts did:

  • Public, unauthenticated data: statutory risk is low in the 9th Circuit.
  • Authenticated / logged-in data: this is the opposite side of the line. A login is a gate. The EFF summary is explicit that protections for password-protected or subscriber-only content remain undefined. Most enterprise connector work is authenticated, so hiQ does not shelter it.
  • Contract and terms claims were expressly not resolved, including whether a cease-and-desist can retroactively revoke authorisation. Trespass to chattels was flagged as a live alternative theory. So even where criminal exposure is low, breach-of-contract exposure is not.

The practical consulting posture, and this is a genuine differentiator rather than a disclaimer:

  1. The client authorises access to their own tenant, in writing, in the statement of work. When the enterprise is the account holder and instructs you to automate their own data, the "authorisation" question mostly dissolves. This is the entire reason this skill is more sellable to enterprises than to consumer-side hackers.
  2. Check the vendor terms for automated-access clauses and surface them to the client's legal team as a named risk, in writing, before building. You are the one who raises it, not the one who hides it.
  3. Never bypass a paywall, a per-seat licence, or a metered limit. That converts a contract question into a fraud question.
  4. Prefer official when it exists. Discovery should always run first; reverse engineering is what you do after the official path is proven absent.
  5. Jurisdiction matters — the above is US and largely 9th Circuit. EU and UK clients have different database-rights and computer-misuse regimes. Those were not researched and should not be pretended otherwise.

5. The real maintenance burden: silent API changes

Undocumented means no changelog, no deprecation window, no versioning. nba_api (3.7k stars, MIT) is the honest public example — a widely-used client for an undocumented statistics API, whose contributing guide states plainly that the vendor "does not provide information regarding new, changed, or removed endpoints" and asks contributors to report breakage as issues. That is the true operating model: a community, or in your case a monitoring system, is the changelog.

Engineering answers, all of which you already have infrastructure for:

  • Validate every response against the generated schema at runtime. A silent field rename should raise a typed error, not propagate a null into a client deliverable. This is exactly the "a signal that lies beats a crash" principle already in this system's rules.
  • Synthetic canary on a schedule. A cron that exercises each endpoint against a known fixture and alerts on schema drift or status change — before a client discovers it.
  • Version-pin the client fingerprint and the spec together, so you can diff "what changed" when something breaks.
  • Consumer-driven contract testing (Pact is the standard named tool) is the formal version of this pattern, though it was designed for services you control; against a third party you get the consumer half only.
  • Budget the maintenance in the engagement. A reverse-engineered connector is a retainer line item, not a one-time build. Say so up front — it is more honest and it is recurring revenue.

Lens 4 — RPA and automation platforms as the connector layer

Method caveat first, plainly: this session's web-search budget (200/200) was already spent before this lens's first call, so no discovery searches were run. Everything below is direct fetch against named vendor docs and repositories addressable by URL. That means: strong on how the mechanisms work, weak on who runs them in production — no verifiable named enterprise case studies were found and none were invented. Lens 4 TL;DR

There is a real, named, fast path, and it is a three-tier ladder you walk down only as far as you have to. Tier 1 is minutes, Tier 2 is hours, Tier 3 is days and carries the real risk. Most enterprise "this tool has no connector" cases resolve at Tier 1 or 2 — the tool almost always has an API, it just has no plug-in server.

Tier 1 — the tool has an API or OpenAPI spec: generate the connector

FastMCP from_openapi() — an OpenAPI spec plus an authenticated httpx client becomes a working connector server in a handful of lines. Every endpoint becomes a Tool by default; RouteMap objects re-map by HTTP method, path regex or tag into Tool, Resource, ResourceTemplate or Exclude, and mcp_component_fn callbacks rewrite descriptions after generation. It handles path and query parameters, explode on arrays, and flattens allOf and discriminator request bodies into flat tool arguments.

Auth is where it stays safe for enterprise: credentials live on the HTTP client you construct, never in the generated surface.

The caveat is in their own docs and it matters for a consulting engagement: "LLMs achieve significantly better performance with well-designed and curated MCP servers than with auto-converted OpenAPI servers." Auto-generation is a prototyping accelerant. A 400-endpoint enterprise resource spec dumped wholesale into an agent's tool list is a token disaster and a reliability disaster. The billable skill here is curation, not generation — pick the 8 endpoints that matter, exclude the rest, rewrite the descriptions. That is the deliverable.

Tier 2 — no connector, but an automation platform already has it

This is the highest-leverage tier. Every major automation and no-code platform now ships an agent-tool surface, so their pre-built connector catalogs become an agent's tool catalog without writing a connector at all.

n8n — MCP Server Trigger node. Turns n8n itself into a connector server: exposes a URL endpoint, connects only to tool nodes, and any existing workflow is attached via the Custom Workflow Tool node. Auth is Bearer or Header credentials. Transports: SSE and Streamable HTTP; stdio is not supported. Two real operational gotchas — in queue mode with multiple webhook replicas, all /mcp* requests must route to one dedicated replica or the connections go unreliable; behind nginx you must disable proxy buffering, gzip and chunked transfer encoding on those endpoints. Combined with the HTTP Request node, this is the fastest general-purpose answer: build the call once, expose it as a tool, done. Self-hostable, which is the part enterprise security will care about.

Make — hosted server. Converts active and on-demand scenarios into callable tools; scenario inputs are the parameters the agent fills, outputs are what comes back. Stateless Streamable HTTP (their recommendation), SSE, Streamable HTTP. OAuth at https://mcp.make.com or a token URL. The timeouts are the thing to design around: scenario runs cap at 25s (OAuth) / 40s (token), management operations 30s to 5+ minutes. Scenarios keep running up to 40 minutes after the timeout and you retrieve output asynchronously by executionId — so any long-running connector has to be built async-first, not as a blocking tool call. Scenario-run tools are on all plans; management tools are paid-only.

UiPath — uipath-mcp-python SDK. Build and host connector servers inside the UiPath platform. Servers declared in mcp.json, project needs pyproject.toml plus mcp.json plus uipath.json, launched over stdio, then uipath init → uipath run (local test) → uipath pack → uipath publish to the orchestrator. It can also package binary servers written in other languages (Go, for example). This is the one to name in a room with an existing UiPath estate: their governance, credential vault and audit trail stay in place and the agent inherits them.

Sema4.ai Action Server (formerly Robocorp Action Server). Python functions decorated @tool / @action with type hints and docstrings become tools automatically — the docstring and types generate the schema. Environment pinned in package.yaml. Exposed over an /mcp endpoint, plus custom-GPT and LangChain paths. The action packages have moved to the sema4ai-* distributions; the robocorp repository retains the underlying automation libraries (browser, Windows, Excel, vault, work items) and is still actively committed. This is the closest thing to a purpose-built "write your own connector in Python" framework — the ergonomics are better than hand-rolling a server, and it is the one to teach first for custom logic.

Pipedream Connect and Composio solve the adjacent problem, which is the one that actually kills enterprise rollouts: per-user auth. Pipedream: 3,000+ APIs, external_user_id binds each request to that user's connected accounts, no OAuth flows or token storage to build, returns a setup link automatically when an account is not connected, SSE and HTTP transports. Composio: session bound to a user id, managed auth where the agent connects accounts at runtime, REST API v3.1. Their docs did not clearly state a toolkit count or custom-tool story, and that was not filled in.

Tier 3 — genuinely no API. Two real techniques, both with teeth.

3a. Reverse-engineer the private API from traffic. Every web app has an internal JSON API; the browser is already calling it. mitmproxy2swagger turns captured traffic into an OpenAPI 3.0 spec — inputs are flow files or captures exported straight from the browser network tab. Two-pass workflow: first pass generates path templates all marked ignore:, you strip the prefix from the endpoints you want, second pass regenerates with full parameters and schemas. Then that generated spec feeds straight into Tier 1's from_openapi(). That chain — capture → mitmproxy2swagger → FastMCP → connector server — is the sharpest thing in this whole report and it is the technique most people do not know exists.

3b. Drive the interface with a vision or language-model browser agent. Skyvern (AGPL-3.0, managed cloud, anti-bot measures proprietary to the cloud offering): a Playwright extension that replaces selector paths with a swarm of agents that comprehend the page, so it survives layout changes and works on unseen sites. Exposes a REST API, Python and TypeScript SDKs, an agent-tool surface, a CLI, and native Zapier / Make / n8n integrations. Documented uses: invoice downloading, form filing, insurance quotes, procurement. Stagehand (MIT, Browserbase): Playwright-style API plus act() / observe() / extract() — natural-language actions, structured element discovery, and typed extraction — with self-healing primitives, accessibility-tree trimming for token efficiency, handling for out-of-process iframes and closed shadow DOM, and OpenTelemetry observability. Stagehand's MIT licence and determinism-plus-AI posture make it the better enterprise default; Skyvern's AGPL is a real procurement conversation.

The tradeoffs, stated honestly

Security. Tiers 1 and 2 keep credentials in a vault the client already owns (n8n credentials, the UiPath orchestrator, managed auth) — that is the defensible story. Tier 3b is the opposite: a browser agent typically holds a live authenticated session with whatever the human user can do, and a language model decides what to click. Prompt injection from page content is a live attack path there, not a theoretical one. Scope those sessions to a dedicated service account with least privilege, always.

Reliability. Tier 1 is as reliable as the API. Tier 2 inherits the platform's failure modes and its timeouts — Make's 25/40s ceiling and n8n's queue-mode routing requirement are design constraints, not footnotes. Tier 3a is reliable until the vendor changes their private API, silently, with no deprecation notice, because it was never a public contract. Tier 3b is the least reliable and the most expensive per run, and it fails in ways that look like success.

Legal and terms. Tier 3 is where a fractional engagement gets a client in trouble. Reverse-engineering a private API and driving an interface with a bot both commonly violate software terms of service, and Skyvern's cloud explicitly sells anti-bot circumvention. Rule to carry into every enterprise room: Tier 3 against a vendor the client pays for is a contract question before it is a technical one. Get it in writing, or use it only against systems the client owns.

Maintenance. Tier 1 regenerates from the spec — near zero. Tier 2 is maintained by the platform vendor, which is exactly what you are paying them for. Tier 3 is permanent maintenance debt and should be priced as a retainer, never as a fixed-fee build.

What to build on rather than from scratch

FastMCP as the connector-authoring core, Sema4.ai Action Server for custom Python logic with real environment pinning, the n8n MCP Server Trigger as the general-purpose bridge to anything with an HTTP endpoint, mitmproxy2swagger as the undocumented-API opener, Stagehand as the no-API fallback. UiPath's SDK only when the client already runs UiPath.

The Contrarian

The fatal flaw is not in the pipeline. The pipeline works. The fatal flaw is that this document confuses "I can build a connector" with "an enterprise will let me run this inside their walls," and those are separated by a procurement, security, and liability chasm that no amount of FastMCP elegance crosses.

Walk the actual enterprise engagement. Boubacar shows up as a fractional AI consultant. To execute Rung 1, he needs to capture the client's live private-API traffic. That means one of two things: he sits at a machine authenticated as a real user inside the client's tenant, or the client hands him credentials. Both trigger the client's security team. The moment a security reviewer hears "I'm going to run a TLS-intercepting proxy against your production SaaS session and generate a spec from the capture," the engagement stops for a security review that takes weeks, not hours. The "hours-not-weeks" claim is true only for the code generation. It is wildly false for the thing that gates revenue: getting authorized to capture at all. The report measures the fast part and ignores the slow part, which is the classic error of the person who loves the engineering.

Second flaw, sharper: the vendor ToS problem is not a "raise it to legal" footnote, it is frequently a hard no that kills the whole technique for the exact tools enterprises care about. Salesforce, Workday, SAP, ServiceNow, NetSuite — the tools where "no accessible API" actually bites in enterprise — have ToS that explicitly prohibit reverse-engineering and automated access outside published interfaces, and they have the lawyers to mean it. The report's own strongest legal point (hiQ lost on contract) is the counterargument to the report's own recommendation. Authenticated + contract-prohibited is the default enterprise case, and it is precisely the case with the least legal shelter. So the technique is strongest exactly where the client has no ToS problem (their own scrappy internal tool with no vendor) and weakest exactly where the money is (name-brand enterprise SaaS with real legal teams).

Third: the "already solved in this repo via read_network_requests" adjacency (A) is presented as the crown jewel, and it is the most dangerous line in the document. It says an agent in this system can drive a client's UI and read the client's authenticated network traffic autonomously. That is not a feature. That is an LLM holding a live enterprise session and exfiltrating the session's bearer tokens into a capture. If that ever runs against a client tenant without an airtight, written, scoped authorization and a hardened capture path, it is a security incident with Boubacar's name on it. The report treats the automation of the capture step as pure upside. It is the single highest-liability capability in the entire proposal.

Fourth, the quiet one: who maintains 20 reverse-engineered connectors across 6 clients when the vendors silently ship breaking changes? The report brilliantly reframes drift monitoring as recurring revenue (adjacency D). But recurring revenue is recurring obligation. A canary that fires at 2am telling you Client C's undocumented Workday endpoint changed shape means someone — Boubacar, a one-man fractional consultant — now owes an emergency fix on a connector he cannot get vendor support for, because officially it does not exist. Sell ten of these and you have not built a business, you have built a pager rotation for one person against ten vendors who are actively, if unknowingly, trying to break you. The maintenance-as-product framing is honest about the revenue and silent about the load.

The First-Principles Thinker

Strip the framing. The question the document answers is "how do I build a connector for a tool with no API." That is the wrong variable. Boubacar's stated goal is fractional AI engagements at enterprises. The actual problem an enterprise is paying to solve is not "connect to tool X." It is "make this business process that currently requires a human clicking through tool X happen reliably without the human." The connector is one possible means. The document has quietly promoted a means to the status of the goal, and in doing so has committed him to the single hardest, highest-liability, least-supportable path as his specialty.

Reframe: the enterprise does not care whether the automation runs through a reverse-engineered private API, a vendor's real API you finally found, an iPaaS the client already owns, or a documented partner integration. They care that it works, that it survives audit, and that when it breaks it is not their consultant's undocumented hack that broke. Once you optimize for reliable authorized automation of a process instead of connector for an unconnectable tool, the entire ranking inverts. Rung 2 (bridge through n8n/UiPath the client already owns) stops being "check this first" and becomes the product, because it inherits the client's vault, audit trail, and vendor relationships — the three things that make enterprise security teams say yes. Rung 1 (private-API capture) drops to "the thing you do only when no authorized path exists and the client's legal team signs off in writing," which is rare in enterprise, not the default.

The wrong variable being optimized is engineering difficulty overcome. The document is proud of defeating TLS pinning, impersonating JA3 fingerprints, patching APKs with Frida. That pride is the tell. The value to an enterprise client is inversely proportional to how much of that you had to do. A consultant who says "I found your process runs on a tool that has a real API nobody knew about, and I wired it up cleanly in your existing iPaaS" is worth more, and is more re-hireable, than one who says "I reverse-engineered your vendor's private endpoints and here's a Go binary that impersonates a browser to dodge their WAF." The second one is more impressive and less valuable, because the enterprise buyer's actual utility function is risk-adjusted, and every clever technique in Rung 3 is negative risk-adjusted value in a boardroom.

The reframe that makes the original question irrelevant: the skill to master is not connector-building, it is authorized-path discovery plus curation. Discovery — is there a real API, a partner program, an iPaaS node, a documented export — is where 80% of enterprise value hides and where zero legal risk lives. Curation — turning any surface into 8-15 clean business verbs an agent can use reliably — is the genuinely billable, genuinely scarce expertise the document correctly identifies but then buries under the reverse-engineering pyrotechnics. Sell the curation. Treat the private-API capture as the rare last resort it should be, not the "default."

1. The recommended technique, in plain English

Almost no software has "no API." It has no published API. Every modern web and mobile app is already talking to its own private data service in the background, every second you use it, using your own logged-in credentials. So instead of asking the vendor for access you will never get, you sit with the app open, record the conversation it is already having with its own servers, and turn that recording into a written map of what it can do. Then you take that map, throw away 95 percent of it, and keep only the ten or fifteen things a business actually needs ("list open invoices," "mark this ticket resolved"), rewrite those in plain language, and hand that short, clean list to an AI agent as a set of tools it can use. The recording and the map take a couple of hours and are largely automated.

The value is not in the recording. It is in the throwing away. A raw map with four hundred entries makes an AI agent slower, more expensive, and less reliable than no tool at all. A curated list of twelve verbs makes it work. That curation is the part nobody can commoditise, it is the part you sell, and it applies just as much to companies that do have an API and have simply made it unusable by an agent.

Exact steps

Step 0 — Prove the boring path is actually closed (15 min)

Run the discovery phase as written. Search for an official Skool API, an official command-line tool, any community wrapper, any plug-in server. If one exists and clears a security-scan checkpoint, use it and stop. Log the finding either way. This step is the habit that matters most at a client, so practise it here where the stakes are zero.

Step 1 — Verify the capture tool's return shape (15 min, do this before anything else)

Open one Skool page in the browser, navigate, then call the network-request reader. Look at exactly what comes back: does it include request URLs, methods, request bodies, response bodies, and headers? Or only a summary index? Write the answer down. If it is only an index, switch to a hand-exported capture from the browser network tab (right-click, save all with content) and note that the "capture is already automated here" advantage is unproven. Everything downstream is identical either way.

Step 2 — Capture (30 min)

In one session, deliberately perform the actions you want the connector to do, one at a time, slowly: open a classroom, open a lesson, list the attached files, download one file. Do not click around aimlessly; every extra click becomes noise you have to filter later. Save the capture to the quarantined scratch directory, never the repository.

Step 3 — Spec (30 min)

pip install mitmproxy2swagger (pin the version). Pass one over the capture emits every candidate path prefixed ignore:. This is the curation step. Un-ignore only the endpoints matching the four actions above. Pass two generates the schemas. Read the resulting spec by eye and confirm no bearer token or cookie value ended up inside it.

Step 4 — Generate (45 min)

pip install fastmcp (pin the version). FastMCP.from_openapi() with an authenticated httpx client, credentials read from an environment variable, never from a literal. RouteMap to exclude everything except your four verbs. Read-only for v1. Hand-rewrite each of the four tool descriptions in plain business language.

Step 5 — Register and prove it (20 min)

claude mcp add -s user skool -- <command>, then claude mcp list to confirm it actually connected. Then call each of the four tools once and read the output.

Step 6 — Destroy the capture and close out (10 min)

Delete the capture file. Confirm no capture file is tracked by version control. Add a row to the audit registry. Commit the connector code to a worktree, push, let the merge gate take it.

The done condition, stated up front so it can fail loudly: "A registered connector named skool exposes exactly four read-only tools; each returns real data from my own account when called; the guide PDF and the packaged connectors skill zip are downloaded to disk; no credential appears in any tracked file; the capture artifact no longer exists." If any clause fails, it is not done. Total: roughly one focused afternoon.

4. The security checklist that makes this safe to bring to a client

Not "be careful." Ten items, each either mechanically enforced or a signed document. Anything that is only a good intention is marked as such.

Before anything is captured

  1. Written authorisation, naming the system. A one-paragraph clause in the statement of work: which application, which account, which date window, which environment. Not "we may access client systems." Verbal permission is not authorisation.
  2. The vendor terms question goes to their legal team, in writing, on day one. You raise it, they decide, it is documented. This is the single move that separates a consultant from a scraper, and it is also your protection: it is their vendor relationship you would be gambling with. If their legal team says no, the answer is you do not build it and you route to the bridge stage instead. That branch must exist before the first engagement, because for name-brand enterprise software "no" is a likely answer.
  3. Dedicated least-privilege service account, never an employee's login. Scoped to exactly the data the connector needs. Revocable independently. If the client cannot create one, that is a finding to raise, not a thing to work around.
  4. Named capture host. A clean, isolated machine, disk-encrypted, agreed in advance. Your daily laptop is not an enterprise-acceptable capture host and saying so before they ask builds more trust than anything else on this list.

During capture

  1. No language model in the capture loop for v1. An agent driving an authenticated session reads bearer tokens and response bodies straight into model context, upstream of any scrubbing, and page content is an attacker-influenced surface. The published browser-agent prompt-injection numbers (23.6 percent unmitigated, 11.2 percent with defences) apply to this loop, not only to interface-driving automation. A human performs the capture until this capability has its own threat model.
  2. Scripted capture, minimum footprint. Perform only the specific actions being modelled. Never a broad crawl. Never a bulk export.

After capture

  1. Captures never enter a repository. Enforced by a pre-write hard-deny hook plus an ignore entry, on the pattern this system already uses for approval markers. Not a policy line in a document.
  2. Scrub, then destroy on a fixed clock. Strip Authorization, Cookie, Set-Cookie before spec generation. Say plainly that scrubbing is best-effort: you can enumerate known auth headers, you cannot enumerate the response-body field names carrying a client's customer data in a private schema nobody has documented. That is precisely why the real control is a short destruction clock (24 hours), not confidence in the scrubber. Fingerprint secrets to four characters in any log, never print values.
  3. Read-only by default, writes on an explicit allowlist. Every write verb is named individually in the statement of work and justified. A connector that can only read cannot cause an incident.
  4. Tell them what will page their security operations centre. Traffic interception and unusual client fingerprints pattern-match to exactly what their monitoring is built to detect. Notify their security team of the capture window in advance, in writing. Getting your own consultant flagged as a threat actor is an avoidable, relationship-ending own goal.

Two things that must be honestly disclosed and are not on the technical list: a reverse-engineered connector has no vendor support, no changelog, and no deprecation window, so it will break without warning; and there is a limit to how many you can carry at once. Name the number in the contract.

5. What this becomes as a service line

The wrong version: "I build custom connectors for tools with no API." That commits you to the hardest, riskiest, least supportable path as your specialty, sells strongest exactly where the money is not (a company's own scrappy internal tool with no vendor), and turns you into a one-person pager rotation against a dozen vendors who will silently break you.

The right version, tied to fractional engagements:

I make the software you already own legible to AI agents, by the safest authorised path that exists.

Three offerings, ordered by how much of your revenue they should be.

A. Agent-readiness curation the base

The largest share, zero legal risk. Most enterprises do not have a "no API" problem. They have a "our API has thousands of endpoints and every agent chokes on it" problem, plus a "nobody here knows we already have three ways to do this" problem. Collapsing a sprawling surface into ten clean business verbs is the same skill, applies to fully documented APIs, requires no capture, involves no terms question, and has a far larger market. This is the sellable core and it should be the headline. It also lands you inside the enterprise as the person who understands their process, which is the actual doorway to a fractional engagement.

B. Authorised-path integration the middle

Discovery plus bridging through what they already run: their integration platform, their existing connectors, their vault, their audit trail. Fast, inherits their controls, and their security team says yes because nothing new gets access to anything. Boring on the outside, high margin, high trust.

C. Last-resort capture rare, premium, legally gated

For the genuinely stranded system, under a signed authorisation, with the full checklist above. Priced high because of the disclosure burden, not the difficulty. Sold with the drift-monitoring retainer attached, and only offered where the maintenance load is honestly carryable. Never led with. Kept sharp, used rarely.

The asset that outlives any single engagement is the method itself. A named, documented, security-gated pipeline plus a written capture-authorisation protocol is a repeatable thing you walk into any company with. Every consultant pitching AI right now is hand-wavy about security. Walking in with the protocol before they ask it inverts the hardest objection in the sale into your differentiator. That is why the security checklist is the lead slide, not the appendix.
