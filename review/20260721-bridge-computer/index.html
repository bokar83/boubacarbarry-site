2. How This Research Was Done
1

Every price checked against 2+ live listings

Manufacturer store, Amazon, Best Buy, and independent price-tracker sites (Slickdeals, camelcamelcamel) were cross-checked against each other before any number was written down. Where two sources landed close together, that range is quoted directly. Where a single live number could not be pinned this pass, the page says so with a "single source, verify" badge instead of presenting a guess as fact.

2

Disagreements shown honestly, never averaged away

The GEEKOM A9 Max is the clearest case: sources ranged from $999 to $1,999 for close to the same configuration. Rather than splitting the difference into a comfortable-looking $1,400, both ends of the real range are shown, with a plain flag that it only clears the $1,000 cap on an unpredictable promo.

3

Specs confirmed from the source, not inferred

Every "4 native display outputs" claim traces to the manufacturer's own spec page or a retailer listing that states the port count directly. Two mini-PC families that looked capable on paper were rejected once their real spec sheets showed only 2-3 outputs.

4

Real third-party reviews cited, not manufacturer marketing

Notebookcheck, TechPowerUp, DisplayNinja, PCWorld, and Tom's Hardware are cited by name wherever they reviewed the specific product or its direct sibling, including the cons they reported, not just the parts that flatter the pick.

5

Configured price, not landing "starting at" price (added Pass 3, after a real miss)

Every configurable mini PC has a base/barebone SKU that headlines the manufacturer's own product page at a lower price than any real 32GB/1TB unit. Pass 3 quoted that barebone number for the Minisforum pick as if it were the configured price — it was off by roughly $500. The fix: price is now read from the manufacturer's own configurator with the EXACT RAM/storage tier selected, cross-checked against a retailer SKU or ASIN carrying that identical configuration (not just the same product family), before it is written down as confirmed.

5. Why This Fits the Workload

The Docker multi-container, Postgres, headless Chromium/Playwright, and Node stack is RAM- and multi-core-hungry, not GPU-bound. LLM calls route through the OpenRouter API, so there is no local inference and no VRAM need. The integrated GPU in all four picks exists only to drive 4 monitors, not for compute.

All four chips are 8-core-class or better (10-16 cores depending on the pick) with 32GB RAM out of the box, socketed and upgradeable on three of the four (Minisforum to 128GB, GEEKOM IT15 to 64GB, ASUS NUC to 64GB), which comfortably runs the concurrent stack. Socketed RAM is the single most important spec, fixing the actual pain point of the current soldered-8GB laptop. All four leave a free USB port and a wired ethernet path for the future NAS.

6. Doubles As Your Whole-Home Smart Hub (A Real Reason To Buy Sooner)
Buying this now absorbs a $150-$250 expense you would otherwise pay separately
The bridge computer can run the whole-home automation setup itself. The only extra hardware needed is one ~$49.99 USB dongle — not a second standalone hub.

Separately from this build, the plan is whole-home automation: one open-source hub (Home Assistant — free software) controlling the garage door, the central AC thermostat, the lights, and Plex. Home Assistant has to run on something that stays on all the time. There were two ways to provide that:

  • Buy a separate always-on device just for the hub — a Home Assistant Green appliance (~$199), or an equivalent small N100 mini PC (~$180-$290). That means paying for a whole second computer, compute and all, on top of this one.
  • Run the hub on the bridge computer you are already buying — it is already always-on and has spare capacity. The only added hardware is a Home Assistant Connect ZBT-2 Zigbee/Thread USB dongle (~$49.99), which the hub uses to talk to Zigbee devices. Home Assistant itself is free and installs alongside the machine's daily-driver duties.

Because the bridge computer is already being purchased, already always-on, and already has headroom, the second option accomplishes the exact same smart-home outcome for just the ~$49.99 dongle — a fraction of the cost of a second standalone device. In plain terms: this purchase absorbs what would otherwise be a ~$150-$250 additional expense. A separate hub would mean paying for compute twice. That makes the bridge computer cheaper on a total-cost basis than "computer now, hub later" — and it is a concrete reason to buy sooner rather than later, not just a nice-to-have.

What the bridge computer would control

Garage dooreKyro (a Tuya device), via the LocalTuya integration.
Central ACZen Thermostat (model Zen-01-W, confirmed plain Zigbee), paired directly to the ZBT-2 dongle via Zigbee2MQTT — not the ZHA integration, because of a confirmed open ZHA bug on this exact device (home-assistant/core#100570).
Lights + PlexControlled through the same hub.
Extra hardware neededJust the Home Assistant Connect ZBT-2 Zigbee/Thread USB dongle, ~$49.99. Nothing else.

One honest trade-off (already discussed, and accepted)

Like any computer, the bridge computer will need the occasional reboot for OS updates and maintenance. During a reboot, automated control is briefly offline — a few minutes. But every device still works on its own manual controls in the meantime: the garage door opens by hand or its own remote, lights are still lights, the thermostat has its own physical controls. So this is a minor, accepted trade, not a blocker.

Full detail on the hub plan — the hub comparison, the Plex integration specifics, the network fix, and the complete hardware list (now minus the standalone-hub cost this build makes unnecessary) — is on the dedicated research page: Smart Home Hub Plan.
