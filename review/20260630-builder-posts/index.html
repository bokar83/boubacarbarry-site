I was born crippled in Guinea

I was born crippled in Guinea. When I was three years old, my family sent me across an ocean to the United States so a surgeon could fix my leg. Think about that decision for a second. Put a three-year-old on a plane to a continent he has never seen, hand him to strangers, and trust that people you will never meet will build him a working leg and a working life. They did. I grew up with an American family until I was eleven. By then English had taken over and I had lost the languages I was born into. So when I went back to Africa, I had to re-learn French and re-learn my grandparents' language just to talk to the family I was born to. I have thought about that first flight a lot lately, because I spend my days building things now. Tools, systems, a business. And I finally see the through-line. I started life as a problem other people chose to solve. A kid who could not walk right, that a whole chain of strangers decided was worth building for. So building is not a skill I picked up. It is a debt I am paying. I know exactly what it is worth when someone looks at a hard problem, especially a person, and decides to build the fix instead of walking past. Every tool I ship is a small version of that plane. Someone had a problem. I would rather build the answer than leave them standing there. That is the whole job. That has always been the whole job.

I built a system that will not let me build

I am very good at building things. I am not good at asking people to buy them. So last week I built a system that would not let me build. It is my daily operating system. It runs on my phone, syncs to my laptop, and it has one rule I cannot get around. My build tasks stay locked until I have made five real sales asks that day. Not five emails I could hide behind. Five direct asks to actual people. The first ninety minutes of my day are now "asks only." Nothing else opens. I know exactly why I built it this way. Building feels like progress. It is warm, it is quiet, no one can tell you no. Selling is the cold part. So for a long time I would build a beautiful thing and never ask anyone to pay for it. That is not a discipline problem you fix with a motivational quote. It is a design problem. So I designed the thing that fixes it. The system I run my business on now protects me from the one thing I am most likely to avoid. That is the whole point of building your own tools. You do not build for the version of you that has it together. You build for the version that does not. The lock is on. Five asks before I get to touch the fun part. If you are a builder who hides in the building too, tell me what you avoid. I will tell you what I locked to beat it.

LinkedIn · The phone assistant · PASS

My mother connected other people's calls. I built the thing that answers mine.

My mother worked at the government telephone company in Conakry. Guinea. The phone was serious business in our house. Last week I built my own phone assistant. It answers my calls, screens the sales pitches, and books real appointments straight onto my calendar while I stay heads-down on the work that pays. The first test was humbling. It kept saying the word "hangup" out loud to callers. Turns out every instruction I wrote told it to end the call with a "hangup" signal, and nothing was catching that signal before it reached the voice. So it just read it aloud. Like a stage actor reading the stage directions. Then it went quiet for almost five seconds before its first word, and I hung up on my own assistant thinking it was broken. Both fixed now. It speaks the moment it picks up, and the signal never reaches the caller. Here is the part I keep sitting with. The kid whose mother connected other people's calls for a living now builds the thing that answers his own. That is not a full-circle story I planned. It is just what happens when you stop waiting for the tool to exist and go build it. If it is uncomfortable and useful, I would rather build it than wait for it.

I keep building AI that makes me do the hard part myself

Everyone wants AI to do the hard part for them. I keep building AI that makes me do the hard part myself. Two things I shipped in the last couple of weeks. One is a phone assistant that answers my calls and books my meetings, so I stay in the deep work instead of getting pulled out of it forty times a day. The other is a daily system that will not let me touch a single build task until I have made five real sales asks. It locks the fun work behind the uncomfortable work, on purpose. Notice the pattern. Neither one does the hard thing for me. The first one clears the noise so I can do the hard thing. The second one forces me to do the hard thing before I am allowed to avoid it. That is the part most people get wrong about this technology. They want it to replace the difficult human act. Asking for the sale. Having the conversation. Making the call. It cannot, and if it could, it would rot the exact muscle your business runs on. I run my whole operation on a system I built myself. Every tool in it has the same job. Not to do my work for me. To make sure I do the work that only I can do. The wheel is still mine. I just built a better car around it.

LinkedIn · The empty room · HELD

My first workshop had zero people in the room

I ran my first workshop last month. Zero people showed up. Not "low turnout." Zero. I had booked the room for thirty. I had run every piece of the logistics myself, right down to the labels on the water bottles. The only variable I did not solve was the one that mattered. Attendance. Three hundred and fifty people clicked the ad. Zero of them paid to be in the room. I could have blamed the ads. I ran the honest diagnosis instead, and it told me something I did not want to hear. The ads were not the problem. Proof was. Nobody in that room knew me well enough yet to hand me money based on a promise. I spent about fifteen years in HR, most of it inside a large company, watching how people actually adopt something new. They do not adopt because the pitch was good. They adopt when someone they trust has already gone first. I had not earned that trust yet. So the empty room was not a failure of the event. It was accurate feedback on where I actually stand with these people, which is at the beginning. I am filling the next one the slow way, by going and finding the people who belong in that room. An empty room is a brutal teacher. It is also an honest one.
