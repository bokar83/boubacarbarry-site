Built The four-question test: should this work get AI at all

Four questions in a deliberate order. The first two decide whether the work is worth automating, the second two decide whether it is safe to. Volume and shape, whether errors get caught, whether one named person owns the output, and whether somebody's livelihood rides on the decision.

What it proves: judgment and restraint. Every other candidate arrives with use cases. You arrive with the filter that kills most use cases, including attractive ones. For a conservative family-owned manufacturer whose HR and legal sit under one board seat, and whose recruiting AI is high-risk under EU rules, a candidate who says no in the right places is worth more than one who says yes everywhere.

Built The test applied across seven real HR workflows

The same four questions run against policy questions, interview scheduling, drafting, engagement and exit feedback, workforce reporting, candidate screening, and performance calibration. Each gets a verdict.

What it proves, and this is the part that will land: the test produces different answers for work that looks superficially similar. Screening is the first thing every function reaches for, and it is the one place the test says slow down. Answering the same policy question hundreds of times a month is unglamorous, entirely safe, and returns real hours immediately. Starting with the boring row is how you earn the right to attempt the interesting one.

Built The first ninety days, as a causal chain

Five steps, each producing the input the next needs, landing on the single front door their posting calls a One HR AI Experience. Find where the hours actually go, run that list through the test, take the survivors to the affected people before building, pilot exactly one with oversight designed in from day one, then let that pilot's own numbers choose the next two.

What it proves: that you think in consequences rather than activities, and that you know a rollout at a German-headquartered company ships because the affected people agreed to it, not because it worked technically. It closes with the sequencing question their RISE programme creates, which is what turns a generic plan into one written for them.

Built Where the machine stops and a person decides

Seven categories where your own systems are not allowed to act alone: it reaches a real person, it is published under a name, it touches money, it destroys or moves data irreversibly, it changes who can access what, somebody's livelihood rides on it, and it would weaken one of the rules itself.

What it proves: the thing almost nobody else in that process will have. You wrote the governance before the system went live rather than after something went wrong. It is also the most transferable thing you own, because a damage list is scale-independent. The categories do not change when the company gets bigger, only the volume does.

Every internal name is stripped. No codenames, no system names, no ticket references. Each item is described by what it does.

Why not show it

  1. There is nothing built to show. The spec is explicit that no code exists on the mainline. A tool you describe but cannot demonstrate is worse than no tool, because it invites a request you cannot satisfy, and that is the part they remember.
  2. The real risk is what it says about you, not that they build it. This is the part you had not weighted. Telling a prospective employer you are building a product you intend to sell reads, to a manager deciding whether to bet on you, as a business you are going back to. You are already a founder applying for an employed seat and they will already be wondering about that. Handing them a second reason is expensive, and the artifact was never going to pay for it.
  3. The commercial risk is real but secondary. The genuinely defensible parts are describable in one sentence, which is what makes them easy to hand over by accident.
  4. The principle is the part hardest to copy anyway. A feature list can be rebuilt by any competent engineering team. A person who can say why the numbers have to survive, and what breaks if they do not, is the part that took a career in HR to produce. Give away what is hard to copy and costs nothing to say. Keep what is easy to copy.

An employer doing AI in HR, and a tool you own that does AI on HR data, is an intellectual-property conversation to have in writing at offer stage. That is not a reason to avoid the role. Council: five voices on the show-and-tell strategy
