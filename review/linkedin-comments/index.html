1. Paul Rubenstein — trust vs respect, and why staying neutral is a trap

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/paulrubensteinhr_not-my-usual-beat-here-i-spend-most-of-my-activity-7497740905873817600-zd3Q

Posted 2026-08-24. He answered a Fortune "Office Hours" reader question about a coworker who went over two levels of management to get a remote move approved, then came back and vented to the team. The department head asked the group how they would feel if the guy got his way anyway. Two people said they would not care. The reader stayed quiet, and wrote in asking whether that was right. Rubenstein's answer: trusting someone and respecting how they operate are not the same thing, and staying neutral is usually a trap.

The neutral-is-a-trap half is the one I would put in bold. I spent ten years in global HR leadership for a Fortune 100 company, and silence in rooms like that was never neutral. The department head asked the question because he wanted signal, and no answer is still an answer, which gets read as no objection, so the guy who went around two levels just became tolerable. Your trust and respect split is the other piece people flatten. I can respect exactly how someone operates, read them accurately, and still not hand them a call that needs judgment. Two different measurements. Treating them as one is how people get surprised. Glad Fortune sent this one your way.

2. Trent Cotton — AI adoption is a people problem, and managers are the gap

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trentcotton_ai-in-hr-news-week-ending-august-21-2026-activity-7496542805369069569-fRqE

Posted 2026-08-21, 27 comments already. His argument: AI adoption is a people, leadership and operating-model problem, workers who use AI daily feel more secure not less, a policy sitting in a shared drive will not carry the load, and the biggest gap sits with managers who need a new playbook for teams where humans and AI share the workflow.

The manager gap is the right place to point, and I would add one thing to the playbook you are describing. The hard question managers hit isn't what to delegate to AI. It's what still gets checked, and who owns it when the check doesn't happen. Delegation is easy to write down. Accountability for the review step is where teams go quiet, and that is the piece I watch break first in the businesses I talk to, long before the technology does. Your line about a policy sitting in a shared drive not carrying the load deserves more attention than it will get. Nobody ever changed how they work because of a document they had to go find.

3. Russell Sarder — AI adoption goes role-specific, People and Recruiting at 41x

https://www.linkedin.com/posts/russellsarder_ai-aicerts-aiskills-activity-7498117707456884736-YWUr

Posted 2026-08-25, dated inside the post itself. OpenAI enterprise data on weekly active Codex users since February 2026: Legal 108x, Sales 41x, People and Recruiting 41x, Marketing 26x, Healthcare 24x, Finance 20x, Project Management 18x, Engineering 5x. His read: AI literacy is becoming role-specific, and the goal is not turning every professional into an AI engineer.

People and Recruiting at 41x is the number I would sit with longest, because HR is usually the last function to show up in a chart like this, and here it sits at number three. Role-specific is the right read. Where I would push it further: inside a profession the hard part is rarely the tool. It's knowing which decisions you still don't hand over. A recruiter can learn a prompt in an afternoon. Knowing when the shortlist is wrong in a way nobody in the room will catch takes years, and that judgment is the thing that keeps the profession worth having. If the role-based tracks carry that alongside the mechanics, they will hold up long after the tool names change. Useful data to put in front of people.
