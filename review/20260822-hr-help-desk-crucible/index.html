Idea type: business · Crucible verdict: PIVOT · final synthesis verdict: GO on the 3-Day File · Aug 22, 2026 · one page, everything from today's research, complete, nothing removed

Where things stand right now

The original idea was rejected as framed. The direction that replaced it turned out to be bigger than one product: four bots that share one loop, one billing chain and one review surface. Only one of them is buildable this weekend, and that is the whole decision on this page.

  1. The original idea — a generic AI-assisted HR help desk — came back PIVOT, not GO. The demand is real and proven, but the core function is already given away free by payroll providers, the market sells on a certification he does not hold, and unlimited personal escalation at a Bambee-anchored price is a founder-time trap. Full reasoning is preserved below, unchanged.
  2. The winning direction is “AI in People Decisions.” Same person, different headline and different buyer: 20-75 employee companies already running an ATS or AI screening, with California, NYC, Illinois or Colorado exposure. Two mature industries sit next to each other and neither can cross — SMB HR outsourcing has no AI-governance credibility, fractional AI consulting has no employment-law depth. That intersection is unowned, and it carries roughly ten times the price ceiling of the generic version.
  3. Follow-on research turned that into a portfolio of four bots, not a single product. The people-decisions desk, an ongoing AI-hiring evidence file, a pre-decision risk check, and a white-label module for other HR consultants. They are a portfolio rather than four businesses because they share the same intake → AI draft → his review → defensible artifact loop, the same payment and fulfilment chain, the same escalation queue, and one disclaimer layer.
  4. The weekend starter is Bot 1, the People-Decisions Desk. Not a preference — the other three are each blocked on something only Bot 1 produces, Bot 1 is the only one under a day of net-new code, and starting instead with Bot 3 (the highest-liability one) before insurance is in force would be the single genuinely bad sequencing choice available.
  5. Nothing takes money until the liability layer is live. Disclaimer, a named scope-exclusion list, a candid credential statement, and errors-and-omissions insurance in force — same week, not after the first client.
  6. The boring-and-mandatory compliance search came back, and it came back negative — which is useful. I-9 reverification tracking and FMLA/ADA leave tracking both have real, verified demand, and both are already held by funded vendors who sell on relationship trust rather than self-serve. No clean weekend win there. The recommended move is a cheap fake-door test in front of 15-20 real HR generalists before any tracker gets built. Details at the bottom of this page. The larger distribution, moat and partnership workflow that was still running when that section was written has now returned — it is the next beat, and it is the last piece of today's research.
  7. That broader search — a $10,000-profit-in-60-days hunt across distribution, moat and partnership — converged on a different specific product: “The 3-Day File.” An I-9 inspection-readiness audit plus a dated remediation ledger, timed to a real dated event: ICE reclassified many previously-correctable I-9 errors as immediately-fined violations on March 16, 2026, and a Notice of Inspection gives an employer three business days to produce every file. Verdict GO, with the literal $10,000-in-60-days number held at under 40% probability and a realistic read of $3,000-$7,000 profit by day 60.

    The editorial call, stated so nobody has to guess: these two are complementary, not competing — and the sequence flips. The 3-Day File becomes the door-opener he sells first; Bot 1, the People-Decisions Desk, becomes the second engagement into the same account. Why, in one line: they share the same buyer, the same intake → AI draft → his review → dated defensible artifact loop, and the same liability layer, but only the 3-Day File arrives with a distribution channel that is reachable in one to three weeks — employment and immigration law firms, who were already Bot 1's adjacency #3 wedge and who here become the business model itself rather than a referral hope. Bot 1's hardest unsolved problem is getting in front of an employer cold. The 3-Day File solves that problem for both products at once, so nothing built for Bot 1 is wasted and nothing about Bot 1's reasoning is retracted. This makes the portfolio five, not four, with a changed running order.

The one-sentence version, updated by the final beat: get the disclaimer, the scope-exclusion list and the insurance up first, then walk into two or three employment and immigration law firms with the 3-Day File and work under their supervision — and sell the $1,500-$2,500 people-decisions review as the second engagement into the employers those firms already represent, with every answer that comes back feeding the other bots.

That shared spine is what makes it a portfolio and not four separate businesses. Every additional bot reuses the spine instead of rebuilding it.

Bot 3 — The Pre-Decision Risk Check

The bot walks the standard risk structure — recent protected activity, leave status, documentation gaps, comparator treatment — and returns low, medium or high, plus either a documentation path or a hard route to counsel.

Buyer A 20-200 employee owner, or the person doing HR part-time, at the moment of maximum fear. The highest willingness to pay of all four.

Crucible TL;DR — the original verdict, preserved

Everything from here down is the original Crucible report, kept word for word. Only its position on the page changed.

Verdict: PIVOT. The category has strong, proven demand (Bambee runs an estimated $17.8M ARR on exactly this promise), but the idea as framed loses on every other axis at once: the core function is already given away free by payroll providers, his credential reads weaker than competitors because he holds no HR certification in a market that sells on certification, and unlimited personal escalation at a Bambee-anchored price is a founder-time trap that is already documented in Bambee's own bad reviews.

The pivot, specifically: change the headline from HR help desk to “AI in your people decisions.” Move the buyer from the 4-employee shop to 20-75 employee companies already running an ATS or AI screening. Change the shape from an unlimited-escalation subscription to a fixed-price first engagement ($1,500-$2,500) with a capped retainer behind it. This version has a real moat, a live regulatory clock, and roughly ten times the price ceiling of the generic version.

One correction to prior work that matters. docs/roadmap/atelier/plans/hr-go-to-market-plan.md and its June-era siblings lean on the EU AI Act's August 2, 2026 high-risk-employment deadline as the timing wedge. That deadline moved to December 2027 (Nimble Advisors). Any positioning still built on “August 2 is coming” is now false. The live US wedge that replaces it is better: California's FEHA automated-decision-system regulations, effective October 1, 2025, reaching any employer with 5+ employees, and NYC Local Law 144, which applies regardless of company size. This has been corrected in the roadmap doc in the same pass as this report — see the callout in the Path-to-GO section below.

Why his real background helps here and hurts there. Every incumbent in the generic HR-advisory slot sells on certification — SHRM, HRCI, “certified HR managers.” He holds none of those. On the AI-in-people-decisions angle, no one holds a certification at all, and his credential — fifteen years in HR, ten-plus inside a Fortune 100, and the fact that he personally architects and runs a production multi-agent AI system — becomes close to unique. That asymmetry is the single most important finding in this run.

What has to happen before the verdict can flip to GO: three paying clients at $1,500+ within 30 days, at least two sourced outside his warm network, OR ten fractional HR consultants paying for the AI module. Until that evidence exists, the verdict stays PIVOT.

The gap, stated plainly: two mature industries sit next to each other and neither can cross it. SMB HR outsourcing has zero AI-governance credibility. The live regulatory clock — this is the wedge, and it is US, not EU

  • California FEHA automated-decision-system regulations, effective October 1, 2025, applying to any employer with at least five employees, with at least one in California (Paul Hastings, Jackson Lewis). Five employees — that is Bambee's entry tier.
  • NYC Local Law 144 applies regardless of company size — “a five-person startup using an AI resume screener faces the same bias audit, applicant notice, and penalty structure as a multinational” (Nimble Advisors).
  • Colorado SB 189, signed May 14, 2026 — repealed and replaced the Colorado AI Act, delayed to January 1, 2027, narrowed to notice and recordkeeping (Skadden, Littler). Weaker than earlier assumed, but a dated obligation on the calendar.
  • Illinois disclosure law in effect; Texas in the patchwork (Seyfarth).
  • EU: moved to December 2027. Do not build copy on August 2026.

Against exposure: 47% of SMBs now use AI in HR for resume screening, onboarding or engagement (Business.com 2026 Small Business AI Outlook); 73% of SMB employees use at least one AI tool at work, mostly with no governing policy; 63% of organizations have no AI governance policy (Layer27, ACS); 73% of SMBs say they need more training and resources for AI implementation (Goldman Sachs, via digitalapplied).

Smoke test + lean MVP scope

Step 7 — smoke test plan

  • Surface: one page on /review/ or a Carrd one-pager, mobile-readable, collapsible sections. Email captured and persisted before any handoff to checkout — proven by reading a row back and stating the count.
  • Offer: a fixed-price AI in Your People Decisions review at $1,500, first five clients, no retainer required. Real founding scarcity (five is genuinely all he can deliver).
  • One traffic source: LinkedIn, to fractional HR consultants and 20-75-employee owners, leading with the California five-employee threshold, which almost none of them know about.
  • Pass bar: 3 paid at $1,500+ within 14 days, or 8 booked calls. At least two of the three must come from outside his warm network — the parachute test is a hard rule and warm leads are a bonus, never the proof.

Step 8 — lean MVP scope

Core job: when I realize the tools I use to hire and evaluate people are now regulated and I have no record of anything, help me get a written policy, a human-review step and a defensible file, so I can answer the question if it ever gets asked.

Must build (5, hard cap):

  1. Take payment for a named, fixed-price engagement, with email captured and persisted first.
  2. Accept a client question through one intake surface and thread it.
  3. Draft an answer with AI, hold it for his review — never auto-send.
  4. Let him edit that draft in place and send the edited version as-is.
  5. Show him, and the client, the ToS and scope-exclusion list before money changes hands.

CUT list (won't build in v1): the Slack app, the Teams app, “AI trained on his voice” (the corpus does not exist), real-time chat, subscription lifecycle, per-client document storage, e-signature, multi-seat accounts, payroll integration, any autonomous answering of anything on the escalation list.

“It worked” metric: three paying clients complete the full loop — question in, his reviewed answer out, and they ask a second one. Not signups.

Step 9 — build-in-public plan

Public commitment: “First paying client for this in 30 days, and I'll show what I learn.” Dated, specific. Checkpoints: (1) what the first ten questions people actually ask reveal about where small employers are exposed; (2) what the California five-employee threshold does to a room when you say it out loud; (3) what a real AI-in-hiring evidence file looks like, redacted. Ask framing: “follow along, and tell me if this is you” — never “buy this.”

The disclaimer + liability layer

Adapted from what real HR-advisory firms actually publish (Expert HR Consulting verbatim: “Information contained on or made available through this Web site is not intended to and does not constitute legal advice” and “does not create a consultant-client relationship”). Seven components, all required:

  1. Not legal advice; no attorney-client relationship formed.
  2. Not a law firm; he is not an attorney. State it affirmatively.
  3. An honest credential statement. What he can truthfully say: working in HR since 2010, roughly fifteen years; ten-plus years inside a Fortune 100 (GE, 2015-2025); HRBP roles 2017-2025; MBA in Organizational Behavior and Human Resources, BYU Marriott. What he must never say: certified in any form (no SHRM-SCP, no SPHR), attorney, “ran HR and operations,” “executive,” or “fifteen years at a Fortune 100.”

    Recommended honest framing (a selling line, not a concession): “I am an HR practitioner with an MBA in organizational behavior and human resources and fifteen years working in HR, ten of those inside a Fortune 100. I am not an attorney and I do not hold an HR certification. What you get from me is practitioner judgment, not a legal opinion.” In a market where every competitor leads with a certification badge, that candor differentiates. This is copy in his name and must clear boub_voice_mastery at ≥90 before it goes on a page.

  4. A named scope-exclusion list in the ToS, always routed to counsel with a referral: representation before any agency; legal opinions; exempt/non-exempt classification as a legal conclusion; immigration; ERISA/benefits determinations; anything involving a filed charge or active complaint; termination of an employee in a protected class; ADA accommodation determinations.   5. AI disclosure, stated plainly: routine answers are drafted with AI and reviewed by a human before sending, nothing goes out unreviewed — a live demonstration of the exact governance he is selling.
  6. Limitation of liability capped at fees paid, plus E&O / professional liability insurance in force before the first client — $300-$1,500/yr solo.

The key insight — privilege inversion

The single biggest risk and the single biggest opportunity are the same mechanism

This is the payoff of the entire search, and it came from connecting two findings that sat in two separate research lanes and had never been put next to each other.

Finding one, from the Council's Contrarian voice: the remediation ledger — a document that states exactly what is wrong inside a client's I-9 files — is legally dangerous on its own. Built by a non-lawyer and handed straight to the client, it becomes a discoverable document that can be used against that client if ICE ever audits them. Boubacar would be manufacturing evidence against the very client who paid him for protection.

Finding two, from a different lane: the ledger's moat looked weak, because it only compounds per customer. It makes one client harder to leave; it does nothing to keep a competitor out of the market.

Both are fixed by one structural choice. If the same audit and the same ledger are produced under the supervision of an employment or immigration attorney, the identical document flips from discoverable evidence against the client into privileged attorney work product protecting the client. One decision, three consequences:

  1. It removes his own liability exposure, because he is no longer the author of an unprotected accusation about a client's files.
  2. It creates a real moat. A solo competitor with no attorney relationship cannot legally offer the safe version of this product. That is a structural market barrier, not a product feature.
  3. It hands him the distribution channel for free. The law firms that confer the privilege already hold the exact client lists he has no way to reach cold.

Practical conclusion: sell to law firms first, employers second. The counsel partnership is not a compliance checkbox bolted onto the plan — it is the plan.
