DAY 1 · The "Jargon Shield"
Subject: Why "smart" sounds like nothing
Sub-text: Mistake #1: hiding behind the language of your own industry.

Hey there,

Welcome to Day 1 of The Fintech Founder's Authority Playbook. Today: Mistake #1, The "Jargon Shield." Why founders reach for it, why it quietly costs you deals, and how to drop it. Let's dive in.

The Mistake: The "Jargon Shield"

You hide behind the language of your industry, rails and orchestration and interchange and risk primitives, so you sound credible. The problem is simple. A buyer reads it, feels nothing, and moves on. Sounding smart and being understood are not the same thing, and in fintech the gap is enormous.

I learned this the hard way somewhere unexpected. For ten years I was at a company that loved acronyms. We had one for everything. And after a while, a strange thing happened: people forgot the actual words behind the letters. The acronym took on a life of its own, drifting away from what it originally meant, until nobody quite remembered why it mattered. It got dangerous when it touched safety, because an acronym does not carry weight, and people stopped treating serious things as serious. So when we ran our biggest safety campaigns near the end, we made a rule: no acronyms. We spelled everything out, in plain words, so people understood exactly what each thing was and exactly why it mattered. The day we dropped the shorthand was the day people started paying attention.

That is what jargon does to your marketing. It drifts from meaning, and your reader stops treating you as serious.

This mistake leads to the following:

  • Prospects can't repeat what you do. If they can't say it back to their boss in one sentence, they can't champion you in the room you're not in.
  • You blend into the wall of sameness. Every fintech site says seamless, secure, scalable. Jargon is camouflage, and camouflage is the opposite of authority.
  • The buyer books the competitor whose post they actually understood. Not the best product. The clearest one.

The Reason This Happens

  • You're closer to the engine than the driver. You live in the mechanics, so you describe the machine instead of the outcome the buyer wants.
  • Compliance trained the voice out of you. Regulated language is careful by design, and careful leaks into your marketing until nothing has a point of view.
  • Clever feels like authority. It isn't. Clear feels like authority.

How To Fix It

  • Run the plain-words test. Could a smart person outside fintech understand the sentence? If not, rewrite until they could. Spell out the acronym.
  • Name the outcome, not the mechanism. Not "real-time ledger reconciliation," but "you stop chasing where the money went."
  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence holds two concepts, split it. Clarity is a series of short, certain statements.

DAY 2 · The "Feature Dump"
Subject: The fastest way to attract tire-kickers
Sub-text: Mistake #2: leading with what your product does.

Hey there,

Welcome to Day 2. Today: Mistake #2, The "Feature Dump." Why it happens, why it fills your pipeline with the wrong people, and how to fix it. Let's dive in.

The Mistake: The "Feature Dump"

You talk about what your product does, the integrations, the dashboard, the API, instead of what your buyer is getting wrong right now. Features describe you. Problems describe them. Buyers move for the second one.

I caught myself doing this with my own work. I built an autonomous operator that runs the back office of my business, and for a while my instinct was to describe what it could do: the skills, the automations, everything technically possible. Eyes glazed over. It only landed when I stopped listing capabilities and showed it doing one real, boring, painful job an owner hates. It reads a prospect's whole site and history and hands me the one thing actually worth saying, the hour of digging nobody wants to do. Nobody cares what it can do. They care that the thing they dread is handled.

This mistake leads to:

  • You attract comparison-shoppers, not believers. Lead with features and you invite a spec-sheet bake-off where the only lever left is price.
  • You compete on price you didn't want to compete on. Feature parity is a race to the cheapest. Problem-ownership is a category of one.
  • The buyer who'd pay a premium scrolls past. They wanted someone who understood their problem, and you handed them a brochure.

The Reason This Happens

  • You're proud of what you built, rightly. But pride points the camera at the product, not the customer.
  • Features are easy; problems are scary. Naming the buyer's real problem feels like a claim you have to back up, so founders retreat to the safe list of what the thing does.
  • Your roadmap is your worldview. You think in features because you build in features. The buyer thinks in Tuesdays that hurt.

How To Fix It

  • Lead with the wrong thing they're doing today. Open on their current painful workaround, not your solution.
  • Earn the feature. Only mention it after you've named the problem it kills.
  • Sell the after, not the engine. Paint the Tuesday where the problem is gone.

DAY 3 · The "Ship-the-Product-First Reflex"
Subject: Heads-down is how you disappear
Sub-text: Mistake #3: building so hard the market forgets you.

Hey there,

Welcome to Day 3. Today: Mistake #3, The "Ship-the-Product-First Reflex." Why founders default to it, why it hands your market to someone else, and how to fix it without becoming a full-time poster. Let's dive in.

The Mistake: The "Ship-the-Product-First Reflex"

You pour every hour into the roadmap and the raise, and you tell yourself the market will notice once the product is good enough. So the market never hears your point of view. And a product nobody hears about is a tree falling in an empty forest.

I've watched the perfectionist version of this kill momentum for years. Founders polishing a product nobody has seen yet, waiting for ready. At the big company I was at, we tried to fight that instinct in one division: run things like a startup, build a minimum version, ship it, learn fast, fix it. The mindset was right. Do not wait for perfect, put it in front of people, see what breaks, improve it. (Not for safety, to be clear, some things you do not iterate on in public.) But here is what I learned: people heard "ship fast" and turned it into "cut corners," and the program got shut down. The lesson stuck with me. The founder mindset is not about doing less work. It is about not hiding your work until it is perfect, because perfect never comes, and the feedback you need is out there, not in your head.

The same thing is true for your voice. You do not need the perfect post or the finished thesis. You need to put your point of view out, see what lands, and adjust. Waiting until you are ready is just the perfectionist reflex wearing a different coat.

This mistake leads to:

  • Months pass and you go silent. The few who knew you forget; the many who could have known you never do.
  • A competitor owns the conversation you should have owned. Often a worse product with a louder founder.
  • Every launch starts cold. No warm audience means each release begins from zero instead of compounding.

The Reason This Happens

  • Building feels productive; posting feels like vanity. So when time is short, the roadmap always wins.
  • "The product will speak for itself" is a comforting myth. Products don't speak. Founders do.
  • You're protecting your time, and visibility looks like a distraction. But invisible is the most expensive place a fintech founder can sit.

How To Fix It

  • Turn the build into the content. You're already making decisions all day. Share the thinking behind one of them. Zero extra research; your real work is the material.
  • One point of view a week, not a content calendar. Authority is consistency over volume. One sharp take, weekly, beats a burst then silence.
  • Ship your voice like an MVP. Imperfect and out beats polished and hidden. Let the market tell you what resonates.

DAY 4 · The "Secondhand Expert"
Subject: Why "McKinsey says" weakens you
Sub-text: Mistake #4: borrowing authority instead of earning it.

Hey there,

Welcome to Day 4. Today: Mistake #4, The "Secondhand Expert." Why smart founders lean on it, why it makes buyers trust you less with their money, and how to fix it. Let's dive in.

The Mistake: The "Secondhand Expert"

You teach what you read, and you prop it up with logos, stats, and big-name frameworks instead of the hard lessons only you have lived. You sound like a curator of other people's ideas. And a curator is not who a buyer trusts with their money.

This one is personal. For years I was the expert in the shadows. I was the corporate person who understood the problem, knew what needed to change, and quietly made things better. But I did not have the authority, the visible standing, to make people listen the first time. So I would suggest something, watch it get waved off, and then watch the exact same idea get embraced years later, championed by someone else, who got the credit. I was right. I just was not loud, and I had borrowed all my authority from my title instead of building it on my own name. That is the trap. Your expertise can be real and still go nowhere if the authority sitting on top of it belongs to someone else.

This mistake leads to:

  • You're forgettable. Borrowed insight is everywhere; the reader has seen the chart already.
  • Prospects can't tell why you specifically. If your content could have been written by any consultant, why trust their money to you over them?
  • You train buyers to value the source, not you. Cite enough authorities and the authority you build belongs to them.

The Reason This Happens

  • Citing experts feels safer than having a take. A named source can't be wrong; you can. So founders hide behind the source.
  • You undervalue your own scars. The thing you learned the hard way feels obvious to you, so you skip it and quote someone famous instead. That scar is the only thing no one can copy.
  • Frameworks feel like authority. Lived lessons are authority.

How To Fix It

  • Lead with the lesson you paid for. The mistake you made, the call that went wrong, the thing you'd tell a younger founder. Specific and yours.
  • Use stats as seasoning, not the meal. One number to anchor, then your point of view does the work.
  • Build the authority on your own name. When the insight traces to your real experience, you stop borrowing standing and start owning it. Then the credit, and the trust, comes to you.

DAY 5 · The "Audience-of-Peers Trap"
Subject: Lots of "great post," zero pipeline
Sub-text: Mistake #5: writing for your peers instead of your buyer.

Hey there,

Welcome to Day 5, the last one. Today: Mistake #5, The "Audience-of-Peers Trap." Why it's the most flattering mistake of all, why it empties your pipeline, and how to fix it. Let's dive in.

The Mistake: The "Audience-of-Peers Trap"

You write to impress other founders, investors, and experts instead of the buyer who actually signs. The applause feels like traction. But it's coming from people who will never buy from you.

I have a soft spot for this mistake because the cure is the same one I had to learn myself. For years I aimed my best thinking at the rooms that would nod at me, the people who already spoke my language. The respect felt good and changed nothing. The work only started to matter when I pointed it at the person who actually needed it and could act on it, and stopped performing for the people who were just going to agree with me. Applause from your peers is the easiest signal in the world to mistake for demand.

This mistake leads to:

  • A feed full of "great post," from competitors. Validation from peers is the easiest signal to mistake for demand.
  • A pipeline with nothing in it. Your buyer isn't in the comments; they're quietly reading, deciding whether you understand them.
  • You optimize for the wrong reaction. Clever-for-founders and clear-for-buyers pull in opposite directions, and the dopamine of peer praise wins.

The Reason This Happens

  • Peers respond; buyers lurk. Founders reward you with likes. Buyers reward you with a reply six weeks later. The fast feedback trains the wrong behavior.
  • Impressing peers protects the ego. Writing plainly for a non-founder buyer feels less smart, so the trap is comfortable.
  • You're in the founder bubble all day. So you write in its dialect, to its people.

How To Fix It

  • Write to one buyer by name. Picture the actual person who'd sign, their title, their Tuesday, and write the email to them.
  • Measure replies and calls, not likes. If a post got applause but no inbound, it impressed the wrong room.
  • Trade clever for useful. Useful is what the buyer remembers; clever is what the peer screenshots.
