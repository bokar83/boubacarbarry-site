1. The three scripts, ready to record

Script A -- Shadow AI policy value test: pass

Somebody on your team pasted a client contract into ChatGPT last week to get a quick summary. You don't know this happened. You don't have a policy that would have stopped it, and you don't have one that tells them what to do instead. Here's the whole policy, in three lines, the one I use in my own work. One. Nothing with a client name, a dollar figure, or an employee's personal information goes into a public AI tool. Ever. Two. If you need AI on something sensitive, it runs on a tool that does not train on your input. If you don't know whether yours does, that's today's task, not next quarter's. Three. Whoever finds a good use for AI in their job writes it down and tells someone. Right now everybody's building their own workaround alone, and nobody's workaround is getting better because of it. Say those three lines out loud in your next team meeting. That's the whole rollout. Tell me the version of this that already happened on your team. I'll bet it already has.
Setup: stand or sit still, plain background, no whiteboard needed. About 75 seconds at a normal pace. Nothing on hand required. Count the three points on your fingers if it helps the pacing.

Script B -- The reskilling advice is garbage value test: pass

The advice everybody's giving people my age about AI is garbage. "Learn AI." That's the whole tip. Nobody says in what order, and nobody says when to stop. Here's the order I'd actually use. Start with the tool you already use the most. Not a new one. Teach it your actual job, in your own words, the way you'd explain it to a new hire. Second, pick one recurring decision you make and use it to argue with yourself before you decide. Not to decide for you. Third, once you've done that for two weeks, stop adding tools. The stopping point matters as much as the starting point. The churn of always trying the next tool is what actually burns people out. Not the learning curve. I run this exact order on my own business today. It took less time than most people spend deciding whether to bother. What's the one tool you already use the most? Start there, and tell me how week one goes.
Setup: direct to camera, a little more energy than script A since the opening line is a real opinion. About 80 seconds. Nothing on hand required.

Script C -- Five vendor questions value test: pass

If you're sitting in an AI vendor demo this month, here are the five questions that tell you the truth before you sign anything. One. Ask what happens to your data after the demo ends. Specifically. Not generally. Two. Ask them to show you a wrong answer the tool gave a real customer, and how they caught it. If they can't produce one, they haven't been watching it closely enough to sell it to you. Three. Ask for one customer in your exact situation. Not a logo wall. Four. Ask what breaks if their system goes down for a day, and what you're left holding. Five. Ask them to turn off the polish and run it live, unscripted, on your actual problem, right there in the room. Every one of these has a real answer if the tool is real. A vendor who gets uncomfortable at any of them just told you something their slide deck never will. Which one of these have you never asked in a demo? Tell me, and I'll tell you what the silence usually means when you finally do.
Setup: direct to camera, count the five on your fingers for pacing. About 85 seconds. Nothing on hand required.
Value test applied to all three (Boubacar's own bar): each script hands over the complete, usable thing on camera -- the three policy lines, the three-step order and its stop point, the five questions and the tell. None ends on "reach out if you want help." Each closes with a specific question that invites a real reply, not a survey question.

3. Recording spec -- the safe demo

The screen recording, zero private data

Money Map stays completely off camera -- confirmed, this recording never opens it. The recording shows one real, useful task: turning a messy real job description into a structured interview scorecard, live, in a terminal. About 3 minutes.

  1. Before recording: open a fresh terminal window, close every other tab and app that could show a notification. Pick a job description that is either a real public posting (Indeed/LinkedIn, nothing marked confidential) or a rewritten generic one with no client or company name attached.
  2. On screen, step 1 (0:00-0:30): paste the raw job description into the terminal. Say over it: "This is a real job posting. It's messy, like they always are." No client name, no company name spoken or shown.
  3. On screen, step 2 (0:30-2:00): run the request that turns it into a structured scorecard -- must-have skills, nice-to-have, red flags to probe, and 3-4 real interview questions tied to the specific posting. Say over it what it's doing as it runs, in plain language, not tool-name-dropping.
  4. On screen, step 3 (2:00-2:45): show the finished scorecard. Point at one specific line that would have taken a hiring manager real time to think of unprompted (a red flag or a sharp follow-up question).
  5. Close (2:45-3:00): one sentence on what this replaces -- staring at a job description with no structure -- and nothing else. No pitch, no "reach out."

What is NOT visible at any point: the Money Map, any client name, any dollar figure, any real person's contact info, any internal codename, any other open tab or notification.

Alternative, if the scorecard demo feels weak on the day

The messy-inbox triage demo. Paste in three or four generic, fabricated-for-demo sample emails (never real ones) representing a small business owner's unread inbox, and show the same system sorting them into "needs a reply today," "can wait," and "ignore," with the one-line reason for each. Same zero-private-data rule: every sample email is written fresh for the demo, not pulled from a real inbox. Slightly weaker than the scorecard because it needs invented-but-labeled sample data rather than one real, already-public artifact, so the scorecard demo is the stronger first choice.
