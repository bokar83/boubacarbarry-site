PART A  -  The recruiting money lane (national, audit-to-retainer)
PART A VERDICT

You are worried about the wrong risk. Track A is not too niche - the pool is thousands of findable firms and you need a handful of clients. Keep it as the money lane, and ADD a named local Track B to fix its one real weak leg.

Too-niche worry: not valid. Too-niche only bites if the pool is tiny (a few hundred) or unfindable. Track A is neither. There are roughly 26,000 US staffing and recruiting firms; the 5-50-recruiter, owner-is-buyer slice is conservatively thousands of firms, and it is provably findable with Serper plus Hunter plus LinkedIn. A fractional operator needs about 3 to 8 retainers to hit the money goal. Thousands of findable firms against a need of a handful is the opposite of too niche.

The real risk is not niche size. It is (1) the workshop-reachability weak leg (recruiting is a LinkedIn/email audience, not a local-geo one, so it will not fill July-30 seats on its own), and (2) positioning against a market where most firms already bought some AI. Both are fixable.

Recommended structure: two complementary lanes off one identity. Track A (recruiting firms) = the money lane. A named Track B = local owner-operated professional-services firms, accounting/CPA firms first, independent insurance agencies second = the workshop-seats and breadth lane. Track B passes all four tests without Apollo and is workshop-native, which is exactly what Track A lacks.

The 5-50-recruiter slice estimate, labeled

Start from ~26,000 firms. Remove the solo and 1-4-recruiter micro-shops (the ICP floor excludes them, and they are a large share). Remove the few hundred large firms above 50 recruiters (where the owner stops being the buyer). What remains - the 5-to-50-recruiter owner-run middle - is conservatively 3,000 to 8,000 firms. Even if that estimate is off by half, a floor of ~2,000 firms still dwarfs a need of 3 to 8 clients.

This band is an estimate derived from the total-firm count and the small-firm skew, not a directly published figure. It is labeled as an estimate on purpose - no invented precision.

Now the ratio that matters

  • Pool: thousands of findable firms.
  • Need: a handful of retainers (3 to 8) to meet the money goal.
  • Required conversion of the findable pool: on the order of 0.1% to 0.3%. That is not a niche constraint - that is enormous headroom.

Conclusion: the pool is not tiny and it is not unfindable. The too-niche worry does not survive the math.

So what SHOULD you worry about? (the wrong-risk answer)

"Too niche" is the comfortable worry. The two risks that can actually kill this lane are different, and neither is about the size of the pond.

Real risk 1 - the workshop weak leg

Recruiting firm owners are a LinkedIn-and-email audience spread across the whole country, not a local-geo crowd you can pull into a room. The prior validation already scored this leg MODERATE, and it is the one test recruiting does not ace. If the July-30 workshop depends on recruiting firms filling seats, it will underfill. That is not a reason to drop Track A - it is the reason to ADD a local Track B whose buyer IS workshop-native.

Real risk 2 - positioning in a market that already bought AI

Most staffing firms have already purchased, built, or are experimenting with AI. Sold as "an AI consultant," you get waved off as number 47. Sold as the insider operator who ran talent inside a Fortune 100 and makes the stack they already own actually cut their drag, you are a category of one. The moat is the positioning - use it or the lane commoditizes.

Truth guardrail: the edge is corporate-talent-insider - 10 years inside GE, a Fortune 100 company, in HR, and 15 years in HR total. Never "15 years Fortune 100," and never imply you ran a staffing desk or an agency. You bought from and worked alongside firms like theirs from the corporate seat. That is the honest, and stronger, frame.

Council - 5 angles on Track A

Five independent pressure angles. No validation - only stress.

1. Demand / saturation angle

Most staffing firms already bought some AI, so the "we're covered" reflex is real. Holds only if you sell a tool. Sell the gap-audit (what they pay for vs what it delivers) plus a fractional hand, and saturation flips into your favor - they have tools and no operator. Manageable with positioning.

2. Findability / throughput angle

List-build and pattern-emails are proven, but small firms hide behind info@ inboxes and the shared Hunter key rate-limits (429 hit this session). The risk is not "can we find them" - it is "can we generate enough verified NAMED owners per week to feed outreach at volume." Mitigation: LinkedIn owner-name to Hunter email-finder (not just domain-search) + LinkedIn DM as a parallel channel so you are never blocked on one path. Watch throughput.

3. Positioning / moat-authenticity angle

You were a corporate HR insider, not a staffing-desk owner. A sharp buyer can sense "you never ran a desk." Mitigation and truth: frame as the buy-side talent insider who worked with and bought from firms like theirs inside a Fortune 100 - that is more credible than pretending to have run an agency, and it is true. Never claim the desk. Real edge if framed honestly.

4. Unit-economics / retainer-durability angle

$250 audit to $597 to retainer only works if the retainer is defined and sticky. An audit that does not convert to an operator retainer is a one-time $250. Mitigation: the companion "where are your hours leaking" operator retainer must be built and priced before scaling outbound, so the audit has somewhere to land. Define the retainer offer first.

5. Channel / workshop angle

Recruiting is not local-geo, so betting the workshop on it starves the seats. This is the single strongest argument for adding Track B - and it is why the recommendation is A + B, not A alone. Do not fill seats from Track A.

Premortem - it is 6 months later and this failed. Why?

Top three failure modes, each with the mitigation that defuses it.

1. Top-of-funnel starved on verified named owners

Hunter throttling + info@ walls meant too few named-owner contacts per week, so there were never enough at-bats to convert even 0.2%. Mitigation: LinkedIn name to Hunter email-finder, LinkedIn DM as a parallel channel, batched weekly cadence, and a second cheap enrichment fallback so no single throttle stops the pipe.

2. Positioning collapsed into "another AI guy"

Buyers heard "AI consultant," lumped you with the tools they already bought, and never engaged the moat. Mitigation: lead every touch with the insider-buyer story + the gap-audit + the human-in-AI wedge (everyone else automates and ghosts; you stay on as a partner). Specialist title, one named pain, not "AI for everything."

3. Workshop starved because seats were bet on the wrong audience

Seats were pinned to non-local recruiting owners, did not fill, and the warm-room channel died. Mitigation: this is the whole reason for Track B - run local accounting/insurance for seats and the local flywheel; never bet the workshop on recruiting.

Latent fourth: the audit never converts to a retainer because the retainer offer was never built. Defused by Council angle 4 - build and price the operator retainer before scaling outbound.

PART B  -  The local workshop play (Salt Lake / Utah, July 30, separate from recruiting)
PART B VERDICT

Treat the workshop as its own local thing. Its only job is butts in July-30 seats in Sandy plus a local flywheel and list. Fill it from the densest, most workshop-native Utah verticals - not from recruiting firms.

The workshop buyer is "an SMB leader who runs their own shop, locally," narrowed to 2 to 3 verticals that are dense in Utah and love an in-person room. Recommended: (1) home services / trades, (2) real estate agents & brokers, (3) local professional-services owners (accounting + insurance). The third also feeds the Part A money lane, so a $47 seat can become a $250 audit and then a retainer. Recruiting firms stay in Part A and are NOT a seat-fill source.

Why home services is the primary

  • Utah's economy is construction- and housing-heavy - the home-services base is deep and owner-run. One contractor network alone lists 1,760 general contractors in Utah, before counting the far larger pool of HVAC / plumbing / electrical / roofing / landscaping shops.
  • Most workshop-native and geo-findable audience on the whole list - exactly the "real room, not a webinar" promise on the page.
  • You already run a live local-visibility engine for trades, so this vertical has a warm front door instead of a cold start.

Why real estate is the secondary

  • The SLC metro housing market is active and the agent/broker population is large and locally concentrated. Agents are a proven live-event and continuing-education audience, so seats are easy to pull.
  • Owner-operators (independent agents, team leads, small brokerages) are findable on LinkedIn and local rosters, and they buy their own tools.

Why local pro-services is the strategic third

  • Accounting/CPA and independent insurance owners are owner-buyers, local, and findable (proven in Part A). They are workshop-appropriate and they are the SAME population as the Part A Track B.
  • This is the only vertical that does double duty: a $47 seat is a low-friction first touch that can convert into the $250 hours-leak audit and then a retainer. The other two are seat-fill and list-build; this one is seat-fill AND a money-lane feeder.

Honest limit: I do not have clean per-capita Utah counts for each vertical, so density above is ranked qualitatively from SLC's ~20,000+ businesses, its construction/housing weight, and one contractor network's 1,760 Utah GCs - not from invented precision. Home services and real estate are unambiguously the densest, most workshop-native local buyers; pro-services is the deliberate bridge.
