3. Plex Control — The Deciding Factor

Verdict: Plex reinforces the Home Assistant pick, decisively. Of the four hubs, Home Assistant is the only one with a real, official, two-way Plex integration. The other three range from "monitor-only community hack" to "nothing at all." If controlling Plex from the same hub as your lights matters — and you said it does — this closes the question.

Home Assistant — official Plex integration (the winner)

Home Assistant ships an official Plex Media Server integration. What it gives you:

  • A media_player entity for every connected Plex client — play, pause, stop, seek, see what is playing and how far in. Covers local, managed, and shared users on the server.
  • "Now playing" / activity sensor — counts current viewers with per-stream user and title detail. This is what powers "dim the lights when a movie starts."
  • Library sensors — item counts per library (movies, shows, seasons, episodes) plus last-added tracking. Disabled by default; you switch them on in the integration page.
  • Play-media action — tell Home Assistant to start a specific movie, show, playlist, or track on a chosen Plex player.
  • Automation triggers on play / pause / stop — e.g. dim lights on play, restore on pause, lights up on stop, or on the built-in credits/intro markers.

Auth / token: you connect by signing in as a Plex server administrator, or by supplying a Plex auth token. It then auto-discovers the best way to reach the server — local address, public address, a plex.direct secure subdomain, or a Plex relay — and prefers local and secure. Multiple Plex servers and multiple users are supported.

Known caveats (HA Plex integration)

Playback control is not available for "remote" clients or for Plex Desktop & Web (state and sensors still work; you just cannot push play/pause to those specific client types). A token is required for playback commands. And if your router runs DNS-rebind protection it can block the plex.direct path — a network-config fix, not a dealbreaker. None of these affect the core "dim the lights when Plex plays" use case.

The other three — weak or nonexistent
  • SmartThings: no native Plex integration. Community projects (Plex2SmartThings) use Plex webhooks to notify a SmartApp when media starts/pauses/stops — monitoring only, no playback control.
  • Hubitat: no native integration. The community "Plex Communicator" app uses Plex webhooks to expose play state so you can trigger lights — again monitor-only, no control.
  • Google Home: nothing. Google Home has no Plex integration at all (Alexa does, Google does not). The only workaround is casting from the Plex app itself — no sensors, no automation triggers.

Sources: home-assistant.io/integrations/plex · How-To Geek: HA + Plex automations · Plex2SmartThings (community) · Hubitat Plex Communicator · Google Nest Community: Plex support thread.

4. Your Devices — How Each One Connects

The two devices you already own both connect straight to the one Home Assistant hub. No SmartThings, no per-device hub, no subscription.

Garage door — eKyro universal smart opener (you own this)

Rebadged Tuya device · Wi-Fi only · configured in the Smart Life app

It is a white-label Tuya device with no hub, Matter, or Zigbee of its own. Best integration path into Home Assistant, in order:

  1. Tuya Local (LocalTuya) — recommended. A HACS integration that controls the opener over your LAN after a one-time cloud handshake to grab the device's local key. No ongoing cloud dependency; works during an internet outage.
  2. Official Tuya cloud integration (built into HA) — easiest to set up but round-trips through Tuya's cloud every time (slight lag, breaks if the internet or Tuya's cloud is down).
  3. make-all/tuya-local (GitHub community fork) — skips the Tuya IoT Cloud developer-project step.

Two things to know

Depending on firmware it may show up as a plain switch (momentary trigger) instead of a proper open/closed cover — fixable with a template-cover, not a blocker. And do not run the cloud and local Tuya integrations on the same device at once; they conflict. Pick one (LocalTuya).

AC — Zen Thermostat, model Zen-01-W (you own this)

Plain Zigbee device · no Wi-Fi · no proprietary Zen hub · SmartThings NOT required

Confirmed via the Zigbee2MQTT device page and a real GitHub issue: the Zen Thermostat is a plain Zigbee (Zigbee HA 1.2) device — not Z-Wave, no Wi-Fi, and it has no proprietary Zen hub. SmartThings was just one option you tried, never a requirement. It pairs directly to Home Assistant through the ZBT-2 dongle (the ZBT-2 is a Zigbee coordinator — it is the hub). You get a full climate entity: heating/cooling setpoint, local temperature, system mode, fan mode.

One real caveat — use Zigbee2MQTT, not ZHA, for this thermostat

A confirmed open bug (home-assistant/core #100570, "ZHA Zigbee cannot change setpoint on Zen Thermostat") means recent ZHA can read the thermostat but fails to reliably change the setpoint on this exact device. Fix: pair it via Zigbee2MQTT instead of ZHA. Both run on the same ZBT-2 dongle, so no extra hardware either way.

Bottom line on your devices

Garage (Tuya/eKyro) + AC (Zen Zigbee) + lights all run through the one ZBT-2-equipped Home Assistant hub. SmartThings is fully unnecessary — no second hub, no per-device subscription, and zero exposure to the October 2026 SmartThings API paywall, because you are not dependent on it at all.

5. Matter / Thread — Where It Stands (2026)

Matter has real momentum — 700+ certified products — but real-world rough edges remain: the "popcorn effect" when a group of bulbs turns on unevenly, friction sharing Thread credentials between ecosystems, and shorter battery life than mature Zigbee sensors.

Verdict

Do not rely on Matter alone for day-one reliability with cheap no-name gear. Zigbee via the ZBT-2 (using Zigbee2MQTT or ZHA) is still the proven path for inexpensive devices. Treat Matter as a nice-to-have you layer on top later, not the foundation.

7. Network Fix — Do This First

You have a Google Fiber box paired with a Linksys mesh. That is very likely a double-NAT situation (both acting as routers), which specifically breaks IoT device discovery — the single most common reason smart-home gear "won't pair."

The fix

Put the Google Fiber box into bridge mode (via the GFiber app) so the Linksys mesh becomes the sole router / DHCP server for the house. Bridge the Fiber box, not the mesh — a mesh needs its primary node to be the real router.

Also watch the 2.4GHz band

Most cheap IoT / Tuya gear is 2.4GHz-only. During pairing, make sure your phone is on 2.4GHz and the devices land on 2.4GHz — not force-steered onto 5GHz by the mesh. Many "it won't connect" pairing failures are just this.

9. Remote Access & Security

Never do this

Never port-forward Home Assistant's port 8123 directly to the internet. That exposes your whole home to the open web. There is no good reason to do it — both safe options below are easy.

Two safe ways to reach your hub from outside the house:

  • Nabu Casa / Home Assistant Cloud (~$6.50/mo) — recommended. Zero-config, encrypted, and it funds Home Assistant's development. The time it saves is worth the small fee.
  • Tailscale or WireGuard VPN — free fallback. Self-hosted, encrypted, no monthly cost. A bit more setup but $0.

10. Connecting It to Your AI System

Honest architecture: Home Assistant does the real physical control — deterministic, fail-safe, state-verified. Your AI operating system sits on top as the smart interface and orchestration layer, giving natural-language commands and writing automations-as-code — but it does not directly throw a physical relay in real time.

1Home Assistant = the safety layer. Talks to the garage, AC, lights, cameras, Plex directly and reliably.
2↓ connects via Home Assistant's official MCP server (exposed at /api/mcp, token-auth) or its REST API.
3Your AI system = the brain on top. Status checks, natural-language commands, automations-as-code — commanding through Home Assistant, never around it.

Why this split matters

An AI agent directly actuating a garage door or a lock with no dedicated home-automation safety layer underneath is a genuine safety and reliability risk. Home Assistant provides that layer; your AI system commands through it. Reliability first, intelligence on top.
