The Operational Pain Toolkit

The questions I ask to find out where a business is bleeding, and what to listen for in the answer. Built for the architect conversation. Runs with a recruiter, a CPA, or an insurance agency without changing a word.

Use: live, on the call Opener: where are your hours leaking Rule: question first, tool second Never: lead with AI
Pain is industry agnostic. I don't come in with a hammer looking for a nail. I have a full toolbox, and by asking the right question I know which tool to pull out. So I go narrow on the problem and stay wide open on who has it.

Ask This (the live block)

Open here, every time:
"Where are your hours leaking?"

How to run it

Ask, then stop talking. The pain that comes out first is usually the loudest, not the most expensive. Keep going. I'm listening for which one they get emotional about, because that's the one they'll pay to fix.

The whole move

I'm listening for what's costing them, in their words. The tool comes after, and often it isn't AI.

Close on their priority, not mine

"Of everything you just told me, which one costs you the most?" Let them rank it. Then I fix what matters most first.

Eight questions. I don't need all of them. Two good ones and real listening beats running the list.

1. "What eats your week that has nothing to do with the work you trained for?"
Listen for: paperwork, email, chasing people, fixing someone else's mistake. Anything an owner resents is a pain they'll fund. If they laugh before answering, that's the one.
Reveals: admin eating the billable day
2. "What's the last thing that fell through the cracks? What happened?"
Listen for: "it was in my inbox," "I thought she had it," "nobody told me." Dropped work points at a missing system. Ask what it cost them and let the number sit.
Reveals: work that gets dropped
3. "If you took two weeks off with no phone, what breaks first?"
Listen for: a name. Sometimes it's their own. That name is the bottleneck and the risk in one answer. Follow with "and what happens if that person leaves?"
Reveals: the one-person dependency
4. "Walk me through what happens between winning the work and starting it."
Listen for: the pauses and the "then I send it to." Every handoff is a place work waits or gets re-typed. Count the times the same information gets entered twice.
Reveals: handoffs that leak
5. "How long does it take to get a number in front of a client?"
Listen for: "it depends," "a few days," "I have to dig up the last one we did." Slow quoting loses work they never hear about. Ask how many they'd win if it took an hour.
Reveals: quoting and estimating drag
6. "Who do you have to chase, and how often?"
Listen for: clients, subs, approvals, unpaid invoices. Chasing is pure unpaid labor, and it's usually the cheapest thing on this list to fix.
Reveals: chasing and follow-up
7. "If I asked how many hours went into that project last month, how fast could you tell me?"
Listen for: the hesitation before the answer. If they can't see it, they can't price it, and they've been eating the difference. "We'd have to pull that together" is the whole diagnosis.
Reveals: utilization blind spots
8. "What takes a week that you feel should take a day?"
Listen for: waiting on one approval, one person, one file. Usually the work is fine and the waiting between the work is what stretches it. Ask where it sits and who it sits with.
Reveals: work that takes too long
If they ask what I'd do about it Most of what I just heard doesn't need AI. A lot of it is plain automation, and some of it is just a better process. I'll tell you straight which is which, and if AI isn't the answer, I'll say so.

The Pain Map (the detail, read before the call)

Eight pains that keep showing up, whatever the business does for a living. The bones are industry agnostic. The examples underneath are architecture-flavored, and they're the only part I swap when I walk into a different room.

1. Admin eating the billable day›

The shape: the people who make the money spend their day on work that doesn't make money. The most expensive person in the building is doing $20-an-hour tasks.

In an architecture firm: a principal who bills for design judgment is formatting fee proposals, filing RFIs, and chasing consultant drawings.

What it costs: this one is measured in the gap between the rate they charge and the work they're actually doing.

The question: "What eats your week that has nothing to do with the work you trained for?"

2. Work that gets dropped›

The shape: things fall through, and nobody finds out until the client does. The tracking lives in an inbox or a head, so there's no moment where a miss becomes visible.

In an architecture firm: an RFI answered in an email thread and never logged. A revision request from a client that dies in someone's inbox and shows up at the next milestone.

What it costs: rework, an unpaid change, and the client's trust, which is the expensive one.

The question: "What's the last thing that fell through the cracks? What happened?"

3. The one-person dependency›

The shape: one person is the only one who can do a thing, and it isn't written down anywhere. That's a capacity ceiling and a business risk wearing the same coat.

In an architecture firm: one person knows the drafting standards, the sheet setup, and how the firm does things. New hires learn by interrupting them. When they're out, everything queues behind them.

What it costs: the firm can't grow past that person, and can't survive them leaving.

The question: "If you took two weeks off with no phone, what breaks first?"

4. Handoffs that leak›

The shape: work is fine inside each step and falls apart between them. Information gets re-typed, context gets lost, and the work waits.

In an architecture firm: design to production. Production to the consultants. Drawings to the contractor. Every boundary is a place where a decision made in a meeting never makes it onto the sheet.

What it costs: the same information entered three times, and errors that surface downstream where they're most expensive.

The question: "Walk me through what happens between winning the work and starting it."

5. Quoting and estimating drag›

The shape: getting a number to a client is slow and starts from scratch every time, so it's inconsistent and often wrong.

In an architecture firm: fee proposals rebuilt by hand from the last similar project, which someone has to find first. The hours get guessed because nobody's sure what the last one actually took.

What it costs: work lost to whoever answered first, and work won at a fee that loses money.

The question: "How long does it take to get a number in front of a client?"

6. Chasing and follow-up›

The shape: a human being's day spent asking other people for things they already agreed to send. Nobody's job, everybody's problem.

In an architecture firm: chasing consultant deliverables, chasing client sign-off on a revision, chasing an unanswered RFI, chasing an invoice that's forty days out.

What it costs: unpaid hours, plus a schedule that slips for reasons that have nothing to do with the work.

The question: "Who do you have to chase, and how often?"

7. Utilization blind spots›

The shape: they can't see where the hours went until after the money's gone. Every decision about pricing and capacity is a guess.

In an architecture firm: the classic one. Billable hours against a fixed fee, found out at the end. Which projects actually made money, and which phase ate the budget, is a question answered in hindsight.

What it costs: underpriced work repeated, because nobody knew the last one lost money.

The question: "If I asked how many hours went into that project last month, how fast could you tell me?"

8. Work that takes too long›

The shape: the cycle is slow, but the work isn't. It's the waiting between the work. Approvals, reviews, one person's attention.

In an architecture firm: a revision loop that runs three rounds because the first round asked the wrong question. Spec compliance checked at the end instead of along the way, so it fails late.

What it costs: deadline pressure that gets paid for with unbilled overtime.

The question: "What takes a week that you feel should take a day?"

Pain to Tool Map (which tool comes out of the box)

Look at the middle column. Most of it isn't AI. Saying that out loud is the whole differentiator, because everyone else in the market has one tool and it's always the answer.

PainFirst tool outWhat it actually is
Admin eating the billable day Automation AI fits Take the repetitive intake and paperwork off the expensive person. Notes and first drafts are a fair AI job. The filing is not.
Work that gets dropped Automation Something that remembers and nags. No intelligence required. This is a tracker and a reminder, and it's boring on purpose.
The one-person dependency Process AI helps Write down what's in their head, then remove them from the path. AI is useful for turning a recording of them explaining it into the first draft of the doc.
Handoffs that leak Process Automation A checklist at the boundary, then automate the notification. Almost never a tool problem. Usually nobody owns the seam.
Quoting and estimating drag Process AI fits A reusable model beats a blank page. Once past quotes are in one place, AI can draft from them, but the template does most of the work.
Chasing and follow-up Automation Sequenced reminders that go out whether anyone remembers or not. Cheapest fix on this page and usually the fastest win. No AI anywhere near it.
Utilization blind spots Measure first You can't fix what you can't see. Get the hours visible before touching anything else. This is a reporting job, and pretending it's an AI job is how people waste money.
Work that takes too long Process Usually one approval nobody needs. Deleting a step beats automating it. The best tool here is the one I don't use.
Read the column again before the call. Most of these lead with automation or process. A couple make room for AI. One says measure first and don't build anything yet. That ratio is the position. If I walk in and everything looks like an AI job, I've stopped diagnosing and started selling.

Architecture Flavor (for this specific room)

What I know before I walk in›

The buyer: Principal or Director of Operations. Not a price-shopper, a peer.

The shape of the business: deadline-driven, project-based, billable hours against a fee. Utilization is the number that decides whether the year worked.

The wedge: "where are your hours leaking." It lands on this buyer because it's the same question they're already asking themselves and answering too late.

Where it sits for me: a solid fit. Not the strongest one I have, since my HR and Fortune 100 background doesn't carry the same weight here that it does with a recruiter. The operator angle is what earns the room. Worth the meeting, and worth building this sheet once and reusing it everywhere else.

Vocabulary that shows I've been in the room›

Use sparingly, and only where it's true. Enough to show I know the shape of the work. Not so much that I'm pretending to be an architect.

  • RFI, request for information, and the chasing that comes with it
  • Redlines and markups, the revision loop
  • Spec compliance, checked late instead of along the way
  • Drafting standards, the thing living in one person's head
  • Consultant coordination, structural, mechanical, electrical, and who's late
  • Utilization and realization, hours against the fee
  • Phases, schematic through construction administration, and which one eats the budget
What I'm not doing here›

I'm not walking in with an AI-for-architects pitch. This is operational, and it stays operational until they tell me otherwise. If they want to talk about where AI fits in the firm, I let them raise it, and then I'm honest about the parts where it doesn't.

I'm also not solving it in the room. I'm finding out what hurts and which one costs the most. That's the whole job on this call.

Landmines

×

Leading with AI. They hear it, they picture something huge and expensive, and they're gone before I get to the value. Lead with the pain. Let AI show up later, if it earns the spot.

×

Running the list. Eight questions is a menu, not a script. Two good ones and real listening beats a survey. If I'm working through a list, I've stopped listening.

×

Solving in the room. The moment I start prescribing, I've stopped diagnosing, and I've given away the thing they'd pay for. Find the pain. Rank it with them. Stop.

×

Picking the pain for them. The loudest pain is rarely the most expensive one. I ask which one costs them the most and I take their answer, even when I disagree.

×

Pretending to know their trade. I know operations. I don't know how to design a building. The credibility is in the questions, not in the jargon, and they can tell the difference immediately.

×

Making everything look like an AI job. If every pain maps to AI, I'm the guy with the hammer. Half of what's on this page is a better process or a reminder that fires on its own.

Why This Is Reusable (the positioning underneath)

Narrow on the problem, wide open on who has it›

I had a real tension for a long time. I was worried about missing clients by being too narrow. I also knew that when I'm narrow I'm sharper and I deliver better. Both are true, which is why it never resolved.

It never resolved because I was arguing about the wrong thing. I was asking how narrow my industry should be. On that question there's no answer that doesn't cost me something.

Pain is industry agnostic. That moves the question. It was never narrow versus broad. It's narrow on what, wide open on who. I go deep on the problem, which is the part that makes me better every time I see it again, and I stay open on the industry, which is the part that keeps the room big. An architect has all eight of these. So does a recruiter, a CPA, an insurance agency. Same pain, different costume.

So the sheet is specific and reusable at the same time. That's what happens when the niching lands on the right axis.

The question is the tool that picks the tool›

That hammer is my competition. An AI consultant with one tool finds AI-shaped problems in every room he walks into, because that's what he has to sell.

But the toolbox still isn't the point. The question is. I don't pick the tool by industry, and I don't pick it by what I'd like to sell. I ask, I listen for which pain lights up, and the tool comes after the answer. Diagnosis before prescription, in a market that sells prescriptions to people who were never examined.

Which is why the thing I keep is this question set and not a deck. A deck is locked to one industry and stale on arrival. A good question travels to every room I'll ever walk into, including the ones that don't exist yet.
