The method is documented in this repository and nobody had read it against this offer.

Where the method actually lives docs/reference/pga-lessons-extracted/ holds 1,155 extracted lesson files, including the complete five-module Packaging and Pricing masterclass, a pricing cheat sheet, an outreach cheat sheet, a free-consulting cheat sheet, and the module-5 outreach FAQ. The nine-module curriculum outline is separately recorded in docs/roadmap/ghost.md. So the answer to "is the program's method documented anywhere in the repo" is yes, extensively, and the offer had never been scored against it.

Nine checks. The offer passes most of them, and where it fails, it fails on the number rather than the shape.

That is fine in a buyer conversation and must never appear in a message to the coach or the cohort. The decision package already noted this in one line. Repeating it here because the offer's design now rests on it, which makes the failure mode more likely rather than less.

03  Does it fit the twenty?

Fifteen co-founder CEOs of funded fintech startups, and five publication operators.

The question asked was whether $1,500 is under-priced into non-credibility for a co-founder CEO of a funded startup. It is, and the reason is not confidence. It is arithmetic.

Recommend $3,000 for this list. Nothing else about Option B changes.

Three independent reasons, in order of how much weight they carry

One. The free work is already being done, and a discount pays for the same gap twice

The program teaches that the first project should be free when you are selling something for the first time, and it is explicit about why: one free project buys the credibility you then sell on forever. A discount does the same job by a different route. Both are ways of paying for the fact that there is no case study.

The sequence already does the free work. The founder touch is a finished piece, written from that person's own public material, sent with nothing asked in return. That is the free project. It is already budgeted, already in the plan, and it is hours of real work per prospect.

So charging a discounted price on top of free work pays for one credibility gap twice. Cut one of the two, and the free piece is the one that has to stay, because it is also the only trust anchor this list has.

Two. The floor, and what its carve-out actually licenses

The floor of $3,000 per project or per month is written plainly in the program's pricing cheat sheet, with exactly one exception: if you literally have no savings and need to pay rent next month, take whatever you can get.

His cash position arguably triggers that exception and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise: zero collected, $3,000 needed by 2026-09-20. But read what the carve-out licenses. It licenses accepting a discount from a buyer who will not pay full. It does not license opening at a discount to a pool nobody has quoted yet. No one on this list has said no to $3,000, because no one on this list has been asked anything at all.

Three. The arithmetic, which is what actually decides it

The whole argument in two lines At $1,500 he needs two of twenty to close to hit the September number. At $3,000 he needs one. Reachability is the binding constraint on this list, not price. When reach is the constraint, halving the number of required yeses is worth more than halving the price, because the price is not what is stopping them. No message having arrived is what is stopping them.

Zero of the twenty currently have a verified email address or a verified profile URL. One verified route was found across roughly forty people examined in five discovery modes. Against that, a pricing strategy that doubles the number of humans who must say yes is working against the constraint rather than with it.

The strongest counter-argument, stated fairly, and why it still points at $3,000

The honest counter is that a co-founder CEO of a funded company does not have a $1,500 versus $3,000 decision at all. He has an "is this person credible and is this worth an hour of my attention" decision. Both numbers sit below anything he needs approval for. On that reading the price is irrelevant and the cheaper one loses nothing.

That is true, and it cuts toward the higher number, not the lower one. If the price is not the obstacle at either level, then take the one that gets to the target faster. The decision package's own pressure test reached the halfway point of this argument, concluding that a founder who would have paid more now pays less and calling that the right cost to pay. It is the right cost to pay when the cost buys something. Here it buys nothing, because the free piece has already bought it.

There is also an asymmetry worth naming. $1,500 for three pieces plus a handed-over system, from a stranger, to someone who recently raised, reads as either a bargain or an amateur, and he does not get to choose which one lands. $3,000 reads as a professional quoting a fixed-scope first engagement. The program says this in one sentence: the difference between a ghostwriter charging $500 and one charging $5,000 is the framing of the value, not the work.

The anchor gap, and what to anchor to instead

The program's own worked example for exactly this buyer type anchors the fee to the next funding round. He should not copy that pitch. It is a $30,000 pitch delivered by someone with an agency and a track record behind him, and running it with neither would be the fastest way to lose the room.

But anchoring to nothing is also wrong, and that is what the offer does today. The number arrives naked.

Recommend anchoring to the hour. The engagement costs the founder one hour of his own time and produces three weeks of published output he did not have to write. That is honest, it needs no case study, it is verifiable by the buyer on the spot, and time is the thing this specific buyer actually prices. It is already wired into the reply script in section 5.

What does not change, and what would change my mind

Unchanged: the scope, the 21-day term, ending on delivery, nothing renewing, the three doors with door (c) recommended, the two-revision cap, the capacity cap of three, the rule that he never runs accounts or replies as anyone, payment landing after the first piece, and the decision to keep the price spoken and in email rather than on a page. This is a number change scoped to one list. It is not a re-opened decision.

The continuation doors and their prices are untouched. Door (b) stays $2,500 per month, door (c) stays $2,500 per month.

What would change my mind: a real quote to a real founder that dies at $3,000. That is data and it would settle it in one conversation. Nothing that exists today is data. Every number in the internal record is a range nobody has ever quoted to anyone.

What is irreversible: the first number said out loud to a specific human is permanent for that human. He can raise it for the next buyer and never for that one. That asymmetry argues for the higher number on the first quote, not the lower one.

04  What the closing date actually changes

The finding that it is self-imposed stands. That does not make the next ten days worthless.

Not re-litigated The close-out plan established that 2026-08-21 is a target set at enrollment, that no document describes a consequence for missing it, and that the financing continues on its own schedule into 2027 either way. All of that stands and nothing below contradicts it. The question here is narrower: what is shipping this offer in the next ten days actually worth against that date?

One. The date that has a consequence is 2026-08-17, not 2026-08-21

The coaching call is on the calendar, verified as a real event with both attendees accepted, 13:30 to 14:00 mountain time on Monday 2026-08-17. It is the last coaching touchpoint inside the window, and the stated purpose for it is to build the first outreach message live on the call.

That call is worth a completely different amount depending on what exists when it starts. With the offer decided and the first faucet messages already out, it becomes a working session on real responses from real people. With zero sends, it is a blank-page exercise, run with a coach who has not replied to anything in five days. Same hour, two very different values.

That moves the real deadline six days out, not ten. It is also the only date in this whole picture with a hard, externally verified consequence attached, because the slot is finite and it is the last one.

Two. It converts a lane nobody can diagnose into one that can be diagnosed

The program's outreach cheat sheet sorts every outreach failure into exactly four buckets: not knowing what to do, knowing and not doing it, not believing it will work, and not knowing how to do it better. Each one has a different fix.

At zero sends, none of the four can be told apart. At twenty sends they can. That is the concrete thing the remaining ten days buy, and it is worth real money, because it is the difference between continuing to spend attention on this lane and stopping. The payments continue on their own schedule regardless; the only variable anyone controls is whether the method ever gets tested.

Three. What it does not buy, said plainly

The program publishes its own outreach arithmetic: fifty messages a week produce roughly ten responses, three calls, and one client. Twenty messages total, spread across ten days, has an expected value below half a client.

So the close-out plan's recommendation, send fewer and better rather than firing all twenty to beat a date, is correct on the program's own numbers as well as on its own reasoning. Nothing in this document argues for going faster. It argues for going out with a number that survives a reply.

The rule that governs the whole column, from the program's own module 5 FAQ

On the price: "You should ALWAYS recap before the price in email, DM or on a sales call." It is stated as a golden rule, and the reason is that a bare dollar amount arrives with none of the context that makes it reasonable.

On the call: "DO NOT ask when they have time. Ask for a specific time."

The first message asks for exactly one thing and it is never money. For the five publication operators it is a single yes-or-no question, because a cold first touch gets one decision and not three. For the founders it asks for nothing whatsoever, because the finished piece is the message and attaching a request to it would undo the entire reason the approach was chosen.

The reply script, which is the piece that was missing

This is the artifact that actually connects the decided offer to the sequence. Without it, a founder who asks the price gets an improvised answer at the single highest-stakes moment in the conversation. Two variants, because the sequence has two entry paths.

Variant 1, for a founder who already received the unasked piece

Happy to lay it out.

You have seen how I write. Here is the whole thing.

One hour with you, recorded. Three pieces in your voice over the next three weeks, delivered ready to post, two rounds of revisions on each. A written guide to how you actually sound. A set of prompts your team can run to draft in that voice without me in the room. And a one page process for keeping the cadence going once the three weeks are up.

One hour of your time is the whole ask. What you keep is the ability to publish as yourself without it eating your week.

All of it, [PRICE], one payment. It ends when the third piece lands. Nothing renews, so there is nothing to cancel.

You read the first piece before you pay me anything.

I have Thursday at 10 or Friday at 2 open if you want to talk it through. Either one works.

Variant 2, for a publication operator or anyone who has not received a piece

Identical, with the second line removed. That line is true on the founder path and false on the faucet path, and a false line at the price moment costs more than the sentence is worth.

Gate results on the script, and the anti-patterns deliberately refused

Voice pass: the first draft scored 87 and was rewritten. The rewrite scored 93. What the gate caught: the recap named five deliverables and a price but never said the engagement ends, leaving open the objection the price question is really asking, which is whether this becomes a recurring bill. Zero em-dashes. Zero implied track record, typical client, usual outcome or past result. No refund or guarantee language anywhere; the risk reversal is carried entirely by delivery sequencing.

Offer pass: the draft scored 7.25 out of 10 on the value equation, failing the naming-the-format-instead-of-the-outcome check, since every line was a deliverable and none named what the buyer actually gets. It also left the strongest available proof unused: a founder asking the price has already read a finished piece, and pointing at it lifts credibility without claiming a single client. Revised score 8.5, production ready.

Refused deliberately, with reasons: a tier grid at the price moment, because a second option multiplies the decision exactly when it should be closing, and because the decided package already rules it out. The capacity cap stapled to the number, because three words after a price it reads as pressure rather than as the honest roster limit it actually is; it belongs earlier in the thread or on the call. Refund and guarantee language, per standing rule.

Follow-up, and why it is not the usual eight touches: this is a live thread with someone who already replied, not a cold sequence. One follow-up at day three with a new angle, then stop. An eight-touch cadence aimed at a person mid-conversation reads as automation.

The diagnostic floor: of prospects who reach the price question, at least half should take one of the two offered times. That single number separates the two failure modes cleanly. If they ask the price and then go quiet, the number is wrong. If nobody ever reaches the price question, reach is wrong. Different faults, completely different fixes, and the program's own guidance is to check reach before touching the number.

The trust anchor for these twenty, named specifically

767 cold sends produced zero replies and zero clients, and the cause on record is a missing trust anchor rather than the copy. Any sequence that assumes cold trust is wrong on that evidence. So what is the anchor here?

  • It is not the free email course. That is an asset, and an asset makes a claim about him. A claim is what the 767 already had.
  • It is not the credentials. Ten years inside a Fortune 100 in corporate HR plus a communications degree is real and belongs in a one-to-one email, but it is not evidence he can write for a fintech founder.
  • It is the finished piece, written about their business from their own public words, sent before anything is asked. It is the only artifact that is simultaneously proof of the exact skill being sold, free to receive, and impossible to produce at spam scale. That last property is the one that separates this from the 767, and it is the whole reason the approach works.
  • For the five publication operators, who cannot be sent a piece about themselves, the anchor is the on-spec offer plus an opening line that proves he genuinely reads them. The close-out plan already flags that a generic compliment fails the entire message, and that is right.
