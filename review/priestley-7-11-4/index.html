1. What the framework actually is

The two things Priestley says you need to know

Early in the conversation, Priestley frames it as two prerequisites for getting into someone's head: first, "7-11-4," and second, that the brain is very good at deleting messages — except for five specific kinds of message.

7-11-4, as he defines it

The number comes up twice, both times attached to the same explanation. Talking about why the host's own show has built an outsized audience, Priestley says:

"You have clocked up enormous amounts of standout value for people because you've been consistent, so 7-11-4. People have spent 7 hours, 11 interactions, four locations with you." And later, describing what makes a parasocial relationship at scale: "they clock up 7 hours with you... literally, what was once inside your head word for word is now inside their head word for word."

So the claim is: a person needs, on average, about 7 hours of total exposure to you, across 11 separate interactions, in 4 different places (a podcast, a LinkedIn feed, a newsletter, in person, etc.) before they trust you enough to buy, hire, or follow through. He calls this "clocking up the 7-11-4," and treats it as the mechanism behind "familiar" — one of five things a brain won't delete.

Where it actually comes from (confirmed by a follow-up search, replacing the earlier "the transcript never explains this" answer): "7-11-4" is not something Priestley coined for this podcast. It's a named framework from his 2015 book Oversubscribed: How to Get People Lining Up to Do Business with You, which carries a chapter titled "7-11-4-ing Builds Desire" (around p.31; the chapter is retained in the revised 2nd edition). That's a real, citable primary source — the podcast clip just didn't name it, and an earlier pass on this page wrongly read that silence as "nowhere sourced" instead of "not sourced in this clip."

What is still true, though, and matters more than the book citation: the book itself does not attach the three numbers to any named external study, and no such study exists under Priestley's name or anyone else's. The number is widely, and wrongly, credited online to Google's 2011 "Zero Moment of Truth" (ZMOT) research (the DOAC episode's own title even calls it "the secret marketing hack Google use") — but ZMOT's actual, documented finding is that the average 2011 consumer consulted 10.4 information sources before a purchase (18+ for complex purchases), not 7 hours / 11 interactions / 4 locations. Multiple independent fact-checks that went looking for a Google document containing these exact three numbers (one explicitly titled "7-11-4 Rule: Real Data or Myth?") could not find one and concluded the Google attribution is folklore, repeated because it sounds authoritative. Bottom line: real framework, real book source, unverified numbers. Treat 7/11/4 as Priestley's own rule-of-thumb shorthand for "sustained, repeated, cross-channel exposure," backed by his own experience and teaching, not by cited external data — and don't repeat the "Google study" claim yourself, since it doesn't hold up.

The five things the brain won't delete

Priestley's setup: your brain deletes almost everything it encounters, but five categories of stimulus survive. In the order he gives them:

  1. Scary. Threat gets attention — his example is why fear-based news headlines work.
  2. Strange. Genuinely odd or unexpected things stick (his example: someone dressed as a giant hot dog on the street).
  3. Sexy. Self-explanatory, and he notes most businesses either can't or shouldn't try to be this.
  4. Free (real value). Giving away something a person would otherwise have paid for, packaged well — his analogy is handing someone jewelry in a plastic bag versus a beautifully wrapped box. Same object, very different perceived value.
  5. Familiar. This is where 7-11-4 lives: repeated, cross-channel exposure over time.

He's explicit that scary, strange, and sexy are hard for most businesses to pull off authentically — "only a few lucky people get to do that one." The two he calls genuinely usable by anyone are free value and familiar (7-11-4).

How this connects to the bigger system

7-11-4 isn't standalone — it's one mechanic inside Priestley's larger Key Person of Influence (KPI) framework, built around five capabilities he calls the "five P's": pitch, publish, product, profile, partnerships. His core claim about scale: you don't need a huge following to make real money from a personal brand — "if you've got 5 to 50,000 followers within a high-value niche, if you're seen as a key person of influence in a high-value niche, that's enough of a personal brand to make seven figures." Personal branding, in his framing, is just doing those five P's consistently; 7-11-4 and the five things the brain won't delete are the psychological reasoning for why publish/profile/product work turns into trust at all.

2. Explain it to a 5-year-old

Imagine there's a new kid at the playground. The very first day you meet them, you don't know if you want to be their friend yet. Maybe they seem nice, maybe not — you just don't know them.

Now say you see that same kid again at recess. And then again at lunch. And then you see them at the library, and then at soccer practice, and then again the next week at the playground. Every single time you see them, your brain gets a little less scared and a little more sure: "oh yeah, that's the kid I keep seeing, I know them now." After you've bumped into them enough times, in enough different places, something clicks — and now you actually trust them enough to share your snacks or invite them to your birthday party. That's basically what "7-11-4" means: you need to bump into someone a bunch of times, in a bunch of different places, before your brain decides they're safe to trust.

Now, why do some things get stuck in your head and never go away, even after just ONE time? Because your brain is like a really messy backpack — it throws almost everything out at the end of the day so it has room for new stuff. But there are five kinds of things it always keeps: something scary (like a loud thunderclap), something strange (like a grown-up wearing a hot-dog costume), something silly-fancy (grown-up word: "sexy," meaning flashy and exciting), something somebody gave you for free that was actually really good (like a full-size candy bar, not the tiny fun-size one), and someone who's become familiar because you've seen them so many times, in so many places, that they feel like an old friend.

What this maps to in personal branding: "bumping into the same kid everywhere" is what happens when a person shows up consistently across several different channels — a podcast, LinkedIn, a newsletter, a talk — instead of just one. Each appearance is one more "interaction," each new channel is one more "location," and the total time someone spends consuming your content is the "7 hours." "The full-size candy bar for free" is Priestley's point about giving away something genuinely valuable, well presented, rather than a thin freebie. None of the five things (scary, strange, sexy, free, familiar) are exotic mechanisms — they're just named categories for what already makes anything memorable, applied to how a person builds a reputation.

3. A 30-day plan for you specifically

Grounded in where you actually stand: your niche is locked (the go-to person for the merging of AI and HR), your daily cadence is a LinkedIn comment engine plus posts feeding that niche and, eventually, the Timbuktu book funnel, and your ghostwriting/PGA positioning is already built around three provable layers — noise filtering, relevance, and forward calling. That existing structure already covers most of what 7-11-4 asks for. Here's exactly where it does, and where the one real gap is.

Where you're already doing this, without needing to change anything

  • "Familiar" via repetition: the daily LinkedIn comment + post cadence on the AI/HR niche is already producing a high count of "interactions" on one channel. This is real progress toward 7-11-4's interaction count — it just isn't spread across enough different places yet (see the gap below).
  • "Strange" / distinctive, and part of "familiar" over time: the already-recommended dated public log of forward calls (naming what's coming 2–3 months out, then being provably right) is exactly the kind of memorable, hard-to-fake signal this framework is describing. You don't need a new tactic here — you need to actually start the log if you haven't, since every week without one is a week of proof you can't get back.
  • "Free value, well packaged": the daily AI+HR commentary is already free value. The framework's specific bar is that it should feel like something a company would otherwise pay a consultant for, and packaged like a gift, not a plastic bag. Worth one deliberate upgrade this month (below), not a new content strategy.
  • Priestley's "become an author" advice directly validates the existing Timbuktu book plan — document-the-journey publishing is precisely his recommended path to becoming a Key Person of Influence, and it's already on your roadmap.

The one real gap: "4 locations" — you're concentrated on one channel

This is the part of the framework that names something you are not currently doing. Right now, the overwhelming majority of a prospect's exposure to you happens in one place: LinkedIn (feed posts and comments). By 7-11-4's own logic, showing up 11 times in one place is weaker than showing up across four different places — each new context (a podcast appearance, a newsletter landing in an inbox, a comment thread, an in-person conversation) resets and compounds trust differently than another LinkedIn post does.

4. Honest value verdict

Is the core mechanism novel? No. "Consistency and repeated exposure build trust" and "give real value away for free" are decades-old marketing common sense. The specific numbers 7, 11, and 4 do have a real, citable source — Priestley's own 2015 book Oversubscribed — so this isn't an invented-for-the-podcast claim. But the book itself doesn't attach those numbers to any independent data, and a direct search for the external study people credit them to (Google's ZMOT research) turned up no such document; the real ZMOT numbers (10.4 sources on average, 18+ for complex purchases) don't match 7/11/4 at all. So: real framework, unverified specific digits. The "five things the brain won't delete" is a reasonable, tidy taxonomy, but it's also a repackaging of well-worn attention/memorability psychology (scary and strange are just "novelty and threat capture attention"; free-and-well-packaged is standard reciprocity/perceived-value thinking). None of this would surprise anyone who has studied marketing or persuasion.

Where it's weakest: the numbers themselves are unfalsifiable as stated — there's no way to check whether a given prospect has actually logged "7 hours, 11 interactions, 4 locations" with you, and no external research anywhere (Google's included) actually measured those three figures. It works better as a vivid mnemonic for "be consistently visible across several channels" — which Priestley himself is transparent enough about in the book to frame as a rule of thumb, not a lab result — than as something you can actually measure and hit. Repeating the "Google says" framing (as the DOAC episode title does) overstates the evidence.

Where it earns its place: applying the "4 locations" idea to your actual situation surfaces something concrete and true — you are heavily concentrated on one channel. That's not a restatement of something you're already doing; it's a specific, checkable gap this framework made visible.

Verdict: GO, narrowly. Adopt the "4 locations" prescription specifically: stand up a genuine second channel (a newsletter is the lowest-lift starting point) this month, and start counting exposure across channels rather than just post volume on LinkedIn. That's the single highest-leverage piece to take from this framework, because it's the one piece that changes a concrete action you are not currently taking.

Everything else in 7-11-4 — be consistent, give real free value, be memorable, become an author — is a rationale for and a naming of what your daily comment engine, forward-call log, and book plan are already doing. Worth knowing the language (it's useful shorthand when explaining your own strategy to a client or coach), but treat it as validation, not a new to-do list.
