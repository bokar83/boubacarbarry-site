TL;DR

This is the Cowrie playbook, rendered as a page. It is the same content that lives in the repo as the lane roadmap, published here so it is readable on a phone instead of being a file path.

The bidding rules, if that is all you came for: manifest or no bid. Halve the stated retail and verify the top line items against live Facebook Marketplace and KSL comps. Assume 10 to 30 percent dead units. Run the scorer and bid its max_bid, never max_bid_ceiling. Land at 20 percent of verified retail, 30 percent is the absolute wall. One proxy bid, then walk away.

Guiding principle

This lane runs inside the active 30-day sprint, and the sprint's locked operating philosophy governs it:

Bias for action. Stop thinking and start doing. Decision D-20260907-5291cc (docs/decisions/LEDGER.md), Boubacar 2026-09-07: "It's easy to win because most people don't take action... I'm all about the action: less say and more do."

What that means concretely here, because this lane is unusually easy to research forever without buying anything:

  • The gate on a pallet is the math, not more reading. If the number clears the ceiling in this playbook, bid. If it does not, pass. There is no third state called "research it more."
  • One real pallet beats ten more absorbed videos. Every recovery percentage in our calculator is currently a hand-picked prior with n=0 real outcomes behind it. The only thing that converts theory into a number is buying, selling, and logging one lot.
  • Losing an auction is the system working. Expect to lose 70-80% of bids. Winning most of them means the ceiling is set too high.

Scope of the evidence behind this playbook (closed-world statement, read this first)

This playbook is built from a supplied sample, not a population. Specifically: ~50 YouTube videos absorbed on 2026-09-07 (of which roughly 20 were genuinely on-topic), consolidated across 15 review docs at docs/reviews/2026-09-07-cowrie-absorb-*.md; two prior internal strategy docs; one memory reference; the pallet-scorer source code; and a single web research pass run 2026-09-08.

What that does not entitle us to claim:

  • It is not exhaustive of the reselling literature, and absence of a tactic here means "not in this sample," never "does not exist."
  • Nearly the whole video corpus is one creator ecosystem (a paid mastermind and its guests) plus a handful of independent operators. Every dollar figure in it is self-reported and unaudited, and one operator noted ~30% of an annual profit came from a single lot – meaning the median outcome is very likely far below the quoted averages.
  • The web pass leaned heavily on SEO and affiliate content sites, which are supply-side sources selling pallets or courses. Reddit was unreachable to the search tool, so reseller-forum consensus is a named gap, not a confirmation.
  • Several category signals below are supply-side (how many listings exist, what a lot closed at). A count of competitors is a supply signal and cannot carry a demand verdict on its own. Where the evidence is supply-side, this doc says so.

Treat every number as a planning prior to be replaced by our own measured outcomes as soon as we have them.

IN – Tier 1, start here (weeks 1-4, no new capital equipment)

Small machines and outdoor power equipment, plus small appliances. Pressure washers, generators, mowers, blowers, trimmers, air compressors, shop vacs, and the small-appliance band (kitchen and household).

Why this and not something else:

  • A documented single-operator proof exists at exactly our scale. Joe Earl (video Nqz_W5zKN_Q, the one Boubacar re-flagged) started with zero pressure-washer experience, bought a 26-unit load for ~$1,050 landed (~$40/unit), sold the first unit on day two for $275, cleared 24-26 units in 12 days, and reached roughly 480 units sold of 600 bought in ten months and ~$100,000 in Facebook Marketplace revenue – run out of a garage, with no truck, while holding a corporate job. That is the closest analogue to Boubacar's actual starting position anywhere in the corpus.
  • Unit economics clear the "no cheap junk" bar. $40-60 landed per unit against $90-275 realized. The corpus rule is to avoid anything under ~$25/unit: "it's just as easy to sell an item for $100 as for $10."
  • It fits a car and a garage. No liftgate, no dock, no second person.
  • The season is right now, and it is closing. September is when pressure washers, mowers and warm-season power equipment go into the off-season and bidder attention collapses. Buy off-season at 20-30% of retail; the same categories bid up to 50-58% in season. Joe bought his first load in mid-September for exactly this reason, and sold through the following January and February.
  • Web research agrees on the resale side (supply-side sources, so weight accordingly): used power tools in working condition resell at 40-70% of original retail, with DeWalt/Milwaukee/Makita holding 50-75%, and Facebook Marketplace named the best channel for them because it is local, fee-free and needs no shipping.

Sub-category rule inside Tier 1, taken directly from Joe: prefer electric over gas. Electric units have materially fewer failures; gas means carburetors, fuel smell, small-engine labor and fuel that cannot sit for three months. Deliberately manage the electric-to-gas ratio in every load rather than accepting whatever the manifest holds.

IN – Tier 2, scale into it (only after two Tier 1 loads have cleared and been logged)

Scratch-and-dent large appliances, SLC-origin where possible.

  • Best evidenced closing prices in the entire corpus: a 57-unit Costco scratch-and-dent lot closed at $12,827 against $74,999 Costco price (17%); a 47-unit lot closed at ~16% of a $66,000 retail; 58 refrigerators at $34/unit landed; washer/dryer units bought around $250 and resold at $750-900.
  • "Scratch and dent" is a genuinely different and better grade than "customer return." It is unsold, never-owned product with cosmetic damage only, and it frequently carries a pass-through 1-year manufacturer warranty that activates on the date of resale – a real trust lever to state in the listing, and something no thrift or private-party competitor can offer.
  • The weight is the moat, not the obstacle. These lots clear at 16-17% of retail because most casual resellers cannot take a heavy residential delivery. The discount is payment for solving a logistics problem once.
  • B-Stock lists Salt Lake City as a shipping origin, which if regularly available is the single biggest freight lever we have. Flagged, not confirmed: the B-Stock FAQ page names Monroe Township NJ as the primary location, a direct inconsistency with the About page's SLC listing. Verify inside the authenticated account before pricing a Tier 2 buy on it.

Tier 2 has three prerequisites and does not start before all three are true: (1) truck or trailer access plus a second pair of hands, (2) storage beyond the garage, (3) an appliances_large category prior added to categories.py – that key does not exist today, so a large-appliance lot currently gets scored under a category that was not written for it.

First bid run – the condensed sequence (added 2026-09-09, target 2026-09-10)

The nine steps below are the full process. This is the same process compressed to what a first bid actually costs on the day. It replaces nothing; it is the on-ramp.

Where the last attempt actually stood. As of 2026-09-09 no bid had been placed, and that was the correct outcome, not a missed one: no qualifying Tier 1 lot existed to bid on. The Utah Pallet Auction event running through treasuretrove.hibid.com (Treasure Trove LLC, 3651 Wall Ave, Ogden, 385-370-0090) turned out to be single-item retail liquidation, not manifested bulk pallets – its 115-lot catalog carried zero pressure washers, generators, mowers, blowers or air compressors. There was nothing there that clears the Tier 1 gate. New auction events post Wednesdays around 7pm MST.

The seven moves, in order.

  1. Check the new listing. Open https://treasuretrove.hibid.com/auctions after Wednesday ~7pm MST and filter for Lawn & Garden - Outdoor Power Equipment. Tier 1 only: small machines, outdoor power equipment, small appliances, and prefer electric over gas. If nothing Tier 1 is listed, stop here – that is a pass, not a failure, and it costs nothing.
  2. Pull the manifest. Manifest or no bid, no exceptions. An unmanifested lot is gambling. If there is no itemized manifest, the lot is dead regardless of how good it looks.
  3. Halve the stated retail, then verify the top line items for real. Never trust the manifest's retail number. Take the top items by extended retail and check them against live local Facebook Marketplace and KSL comps – what things actually sell for here, not what a manifest claims they list for.
  4. Deduct the dead units. Assume 10-30% dead, incomplete, or not-as-described even on a manifested good-condition lot, and price known-bad models at scrap rather than at working price.
  5. Run it through the scorer. python -m orchestrator.pallet_scorer.server, then open http://127.0.0.1:8899. Upload the manifest CSV or type the fields. Use max_bid – the conservative number computed off the low end of the revenue range. Do not bid max_bid_ceiling; that one is computed off the midpoint and is the optimistic figure.
  6. Sanity-check against the ceiling. Land at 20% of verified retail as the working number, 30% as the absolute wall. If the scorer's max_bid sits above the wall, the wall wins.
  7. Enter ONE proxy bid at max_bid and walk away. No live bidding, no raising in the room, no second look. Losing is the system working – expect to lose 70-80% of bids.

After a win, and only after a win: run skills/manifest-to-market over the won manifest for draft listing copy. Pointing it at a lot that has not been won is a misuse.

Tool status note. orchestrator/pallet_scorer/hosted_server.py is a feasibility build only – it is not deployed anywhere public, has no entry in docker-compose.yml, and there is no hosted URL to use. The local run command above is the only working path.

Sourcing process

Run in order. A step skipped is the step that loses the money.

1. Pick the lane before you look at lots. Search one category only, and search it every day. Joe's stated discipline: "I literally look at every pressure washer load every day. No matter where they're at." Daily watching is what produced his price benchmarks ($41/unit was his cheapest for months, until a $33/unit load appeared). You cannot recognise a good price in a category you look at occasionally.

2. Manifest or no bid. No exceptions. The most repeated rule in the entire corpus. An unmanifested "mystery pallet" is gambling, not a business. Pull the itemized manifest before anything else.

3. Verify retail. Never trust the manifest's number.

  • Default correction: halve the stated retail, then verify the highest-dollar line items individually against live listings.
  • Real misses from the corpus in both directions: an LED garage light listed at $15 that was really $80; a Target dresser listed at $1,339 that was really $304.
  • Costco is the one trusted exception on manifest retail (no promo/coupon distortion) – still spot-verify the top items.
  • Verify against live local Facebook Marketplace and KSL comps, not national averages. Regional price variance is large and this is the step that AI lookups get wrong.

4. Read the condition grade, and read the terms of purchase.

  • Grade hierarchy, best to worst: new / like-new > scratch-and-dent > used-good > used-fair > mixed-condition.
  • Mixed-condition and AB-grade are a hard pass. Liquidator terms admit these "can include products of any quality including salvage." One operator called AB grade "100% gambling" – a brand-new boxed unit can sit beside a five-year-old return with nothing in the manifest to separate them.
  • Photo tells: items in original manufacturer boxes grade higher and ship better (a customer changed their mind, rather than something broke). Loose or floor-loaded items, or missing under-pallet cardboard, mean the goods rode a dirty forklift and will need cleaning. No photos at all: skip.
  • Read the terms-of-purchase document, not just the manifest. Some retailers contractually bar domestic resale of liquidated goods (Dick's Sporting Goods named specifically), with severe penalties.

5. Deduct the dead units before computing anything. Assume 10-30% dead, incomplete, or not-as-described even on a manifested good-condition lot. Then go further and price known-bad models at scrap: Joe counts a Husqvarna 2300 at $30 rather than its $90-100 working price because he knows its failure rate, and he skips any load carrying ten or more of them. Model-level blacklists beat category-level ones.

6. Compute landed cost, then set the ceiling.

  • Landed cost = hammer price + platform/buyer fees + freight + accessorials (liftgate ~$50, residential ~$50) + storage + disposal + your own hours.
  • Freight is displayed before you bid on B-Stock. Every "shipping wrecked my pallet" story is a discipline failure, not an information failure.
  • Geography is arithmetic, not feeling. Joe buys from New Jersey, Michigan and Georgia and paid $1,000 in shipping without blinking, because he evaluated the whole load. He avoids Florida because Florida lots have empirically been bad for him, which is a measured preference, not a rule. Plan for transit variance: one of his loads was bought 7/21 and arrived 8/13, twenty-three days.
  • The ceiling: land at 20% of verified retail as the working number, 30% as the absolute wall. The corpus itself ranges from 10% to 30% depending on source and category, which is a 3x spread and not a consensus. 20/30 is our settled internal number and stays until our own ledger says otherwise.
  • Sanity check on the exit: expect to sell at 50-70% of retail for A-grade and 30-50% for B-grade.

7. For a high-value or obscure lot, find the buyer before you buy the inventory. Call a category-relevant B2B buyer, share the manifest, and ask "what would you pay me for this" – never revealing your own cost basis – before placing a bid. One operator pre-negotiated an $8-10k wholesale exit before bidding $3,000 on a furniture lot. Inventory is never the hard part; a committed buyer is.

8. Bid once, then walk away.

  • Auctions use a soft close: a bid inside the last 5 minutes extends the clock by 3 minutes. Sniping does not work. Watching the clock only produces emotional re-bidding, which the corpus names as the single most common way margin is destroyed.
  • Enter your true maximum as one proxy bid, early, and leave. "Buy with math, sell with emotion."
  • Timing edges: weekend-closing lots reportedly clear lower on lighter attention (widely repeated, but flagged unverified for the Costco marketplace specifically – treat as plausible folklore, not mechanism). Newly onboarded sellers and categories get less attention. A "no bids, ending soon" filter surfaces underbid lots.
  • Watch the bidder count where it is exposed (visible on Costco lots, closed off on Home Depot). Joe took a patio truckload at $173/item specifically because it had 3 unique bidders while the comparable furniture lots had 8. Fewer bidders is the whole edge.
  • Expect to lose 70-80% of your bids. Winning most of them is the alarm.

9. Source diversity, from day one. Never depend on one platform. Named channels: B-Stock (Costco, Target, Amazon, Walmart marketplaces), the local Utah Pallet Auction in Ogden – which uniquely allows physical pre-bid inspection and offers Buy It Now, GovDeals, GSA, Municibid, liquidation.com, and direct-from-retailer on store closures. Pallet 1 should come from Utah Pallet Auction precisely because it closes the inspection gap B-Stock structurally cannot.

The calculator's role

Two tools, deliberately separate, and using the wrong one at the wrong moment is the failure mode they were split to prevent.

orchestrator/pallet_scorer – BEFORE the bid, only

What it actually is (verified against the source, 2026-09-08, not assumed):

  • Input: a B-Stock manifest CSV (tolerant header matching – ITEM DESCRIPTION, QTY, UNIT RETAIL, EXT. RETAIL, UPC, CATEGORY, CONDITION, etc.), or every field typed by hand. The CSV is a convenience, not a requirement.
  • What it computes, in a deliberate order: breakeven first as pure arithmetic with zero priors (cash_cost / effective_msrp, where effective_msrp already takes a 5% unit-shortfall haircut for B-Stock's variance clause), then a low/mid/high recovery range (category priors x condition multiplier, ±35% band), then hard vetoes, then a verdict off the ends of the range. Condition multipliers: new 1.25, like-new 1.15, used-good 1.00, used-fair 0.75, mixed 0.85, salvage 0.45.
  • The number to use: max_bid, inverted from a 2.5x target ROI and 60% target margin, computed off the conservative end of the revenue range. It also prints max_bid_ceiling off the midpoint – that is the optimistic one; do not bid it.
  • Freight model: calibrated on exactly one real quote (College Park GA to SLC, 4 spaces, 914 lbs, 1900 mi, $1,444.71), with uncertainty markups by distance tier (+15% local, up to +55% cross-country) applied only to estimates, never to a real or flat quote. An estimated freight figure can only ever downgrade a GREEN verdict to YELLOW, never upgrade one.
  • Verdicts: GREEN / YELLOW / RED, with hard vetoes for an excluded (data_cleared) category, a truckload with no dock or liftgate, roi_high &lt; 1.0, or breakeven above the high recovery estimate. There is deliberately no 0-100 score – that was rejected as decoration.
  • Run it: python -m orchestrator.pallet_scorer.server, then http://127.0.0.1:8899.

What it does NOT do, so nobody plans against capability that is not there:

  • No listing or pricing copy. That is the other tool.
  • No live retail or comp lookup. Every recovery percentage is a static hand-picked prior explicitly labeled "THEORY. No pallet has been bought or sold in this lane yet."
  • No post-sale reconciliation. The ledger schema supports outcomes; nothing populates it automatically. It is typed in by hand, and today n=0.
  • No auction API. The manifest CSV is downloaded and uploaded manually.
  • No appliances_large category. A Tier 2 buy needs that prior added first.
  • Not deployed anywhere public. hosted_server.py is a feasibility build with a recommendation to deploy behind a subdomain; there is no evidence it is live. The local server.py's /ledger route is unauthenticated and writes to shared production Postgres by default – do not expose it as-is.

Known-fixed bug, worth understanding because it is the shape of error to watch for: until commit abbaea6ee (2026-09-06), a real manifest CONDITION string that did not match the dropdown verbatim – "Customer Returns", "Shelf Pulls", "Open Box", "Damaged" – silently fell through to "New" (1.25x, 100% sell-through), the most optimistic setting, with no warning. It inflated the recommended max bid on the most common real inputs. Unmatched conditions now fall to "Used Fair" with a named warning. The lesson generalises: a tool that fails silently toward optimism is worse than no tool.

skills/manifest-to-market – AFTER the win, only

Post-auction only. It takes the manifest of a pallet already paid for and produces ready-to-paste Facebook Marketplace and KSL ad copy for every line item, with no skip tier: solo ads, quantity-aware lot ads, and a named mixed-lot ad for tiny leftovers. Pointing it at a lot you have not won is a misuse, corrected on Boubacar's direct instruction 2026-09-06.

The integration warning that matters most

manifest-to-market output is a draft, never a paste-and-post. Joe Earl was shadow-banned by Facebook Marketplace in April, and traced it to reused photos, reused videos, and ChatGPT-written descriptions carrying emoji and em-dashes. He diagnosed it by having a friend search Marketplace and count how far he had to scroll to find his own listings. The fix was fresh photos and a fresh video for every single unit, and varied copy.

So: the skill writes the words, and then every unit gets its own photos and its own video, and the copy gets varied unit to unit. This is also the one place where the skill's efficient "one ad for sixteen identical units" model and platform reality pull in opposite directions – resolve it in favour of the platform.

The loop that actually makes the calculator worth having

Every scored lot goes in the ledger with a sealed prediction of your own (own_predicted_recovery) and your written cap entered before you see the tool's number. After the lot sells, the outcome is recorded by hand. calibration() then reports the mean bias of both the tool and the operator. With n=0 today, the priors are guesses; after four or five logged lots they become measured, and the ceiling in this playbook gets rewritten from our own data instead of a mastermind's anecdotes. This is the highest-value habit in the whole lane and it costs about ten minutes per pallet.

Listing and selling process

1. Video on every listing, no exceptions. The most consistently cited conversion lever in the corpus. Joe scraped Facebook Marketplace and found only ~12% of competing pressure-washer listings within 50 miles used video. It doubles as evidence the unit worked when it left. Music optional, and he uses it to stop the scroll.

2. Fill every photo slot the platform allows (12 on Facebook Marketplace, 16 on eBay). Time-lapse unboxing, assembly or cleaning converts well. Testimonial screenshots go between the product photos, not at the end.

3. The three-part listing formula.

  • A one-to-two sentence credibility opener – why buy from you. Escalate it as the record grows ("sold 200" becomes "sold 500").
  • Rewritten product detail, never copy-pasted (copyright takedowns, and duplicate text is a spam signal).
  • A one-to-two sentence closer on what the money is for.
  • Titles are keyword-first: brand, model, condition, key spec. Buyers search brands, not adjectives.

4. Price at your number and hold it. Joe listed a $400 Costco machine at $270, declined two $250 offers with "appreciate your offer, I'm firm at $270, if it's still available next week maybe I'll connect back", and sold it at full ask within the hour. Set the floor at intake, not during negotiation. Scarcity line that works: "there's been some interest on this."

5. Answer fast, and answer with a next step. Not "yes, it's available" but "Yes it is. When would you like to drop by?" Speed is conversion: someone drove two hours for an $80 unit because nobody closer replied. Use saved-reply templates.

6. Batch the pickups. Offer two fixed windows (Joe uses roughly 7:30-9:30am and 5-7pm). Six buyers once arrived at the same time and it helped – they saw each other buying and talked each other into it, and all six cleared in fifteen minutes. Flake rate after commitment runs ~15%.

7. Know who buys when. Weekday buyers are trades – painters, auto detailers, concrete and seal-coating crews. Saturday buyers are homeowners with a project. Weekday conversations often schedule a Saturday pickup, so weekday message volume is not weekday sales.

8. Farm reviews deliberately. Joe has ~180, all but one or two positive. The mechanic: rate the buyer first, which pushes them a notification, then follow up asking for five stars and say plainly that it helps in the algorithm.

9. Protect the margin after the sale. Hand out a printed tip sheet at pickup (Joe has one for electric and one for gas; the headline tip is never run an electric unit without water flowing). It converts false "it broke" returns into non-events. Talk buyers out of the wrong product – he actively steers non-trade buyers from gas to electric. Take the return, fix it, resell it.

10. Segregate the ugly stock onto a separate account. Joe runs three Facebook accounts (his, his wife's, and a burner) and routes blemished and clunky units to the burner, because a buyer who sees two rough machines in a seller's list assumes all of them are rough.

11. Sell the carcasses too. Strip usable parts for eBay, then list the dead shell free on the burner account. Someone hauls it away, and you have avoided a dump fee.

12. Stack the upsells on anything bulky. The corpus pattern is consistent: $99-150 delivery and ~$125 haul-away/removal on top of the sale price. This is Tier 2 revenue and needs a truck, but it is where the furniture and large-appliance margins actually live.

13. Cross-post. KSL primary, Facebook Marketplace secondary. Never Facebook-only – higher flake rate and arbitrary bans, as Joe's shadow-ban demonstrates.

The absorbed videos are enthusiastic about Whatnot and TikTok live selling, with one operator citing ~$450k revenue in under a year. We are still not doing it, and this is a decision already taken, not an oversight: a prior Council pass rejected it for Cowrie on three grounds – 8-12 operator hours per clearing event against a 6h/week cap, a permanent-ban risk (Whatnot bans do not expire), and the reputational cost of a public liquidation-seller persona under his own name during an HR leadership job search. Revisit only if the time cap changes or the job search closes.

Sales channels and payment safety

Added 2026-09-08 in answer to Boubacar's direct question: where do we sell, which channel takes the least in fees, cash or an app, and how do we not get robbed. This section adds to the listing process above; it does not replace step 13.

Closed-world scope of this section

Recommendation

Primary: KSL Classifieds. Secondary: Facebook Marketplace. List every unit on both, from day one. They cost the same ($0), and they fail differently.

  • KSL is the trust anchor. It is the recognized Utah marketplace, the audience is regional and non-anonymous, and it has no history in our corpus of arbitrary seller bans.
  • Facebook Marketplace is the volume engine. The one operator in the corpus at our exact scale did roughly $100,000 on Facebook Marketplace alone. It also shadow-banned him. Reach and fragility in the same channel is precisely why it is secondary and never sole.
  • OfferUp is a free third listing, same $0 local terms, no reason not to. Craigslist is a free wide net with the weakest buyer quality — post it, expect little.
  • eBay is a narrow specialist tool, not a channel. It earns its 13.6% only where value density is high and the box is small: stripped parts off dead units (already step 11 of the listing process), and any single item worth $150+ that ships under about 5 lb. Never a whole machine.

The tradeoff, stated plainly: free and local means the buyer pool is the Wasatch Front, not the country. Some units will sit longer than they would on eBay, and a genuinely rare item will realize less locally than it would nationally. We accept that, because the entire Tier 1 thesis is heavy, low-value-density goods where shipping eats the margin. The moment a unit is small, light and rare, eBay is the right answer for that one unit.

Payment method

Primary: cash. Fallback: a Venmo business profile. Never Zelle from a stranger, never friends-and-family, never a check of any kind.

The non-obvious reason, and the one that decides this: the "buyer protection" on every app rail is a risk to us, not a benefit. Venmo's and PayPal's Purchase Protection reimburse the buyer; the matching seller protection is built around proof of shipment and delivery to a verified address, which a hand-off in a driveway can never produce. So a chargeback-capable rail means a buyer can take the machine, dispute the payment, and we have no tracking number to defend with. Cash is the only rail with no reversal path at all. It is simultaneously the cheapest (0%) and the safest for our specific sale shape.

The payment procedure, run every single time

Not theory. This is the checklist at the garage door.

  1. Every listing says cash on pickup, local only. Setting the expectation in the ad removes 90% of the conversation.
  2. Payment clears before the item moves. The unit stays in your hands, physically, until step 4 or 5 passes. Not "as he's loading it." Before.
  3. Cash: count it in front of the buyer. Keep a counterfeit-detection pen at the intake station and use it on every $50 and $100 bill. For anything over ~$500, meet at your bank's lobby during business hours and deposit it before he drives off.
  4. App payment: you open your own app, on your own phone, on cellular data. Not his hotspot, not his phone, not a link he sends. Refresh the balance yourself.
  5. A screenshot is not a payment. An email is not a payment. A text is not a payment. The most common Marketplace scam in the 2026 reporting is a forged payment confirmation — there are apps built specifically to generate them. If it is not visible in your app, you have not been paid.
  6. "Pending" is not paid. Wait for completed and available. If it is pending when he wants to leave, the item does not leave.
  7. No shipping to a first-time buyer. Ever, in Tier 1. Local pickup only. This single rule deletes the entire category of shipping-based fraud and costs us nothing, because the model was never going to ship a pressure washer anyway.
  8. Never share a verification code sent to your phone, no matter what the buyer says it is for. A "just verify you're a real person" code is the Google Voice number-theft scam, and it takes your phone number.
  9. Hard-refuse these openers, without negotiating: an offer to pay more than asking; any cashier's check or money order; "I'm out of the country / my nephew will pick it up"; a deposit to hold it sight-unseen; a request for your bank details or account number; a buyer who will not talk on the platform.
  10. Meetups. Daytime only. For our bulky Tier 1 units, driveway or garage-front is normal and fine — but never invite a stranger into the house, have someone home, and screenshot the buyer's platform profile before the meet. For anything you agree to hand over off-site, or any sale over ~$500, use a police-station Safe Exchange Zone (lit, camera-covered lots that most Wasatch Front departments provide).
  11. Log the payment rail in the ledger row alongside the sale. If a rail starts producing disputes, we want the count, not the impression.
  12. KSL has a report flag on every listing (the blue flag icon). Use it on the fraud attempts rather than just blocking. It is free and it thins the pool.

Whatnot: rejection UNCHANGED

The standing Council rejection of live selling stands, and this research did not reverse it. It was never a fee decision, so the fee data is not a reason to reopen it. The three original grounds were 8-12 operator hours per event against a 6h/week cap, permanent-ban risk, and an unwanted public liquidation persona during an active HR leadership job search.

New evidence found on 2026-09-08 strengthens two of the three:

  • Ban risk confirmed at source. Whatnot's own help center states that permanent penalties, including bans and loss of selling access, do not expire, that a banned seller permanently loses the ability to sell or host shows, and that creating a new account to get around it is itself a further violation. The original concern was correct and is now vendor-documented rather than inferred.
  • Fees are middling, not disqualifying: 8% commission plus 2.9% + $0.30 processing, roughly 11.2% on a $200 sale. Cheaper than eBay. Irrelevant, because the constraint is hours and exposure, not percent.

No change proposed. If Boubacar wants to reopen it, the trigger conditions are the ones already written: the time cap changes, or the job search closes.

TikTok: OPEN QUESTION for Boubacar, with a recommendation of NO for now

He raised this himself and said plainly he is not familiar with it. It was not part of the original locked decision, so it is genuinely open — this is input, not a verdict, and it is his call.

The recommendation is no, for Tier 1, on three grounds that have nothing to do with the headline fee:

  1. TikTok Shop is a shipped-commerce channel. It has no local-pickup mode. Selling a 60 lb pressure washer through it means boxing and freighting it, which is the exact cost the entire channel strategy above exists to avoid. The fee is the small problem; the freight is the big one.
  2. The real take is not 6-8%. The referral fee sits at 6% historically, reportedly raised to 8% for most US non-food categories on 2026-08-04 (single unverified source — check the seller dashboard before trusting it). But that is the floor. Stack creator commissions (typically 5-20%), fulfillment at $2.86-4.28 per unit, and ad spend, and one source puts the realistic all-in platform-plus-channel take near 30% of the selling price. A channel that only performs with paid creators and ads is a marketing operation, not a sales channel.
  3. It carries the same persona exposure that killed live selling, for the same reason: TikTok Shop is content-first and rewards volume of face-forward video. That is the identical reputational concern already logged against a public liquidation persona during an HR leadership job search. Deciding yes on TikTok without deciding yes on that exposure would be inconsistent.

The one narrow version that might be worth something later, if he wants it: TikTok as organic top-of-funnel only — short clips of units being cleaned and tested, driving traffic to the free KSL and Facebook listings, with no TikTok Shop account and no shipping. That keeps 100% of the money and adds zero fee. It still costs filming and editing hours against the 6h/week cap, and it still carries the persona question. That is a content-lane decision, not a sales-channel one, and it should be decided as such.

Decision needed from him: (a) no TikTok, matching the recommendation; (b) organic TikTok only, no Shop, accepting the hours and the persona exposure; or (c) full TikTok Shop, which would require reversing the shipping constraint and should not be done inside Tier 1.

Red flags and hard no's

Consolidated. Any one of these kills a bid on its own.

Bid-side

  1. No manifest, no bid. Mystery pallets are gambling.
  2. Mixed-condition or AB-grade lots. Can legally include salvage.
  3. Manifest retail taken at face value. Halve it and verify the top line items.
  4. Consumer electronics as a generalist. Thin margin, untestable defects.
  5. data_cleared / R2-gated goods. Legal gate, hard veto in the tool.
  6. Export-restricted lots. Read the terms of purchase, not just the manifest.
  7. Regulated or recert-dependent items. The compliance cliff is usually why the lot was cheap.
  8. Recalled or recall-prone children's products. Strict liability; ignorance is not a defence.
  9. Structural damage – frame damage, large rips, cracked housings – unless you personally do that repair.
  10. A lot whose margin rests on one or two high-value units. One damaged item wipes the load.
  11. A truckload with no dock, liftgate or forklift. Automatic veto.
  12. Saturated hype items (Stanley tumblers and similar). Already picked over.
  13. Sub-$25 units without exceptional volume.
  14. Bidding while watching the clock. Proxy bid once, walk away.
  15. Shoes and clothing, including baby clothes. Per the category table above.
  16. A model you know fails. Blacklist by model, not just by category.

Sell-side and operations

  1. Reused photos, reused video, or AI-tell copy (emoji, em-dashes). Real shadow-ban risk, confirmed by an operator it happened to.
  2. A single supplier or a single sales channel. Both are single points of failure.
  3. Fixed overhead before the model is proven. No leased warehouse, no second storage unit, on speculation.
  4. The death pile. Unsorted, unpriced inventory is not paper profit, it is trapped cash.
  5. Shipping direct from the liquidator to your downstream buyer. The pallet label exposes your source and they cut you out next time.
  6. Storage-unit lease fine print. Reselling out of a unit violates many leases.
  7. Exposing server.py's /ledger route. Unauthenticated, writes to production Postgres.

Week 2 (Sept 15-21) – intake, list, and sell the first unit

  1. One 3-hour batch intake session for the whole pallet: unbox, test, grade, photograph and video every unit in a single pass. Per-item processing across the week is the throughput killer.
  2. Run manifest-to-market over the won manifest for draft copy, then shoot unique photos and a unique video per unit and vary the text. Do not paste identical listings.
  3. List on KSL primary, Facebook Marketplace secondary. Set up a second selling account now, before you need it, and route blemished units to it.
  4. Sell the strongest unit first, deliberately, for the early win. Joe cleared his best unit on day two at $275.
  5. Recruit a repair partner using Joe's exact structure (~1h). Post an ad on Marketplace: they pick up, they diagnose and fix without adding parts unless they call, and they are paid only after the item sells – 50% of anything above a threshold. Expect ~20 people to say it is crazy and 2-3 to say yes. Convert the one who works out to a flat per-unit fee once trust is established. This protects cash and is the difference between a hobby and a system.
  6. Ski-swap decision point (Sept 19). Boubacar's call, not an agent's. Take it or let it go, but decide.

Week 3 (Sept 22-28) – prove the ceiling on a shipped lot

  1. Buy pallet 2 from B-Stock, off-season, using the price benchmark two weeks of daily watching has now produced. Verify whether SLC-origin lots are genuinely available in the authenticated account, and settle the About-page vs FAQ-page inconsistency while you are in there.
  2. Confirm every unit the repair partner returns starts up in front of you before paying. Warranty it back for a couple of weeks.
  3. Log the first outcome row on pallet 1: actual gross, actual hours, units sold and unsold, days to clear. This is the point where the tool stops guessing.
  4. First calibration read. How far off were the priors, and how far off were you? Both numbers matter.

Week 4 (Sept 29 - Oct 5) – decide with data, not with feeling

  1. Clear the remainder of pallet 1. Drop the price ladder on anything past its base weeks. Strip and free-list the dead units.
  2. Compute the only number that matters: cash collected divided by full pallet cost, not per-item margin. Prioritise getting the money back fast; treat everything after recovery as profit.
  3. Write the go/no-go, using measured numbers from two real pallets rather than any figure in this document. BORING PORT

Per the standing boring-business mandate, the mechanisms surfaced during consolidation that port to a $500-$1k repeatable trade business outside AI/HR:

  • Cowrie is itself the archetype and needs no porting – buy distressed physical goods regionally, resell locally, in a named trade.
  • Repair-partner revenue-share (pick up, fix, paid only on sale) ports directly to appliance repair, small-engine, and mobile-mechanic businesses as a way to acquire inventory with zero cash outlay.
  • Referral-engine acquisition ported from a stump-grinding operator: build standing relationships with 5-10 adjacent trades so the phone rings without ad spend. Applies here via move-out, estate-sale and storage-auction referral partners.
  • Haul-away as a paid upsell (~$125) on delivery is a standalone junk-removal micro-business hiding inside the delivery run.
  • Pallet reclamation – collect discarded shipping pallets free, repair, resell at $5-12 each.
  • The tooling as the product. The sharpest structural point in the corpus: the long-term asset may not be the merchandise margin but the manifest-to-listing pipeline as a productized tool plus the documented operation as a proof asset. A week-4 stop still leaves both standing.
