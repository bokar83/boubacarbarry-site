A mystery worth reading to solve

Inclusive literacy game • Research-stage concept · 2026-09-09

TL;DR

Prototype one mystery, not an entire learning platform. Everyone gets a meaningful clue; private instruction targets the reading skills they need. Strong readers get genuinely challenging evidence.

Existing older-reader programs already work in this space. The hypothesis to test is voluntary, stigma-resistant play plus measurable transfer—not promised rapid catch-up.

1. Verdict

Develop one prototype concept, not a full app yet. The Missing Minute is an original working concept from the requested Astra Medium design pass: a mystery game where reading changes what you can discover. Its promise is social inclusion plus targeted instruction. Neither efficacy, broad appeal nor uniqueness is proven. The name is provisional and has not been trademark-cleared. Track as RESEARCH-STAGE; no build, spending, recruitment or Money Map commitment is approved.

2. The opportunity is narrower—and stronger—than an empty market

Older-reader products already exist. Lexia PowerUp targets grades 6–12 with age-appropriate adaptive work in word study, grammar and comprehension. LANGUAGE! Live targets grades 5–12 and combines private online foundational practice with teacher-guided instruction. Read 180 serves grades 3–12 with software and teacher-led intervention. These are product descriptions, not independently verified efficacy claims. PowerUp, LANGUAGE! Live, Read 180.

Reading Eggspress addresses ages 7–13 who are already reading, emphasizing comprehension and related literacy skills. Prodigy English combines reading/language questions with world-building for grades 1–6. Neither listing establishes suitability for an older beginner. Reading Eggs guidance, Prodigy English.

Our differentiation hypothesis: a phone-first mystery that proficient peers choose for the mystery, with reading instruction embedded in meaningful evidence handling and support invisible in shared results. Private practice alone is not new. This is a design hypothesis, not an established market gap, patent claim or completed competitive usability study.

3. What must actually teach reading

Phonics matters when decoding is weak; it is not the entire treatment for every struggling reader. The IES grades 4–9 guide supports teaching complex-word decoding, deliberate fluency practice, comprehension routines and access to challenging text. Knowledge and vocabulary belong alongside word reading. IES practice guide.

Separate chronological age, language background and interest from reading skill. A grade-8 learner described as three grades behind might decode accurately but lack vocabulary; another needs vowel-pattern and blending instruction. Initial placement must sample these components rather than send both to the same grade-5 course. IES adolescent literacy guide.

A game mechanism is not proof of learning. One WWC-reviewed randomized GraphoGame-Spanish study of 78 first graders found no statistically significant positive outcomes on its reviewed measures. It is not an adolescent study and does not represent the whole GraphoGame evidence base; it is a warning against equating plausible phonics gameplay with demonstrated transfer. WWC study review.

Use systematic, cumulative, specialist-authored instruction: model the sound/spelling pattern; guide blending and spelling; practice it in controlled text; revisit it across sessions; check unfamiliar examples. Pair this with meaningful language and text work. Do not copy Starfall’s art, lessons or proprietary sequences. License a curriculum or author and independently review it. Severe or persistent difficulty calls for qualified educational assessment and support, not an app diagnosis.

Choose The Missing Minute because one authored case can test both enjoyment and meaningful reading without a large action-game engine. If concepts perform equally in testing, prefer the one with the lowest authored-content and accessibility burden.

5. The game: everybody has a clue that matters

Working title: The Missing Minute. A station clock skips a minute; a package vanishes; witnesses disagree. The player reconstructs what happened by authenticating records and challenging an attractive but false theory.

The central mechanic is contradiction ownership: your clue can overturn the current explanation. Reward discoveries, thoughtful revisions and story choices—not speed, reading level or help avoidance.

MVP is solo with a scripted partner. Cooperative pairs are a later tested extension, not open multiplayer. In that extension, everyone investigates the same event but holds different evidence. Adaptation changes the language/support route, not who receives a meaningful clue.

Proficient players investigate unreliable narrators, conflicting instructions, vocabulary and subtle implications. They are not assigned unpaid tutor duty. Everyone has optional private tools. Branding says mystery/puzzle adventure, with honest educational information for players and families; do not conceal what data or learning activities do.

6. One ten-minute mission

Window—not a timerPlayer experienceInstruction/evidence
0–1 minCaptioned animated hook; choose an initial theoryInterest and story access, not a reading score
1–3 minRepair a dispatch whose exact word changes the destinationExplicit modeling, guided blending, then a fresh example
3–5 minRevisit a witness statement to resolve its meaningPhrase-cued reading, supported oral rehearsal when appropriate, purposeful rereading
5–7 minLearn one station-system concept and interpret a new dispatchVocabulary/knowledge plus a brief stretch text with supports
7–9 minArrange evidence and reject one conflicting accountCite the phrase supporting a conclusion
9–10 minWatch the reconstruction, revise, unlock the next mysteryPrivate progress plus a later fresh-item check

Times are a design budget, never a deadline. A learner needing explicit decoding receives more time there; do not cram every strand into every session.

Concrete beginner pathway: distinguish “ship” from “shop”; explicitly teach /sh/ as two letters representing one sound, blend the remaining sounds, spell a modeled word, then attempt a new item such as “shut.” The illustration must not reveal the target word. This is a sketch requiring specialist review, not a complete decodable lesson.

A more advanced learner studies the meaning and structure of “redirected,” then distinguishes redirection from return in a new sentence. A proficient learner checks whether a dense instruction actually authorizes a redirect. Both can reveal the same plot fact. No phoneme lesson is compulsory for someone who already demonstrates that skill.

Age bands are product proposals, not clinical categories. One, two or three grades behind does not map to fixed minutes or a promised recovery date. An adult with foundational gaps must not receive infantile stories.

Maintain separate estimates for decoding, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension. A reasonable prototype advancement rule to test is success on at least 9/10 fresh examples across two separate sessions, followed by a delayed check. That is a provisional engineering threshold—not a validated mastery standard or grade-level certification.

If accurate decoding coexists with weak understanding, direct more practice to language/knowledge instead of repeating phonics. For multilingual learners, distinguish English-language development from reading difficulty. Accent, dialect and speech differences must not be scored as deficient intelligence or literacy.

8. No scarlet letter—and no false mastery

Use one icon, one title and the same narrative stakes. Never display “remedial,” grade level, reading age, support badges or ability leaderboards to peers. Share story artifacts, not performance histories. Private tools appear in every account; skilled readers also use them on harder material.

In cooperative prototypes, reveal standardized evidence cards without completion timestamps. Early finishers get noncompetitive theory exploration. Do not broadcast whose task extended the round. Shoulder-surfing and peer discussion can still expose differences; dignity must be tested, not asserted.

Maintain three distinct records: story completion, supported comprehension and independent skill evidence. Audio can support access and understanding. If it supplies the target word, the attempt is assisted, not independent decoding. Let the story proceed without inflating mastery.

An incorrect answer triggers teaching and a fresh equivalent item, not unlimited answer cycling. Include occasional unillustrated unfamiliar words and new passages in assessment so memorized pictures and plot knowledge cannot carry the score.

Do not make speech recognition the sole judge. Initially use educator-reviewed oral samples where appropriate, accessible alternatives and “not assessed” where the method cannot validly measure the skill. Never withhold the story as punishment for a speech, hearing or motor disability.

Use adjustable text, captions, optional narration, reduced motion, keyboard/switch-compatible controls and pause/resume. A single modality will not meet every learner’s needs. Children’s accessibility and privacy require specialist testing.

9. Prototype and resource envelope

First build proposal: one ten-minute case, three private evidence routes, one limited reading-skill progression, scripted partner, explicit teach/practice/check loops, basic accessibility and an adult-only aggregate review. No open-ended AI tutor, child-facing live generation, multiplayer network, ads, shop, classroom procurement system or math.

AI can help adults draft alternate scenes, check consistency and assemble test cases. A literacy specialist approves every instructional sequence and target-word mapping; humans approve child-facing content. A deterministic content engine is enough for v0. Keep LLMs out of scoring and live child conversation.

Planning estimate, not a quote: 160–260 engineering hours, 60–100 literacy/content hours and 40–80 UX/art/accessibility/QA hours: 260–440 total. At a blended $60–$120/hour, that is $15,600–$52,800 before recruitment, formal evaluation, legal work, devices, licenses and ongoing content. Agent assistance may reduce implementation time but does not remove instructional validation. No spend is approved.

Start with a low-cost storyboard and adult specialist review before code. A focused multidisciplinary team might create the playable slice in 4–6 weeks; founder availability and staffing are not known, so this is a conditional plan, not a delivery commitment. Keep this outside the current revenue sprint unless Boubacar separately prioritizes it.

10. Validation: fun and reading are separate gates

Phase A, proposed two-week discovery: after appropriate permission, consult literacy specialists and co-design with 12–18 learners across struggling and proficient profiles. Recruitment is not authorized by this research request. Test the mystery pitch and storyboard before collecting reading/voice data.

Phase B: a two-week playability test, roughly 24 participants split across profiles. Record voluntary next-case choice, usability, need for adult rescue, perceived age fit and whether the interface reveals support. Proposed progression thresholds: at least 70% in EACH profile voluntarily choose another case; at least 80% finish the basic flow without navigation rescue. Every report of shame or access exclusion triggers investigation; do not average it away. These are product gates, not validated scientific cutoffs.

Phase C: an 8–12-week feasibility pilot, perhaps 40–60 learners, contingent on qualified research design and consent. Proposed usage is 15–20 minutes on 4–5 days/week, alongside—not replacing—needed teaching. Log actual reading/instruction time separately from animation and menus. A small pilot assesses feasibility and variance; it cannot reliably establish universal efficacy.

For efficacy, an independent evaluator should prespecify suitable measures, sample size/power, comparator receiving equal attention/usual support, allocation and assessor masking where feasible. Measure unfamiliar word decoding, passage accuracy/fluency, vocabulary and comprehension; examine delayed retention, subgroup results, dropout and adverse experiences.

Define catch-up with appropriate normative and curriculum-aligned measures. Grade-equivalent scores are not a literal number of school years learned. In-game mastery, a Lexile change or a pre/post gain alone does not establish that the game caused faster progress.

Continue only if appeal survives across profiles and the evaluation supports transfer without material harm. Revise if engagement is good but gains are confined to practiced items. Stop scaling if independent reading does not improve against an appropriate comparator, the effect is too uncertain for the claim, or accessibility/stigma problems persist. Do not advertise a recovery timetable before credible results.

11. Privacy and business boundaries

No ads, loot boxes, paid instructional shortcuts, manipulative streak loss or public voice sharing. A flat family or institutional license with equitable access is a pricing hypothesis to test, not a chosen business model.

Minimize child data, avoid raw voice retention by default, keep identifiable learning records out of model training, restrict adult access and define deletion/retention rules. For a U.S. under-13 audience, obtain specialist COPPA review before a live pilot; school use also requires applicable student-privacy review. Moving the launch to 13+ does not eliminate all privacy obligations. FTC children’s privacy rule update.

These are product safeguards, not a legal-compliance certification. Do not open forms or recruit children from this concept page.

13. Promptfix prompt executed

Research and design a stigma-resistant reading game for older children, teens, and young adults, including learners approximately 1–3 grade levels behind and peers reading at or above grade level.

Use current primary sources to identify existing products and effective instruction. Separate established findings, vendor claims, and design hypotheses. Do not assume an empty market or promise a catch-up timeline.

Provide a ten-minute session; private adaptation by decoding, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension; examples by age and skill profile; reasons proficient readers voluntarily play; safeguards against guessing, audio bypass, public ability labels, unreliable speech scoring and manipulative monetization; prototype scope and staffing/cost assumptions; a staged pilot measuring voluntary engagement and reading transfer; and continue/revise/stop criteria.
