2. What the training did well

It has a real point of view. The workshop does not present AI as a pile of apps. At about 4:40, you explicitly say it is not a software pitch; at about 5:10, you say it is not a coding class or a list of 140 tools. That directly answers the audience’s fear of buying more confusion.

It teaches a coherent operating sequence. The Business Brain gives an LLM durable business context; the Decision Council introduces disagreement and challenge; the Premortem asks why a decision failed before the owner commits. That is much more defensible than “here are prompts to try.”

It makes human judgment a feature. Near the close, you insist on a human in the loop and say AI should not have unchecked authority. That is strong trust-building for an owner who is worried about risk, bad outputs, or handing the business to a machine.

It is hands-on, not merely performative. You tell live attendees to keep the laptop open, build the Business Brain, enter a real decision, and run a Premortem. For a live workshop, that is a strength: the participant leaves with something made.

The commercial CTA is consultative. The 45-minute invitation is framed around understanding how the business runs, finding bottlenecks, and deciding where AI helps—or where changing the system is enough. That matches your positioning and avoids the “AI implementation at all costs” objection.

The edit raised the baseline. The branded opener, captioned master, separate SRT/VTT, normalized audio, removal of the waiting-room opening, and direct CTA slate make the replay usable. The source recording was not damaged.

3. What did not work, and why

Role mismatch: this is a live workshop replay being asked to act as a cold lead magnet. A live attendee tolerates setup instructions and pauses because they have already committed. A cold visitor has not. The video asks for attention before it establishes a specific personal payoff.

The first-minute promise is not sharp enough for replay. The edited version begins cleanly, but the strongest practical promise—Business Brain, Decision Council, and Premortem—does not arrive until the workshop kickoff around source 340 seconds. A cold viewer needs the destination, the fit, and the payoff much earlier.

The method is explained more than demonstrated. The viewer sees how to build the tools, but does not get a crisp before/after teardown of a business bottleneck: “Here was the problem, here was the wrong AI instinct, here was the leverage point, here is what changed.” That missing proof is the largest commercial gap.

Replay friction is left inside the teaching. Instructions such as keeping the laptop open, returning to a kit, copying and pasting, and entering a live decision are appropriate in the room. In a replay, they make the viewer feel they have homework. The video needs chapters and explicit permission to skip the build-along sections.

The CTA arrives too late and is not repeated strategically. The final invitation is good, but it is concentrated at the end. The CTA should appear once after the first useful insight and again after the strongest demonstration, with the end slate as the final reminder.

Proof is mostly self-reported. “I use this every day” is useful context, but it is not the same as a client result. The next version needs one anonymized business example, a concrete time/decision improvement, or a screen-recorded teardown.

Council warning: do not respond by immediately building a complex automated diagnostic product. The audience may need a more diagnostic first interaction, but that hypothesis still needs evidence, consent rules, and a manual test before automation.

4. What I like and do not like about the edit

What I like

  • The waiting-room opening is gone, so a replay no longer begins as if the viewer joined late.
  • The visual system is restrained and consistent: deep neutral, cyan, clay, and a clear CTA rather than decorative effects.
  • The captions make a dense workshop more accessible and give the viewer a second way to follow the ideas.
  • The CTA slate is specific: a free 45-minute working session and a real booking URL.
  • The edit preserves your natural teaching voice and the live-workshop credibility instead of turning it into generic corporate content.

What I do not like yet

  • At 34:42, it is still a long first-touch asset. The edit improved readiness, not the underlying acquisition role.
  • There are no chapter cards or visible “skip to the part you need” signposts. That is the most practical V2 improvement.
  • The edit does not add a short, early business teardown. It still asks the viewer to infer your diagnostic skill from the framework.
  • The static end slate is functional but not emotionally strong. It could be improved later with a spoken CTA, a concrete outcome list, and one proof line.
  • Automatic captions should receive a human spot-check before public distribution, especially around product names, framework names, and proper nouns.

My edit verdict: the V1 is good enough to publish as a nurture/replay asset. I would not spend another large editing cycle trying to make this same 34-minute file behave like a short cold lead magnet. Add chapters and clips; change the funnel role.

6. How to use this in marketing and lead generation

Primary role: nurture and qualification

Put the workshop behind an intentional moment of interest: a Calendly confirmation page, a follow-up email after a warm conversation, a “prepare for your audit” sequence, or a resource page for owners who want to understand your method. Label it honestly: “A practical workshop on finding where AI helps—and where it does not.”

Acquisition path

  1. Short proof: publish a 30–90 second clip that shows one sharp idea from the workshop.
  2. Specific landing page: promise clarity, not education: “Find the first place AI can pay you back in your business.”
  3. Low-friction action: book the free 45-minute working session. Keep the booking action visible without requiring the visitor to watch 34 minutes.
  4. Optional workshop: after booking or opting in, offer the replay as preparation. Tell them what to watch: Business Brain, Decision Council, or Premortem.
  5. Human follow-through: use the call to diagnose; do not send an automated “personalized” conclusion until the rubric and consent boundaries are proven.
