Tessera: The Enterprise Track

Five access plays, the enterprise-readiness verdict, and the services-vs-enterprise sequencing call · 16 September 2026

TL;DR

Tessera is further along than either companion document says, and further from enterprise-ready than that suggests. It is built, not designed: 30 modules, 654 passing tests, a working desktop window, a real two-wrap vault, one pinned dependency. But the Re-identification Trial has not run, so there is no accuracy number, and that single fact governs everything below.

Enterprise-readiness verdict: NOT DEPLOYABLE inside a large organization today. Not because of the code, which is unusually good. Because a Fortune 100 asks for about eleven artifacts and you have one. Top three punch-list items: (1) run the Trial, (2) code-signing certificate and a signed installer (modern endpoint control blocks unsigned binaries at the kernel, and a local admin cannot override it), (3) make the receipt exportable to a location the organization controls.

On services versus enterprise, the recommendation is: do not pivot. Every one of the six reasons the existing plan gave for staying on the consultant path is still true today, six for six. Near-term revenue stays exactly where it is. The enterprise track starts now as a set of cheap options on a long cycle, because a cycle not started pays never.

The honest answer to "what do I do this week" is: almost nothing. Two calendar entries and one five-minute copy fix. Everything else waits for the Trial.

Three findings worth your attention before the detail:

  • "Renova" is almost certainly GE Vernova. GE split into three companies in April 2026. One word from you confirms it, and it makes the target list better rather than worse.
  • Workday is architecturally inadmissible, and that is a clean answer rather than a discouraging one. Every Workday channel needs an integration to certify, and Tessera has none by design.
  • The clock runs against the enterprise case, not for it. The platform vendors are shipping the first line of defence themselves, and the enterprises you would sell to are the ones most able to use it.

The one question only you can answer, and everything moves when it changes: do you want to sell a product to large organizations, or sell engagements to them using the product as proof? This work assumes the second, on five Council voices converging on it independently.

The clock runs against the enterprise case

This is the finding most likely to be unwelcome and it must not be softened. The existing plan warned that anonymisation is becoming a feature rather than a product, and called that risk unbounded. In the last few months it acquired specifics.

  • Microsoft Purview DLP for Copilot reached general availability around mid-2026. It evaluates prompts in real time and blocks sensitive categories from reaching a model. It does not do reversible reconstruction, which is your actual differentiator, but it moves detection and blocking natively into the stack large enterprises already own.
  • Anthropic shipped Enterprise Frontier Safeguards in early September 2026, moving enterprise data storage into customer-controlled infrastructure. Zero Data Retention already existed but is opt-in and negotiated rather than default.

Here is the asymmetry, and it is the whole point. A large organization is precisely the buyer that can negotiate zero retention, can deploy Purview, and can require a vendor to put data in infrastructure it controls. The enterprises you would sell to are the ones least likely to still need this by the time a sale closes. A solo HR consultant has none of those options and never will, which is exactly the buyer the existing plan already chose.

This does not kill the enterprise track. It sets its size. The second layer still has real value: the receipt, the audit trail, the works-council answer, and the case where data must not cross at all regardless of anybody's retention promise. But that is a narrower and more defensible value than "you cannot send this data to a model," and the enterprise pitch has to be built on the narrow version or it will be out of date before it is delivered.

Five ways to get access to people

Deliberately not the obvious ones. Cold outreach, organic content and the free tool already belong to the consultant motion and stay there.

1. Own the question, aimed one level up. The same AI-employee-privacy content position already identified, redirected from solo consultants to privacy officers, in-house counsel and people-analytics leads inside large organizations. Almost everyone writing about employee-data privacy is a lawyer or a privacy engineer. Almost nobody writing about it has been the HR Business Partner on the other end of the request. Available today. Costs nothing Track A needs.

2. The privacy and counsel circuit, not the HR circuit. The HR vendor hall is where this looks like it belongs and is exactly where it gets ignored, against an audience that buys platforms. The privacy circuit is smaller, practitioner-led, and holds the veto over any tool touching employee PII. IAPP runs an open call for proposals closing 2026-02-15; the HR Technology Conference runs a genuine CFP separate from paid sponsor slots. Your talk is the one nobody else can give: what the person holding the employee file actually does at 4pm on a Tuesday, and why the policy does not survive contact with it. Available today, on CFP timing.

3. Free analyst briefings, for intelligence rather than leads. Gartner vendor briefings are free and explicitly do not require client status, requested through a public form, typically answered in two to three business days. This produces no leads and no Magic Quadrant inclusion, and anyone selling it as a channel has misunderstood it. What it buys is a calibrated outside read on the positioning from someone with no stake in the answer, which is the cheapest market intelligence available to an operator with no customers to learn from. Available today. One form, one hour.

4. HRIS boutique implementation partners as a CHANNEL, never as a buyer. Firms of the TopBloc / Invisors / CrossVue / Intecrowd class are already inside the enterprise, already holding real employee data during a migration, and already facing your exact problem. They have the access and the client relationship you do not. This is the highest-value play on the list because it converts a multi-quarter enterprise cycle into a two-call sale to a 20-person firm. Aim at the boutiques deliberately, not the Big Four, who have their own tooling and no reason to depend on a one-person vendor. Needs the Trial first. A partner puts its own delivery reputation behind this, and "I have not measured it" ends that conversation permanently.

5. A free anonymization audit for a company's internal AI-governance task force. Nearly every large organization now has some standing body chartered to answer how people can use AI on company data without an incident. They are typically under-resourced, doing it alongside day jobs, with no budget and no vendor relationship to protect, which is the only combination in a large enterprise where a stranger gets a meeting. Offer a real bounded assessment, not a demo. The trap: an audit that concludes "therefore buy my tool" is a pitch in a lab coat and they will read it that way in the first paragraph. It has to be genuinely willing to conclude they do not need you. Run one as a test, not as a program.

Three things deliberately NOT on this list:

  • The Workday ecosystem. Drafted as a play and removed on the research. See the named-targets section.
  • Open-sourcing part of the engine. The only part worth open-sourcing is the part whose value is that it is verified, and it would compete with a free Microsoft-backed library on its own ground. A worse Presidio is not a distribution strategy.
  • Buying a conference booth. Out of reach, and a booth competes on budget. The CFP is the same room entered through the door where the argument matters more than the spend.

How it would actually get used inside a large organization

There is no viable bottom-up path, and this is the opposite of the instinct. Three reasons, and the third settles it:

  1. Modern endpoint control does not discourage an unsigned executable, it blocks it at the kernel. Microsoft has stopped developing AppLocker and is moving organizations to WDAC, which evaluates a binary before it loads and cannot be overridden by a local administrator. Enterprises increasingly pair it with a managed-installer policy where only software delivered through Intune or SCCM is trusted. This is not "IT would frown on it." It is "the program does not start."
  2. The vault binds to the machine and the user account by design. A laptop refresh loses every live engagement's ability to de-anonymize.
  3. The product sells defensibility, and a shadow-IT deployment is by definition indefensible. An analyst quietly using an unapproved tool on employee PII has not reduced exposure, they have created a finding. Tessera cannot be adopted the way a note-taking app is, because the thing it sells is exactly what shadow IT destroys.

So it goes top-down, through the approval function, from day one.

The receipt is the wedge into that conversation — and Council narrowed this claim, so hold the narrow version. Look at what each approval function needs, and notice only one row is about anonymization at all:

FunctionWhat they need to sign offWhat Tessera gives them
Privacy / DPOA record of what processing happened and what left the boundaryThe receipt
In-house counselEvidence producible later if a regulator or works council asksThe receipt
Internal auditA control that produces evidence, not a policy that asserts behaviourThe receipt
CISO / securityAttack surface, supply chain, what leaves the endpointOne pinned dependency, zero network calls
The analystThe analysis still workingBusiness data preserved byte-for-byte

Three things the receipt is NOT, and each would surface in the first serious meeting:

  • It is not third-party-verifiable. It is generated by the party being audited, and it is an integrity digest rather than a cryptographic signature. An auditor's first question about any control is who produces the evidence and whether they can alter it.
  • It does not help where the processing is unsanctioned. If the activity was approved, they already hold a DPA and the vendor's own logs. If it was not, the receipt is a confession. Its value lives only in the band where the activity is approved but unlogged.
  • That band is closing for exactly these buyers, because Purview logs it natively.

What it genuinely is, and it is still worth a lot: a conversation opener with a privacy function, and evidence inside a services engagement. Those are real jobs. "The thing procurement buys" is not one of them.

The fix that actually answers the objection is punch-list item 6: make the receipt exportable to a location the organization controls. Then it is no longer evidence the vendor holds about himself, it is evidence the organization holds in a place he cannot reach. That is stronger than a signature, costs a modest build, and does not touch the offline guarantee.

So the opening line inside an enterprise is not "this anonymizes your employee data" and not "here is my receipt." It is: every time anyone in this organization sends employee data to an AI tool there is currently no record of it, and there will be no record of it when someone asks — and I know that because I spent ten years inside a company like yours holding that file. The control gap is the hook. The biography makes it credible. The tool is the third thing said, never the first.

The enterprise-readiness audit: the honest verdict

Verdict: NOT DEPLOYABLE inside a large organization today. Not close.

And the reason is not the code. The code is unusually good for its age: 654 tests, one pinned dependency, three review passes including one over the fixes, two reproduced critical findings closed. The gap is almost entirely in the artifacts and evidence an enterprise requires around software, plus one genuine product gap.

BLOCKERS

  • The Trial has not run, and this one dominates everything else. No accuracy number exists. "I have not measured it" ends the conversation permanently rather than temporarily. This is also the highest-leverage item you have: it unblocks the enterprise track, the partner channel, the existing plan's weakest scoring lever, and the right to touch a real client file, all at once.
  • No signed installer. Today it is a Python source install. WDAC blocks unsigned binaries unoverridably. An EV code-signing certificate is roughly $300-700 a year, though signing alone does not grant trust — the organization's own allowlist entry is separate. The cheapest blocker to close, and it gates everything about deployment.
  • No SOC 2 Type II — for a software purchase. NOT required for a services engagement, and that asymmetry is the whole escape hatch. Selling software to a Fortune 100 effectively requires it: roughly $25k-$80k first-year all-in, and 3-5 months minimum because Type II needs an observation window before the audit can conclude. Worse, the controls an auditor observes are exactly what Tessera deliberately lacks by design. Delivering a consulting engagement where you use your own tool on your own machine usually requires none of it.
  • No DPA or data-handling statement. A document, not a build. The parked legal work's trigger has effectively fired. Note a genuine differentiator here: a truly offline tool has no sub-processors to disclose, where reviewers default-assume a long list.
  • Multi-sheet workbooks are refused. Real enterprise HR exports are routinely multi-sheet. A functional blocker hiding as a limitation, and a bounded build.

BEHAVES LIKE A BLOCKER

  • No security questionnaire prepared. This is usually the first artifact requested, before anyone reads a SOC 2, and a PII tool is likely scoped to the long form — roughly 855 questions rather than 126. A pen test report is separately expected, typically $5k-$25k. The good news: your answers are unusually strong — no network, no telemetry, no server, one dependency. Largely a writing exercise, and it should be pre-built rather than improvised under deadline.

NOT APPLICABLE — but with a real question hiding inside

  • SSO / SAML. There is no server and no accounts, so SSO is not missing, it is meaningless. The failure mode is a checkbox marking it non-compliant, so write the answer in advance and write it well. But a good reviewer will find the real question underneath: how is vault access revoked when someone leaves? Today a departing employee's vault dies with their account, which is arguably correct behaviour and arguably a data-loss incident, and nobody has decided which.

THE REAL CONFLICT: offline versus monitoring. Security wants central logs and SIEM integration. Tessera is architecturally incapable of providing any, permanently and deliberately. Framed as a gap, this loses immediately. Framed correctly, it wins: the tool cannot exfiltrate data because it has no network path at all, and what it produces instead of telemetry is a per-run artifact generated at the moment of the action. But that only holds if the receipt can actually be collected — a control producing evidence nobody gathers is not a control. Hence punch-list item 6.

WHAT MUST NOT CHANGE — the deliberate strengths, currently filed as gaps. Every one of these will be described as a weakness by someone in a review, and the instinct will be to fix it. Do not.

  • Zero network calls on every load-bearing path. The strongest security claim you have. Every erosion is irreversible.
  • One pinned dependency. Supply chain is a top-tier enterprise concern and almost no small vendor can answer it. This is an accidental enterprise asset — lead with it, do not bury it.
  • Fail-closed on everything. Reviewers test for partial processing. Refusing and emitting nothing is the correct and rare answer.
  • The un-skippable human approval gate. Reads as friction to a buyer who wants automation. It is what makes the receipt truthful — an automated pipeline produces a log, a human gate produces an attestation, and only one satisfies an auditor.
  • "Anonymized is not unidentifiable," stated in the tool's own output. Feels like a sales liability. In front of a privacy officer it is the single most credibility-building thing in the product, because it is the exact limit they know is true and have heard every vendor deny.
  • No compliance claims, ever. The moment this becomes "GDPR compliant" it is a liability rather than a tool.

THE PUNCH LIST, in order — and the order is the deliverable, not the list.

  1. Run the Trial and publish the numbers. Nothing else starts before this.
  2. Fix "signed receipt" to "integrity-checked receipt" everywhere it appears. Five minutes. The only unblocked item on the list.
  3. Answer the retention-window question, open since September. One decision, and every enterprise intake form asks it.
  4. Code-signing certificate and a signed installer.
  5. Pre-build the security questionnaire answers, including a well-written SSO answer.
  6. Make the receipt exportable to an organization-controlled location, as an explicit separate action, verified to carry nothing identifying. Highest strategic value per hour after the Trial.
  7. DPA and data-handling statement.
  8. Multi-sheet workbook support.

Only with a named, paying enterprise customer — never speculatively: 9. managed packaging, 10. central admin console, 11. multi-user shared vaults.

The line between 8 and 9 is the most important line here. Items 1 to 8 are worth doing regardless — most of them improve the consultant track too, and none is wasted if the enterprise track goes nowhere. Items 9 to 11 are worth doing only for a customer who has said yes. Building them speculatively is how a one-person operation spends a quarter on a product nobody ordered.

One thing to correct about shared vaults specifically, because the existing reasoning does not transfer. The build contract refuses shared vaults because two different clients must not share a fake value. Inside one company there is no client A and client B, and consistency across two analysts would be a feature rather than a leak. So that refusal is not settled for this case — but three different risks replace it: an analyst could de-anonymize data about their own manager and themselves, which needs a real authorization model; the machine-and-user-bound wrap cannot work for a shared vault at all, so the most security-critical component would need redesigning; and the blast radius inverts from one engagement to every employee in the company. Keep the single-user model, and treat the enterprise version as a different product rather than an extension.
