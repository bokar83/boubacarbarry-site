Subject: The most valuable thing AI does in my business is remember

Subtitle / preview: Most owners point AI at the work they can see. The expensive leak is the quote, the reply, and the promise that went cold while you were busy.

The most valuable thing AI does in my business is remember.

That is not where I started. I pointed it at the work you can see, the scheduling and the back office and the outreach. Useful, all of it. None of it was the thing costing me money.

What cost me money was quieter, and I have told you about it before. I had a process that could sort and score incoming work perfectly well, and then nothing happened, because the result landed in a queue I never opened. The machine did its job. I was the broken part. There was no alarm for that, because nothing had failed.

Look at your own week and you will find the same shape. A quote nobody chased. A reply that got read and never answered. And somewhere in there, a promise to circle back that has aged past the point where circling back is comfortable. None of it feels like a crisis. It feels like a normal busy week. And it is the most expensive habit in a small business, because every one of those people had already raised their hand. You already paid to get them there.

In my old life in HR, nothing about a person was allowed to just sit. Every case had an owner and a date, and if the date passed, somebody heard about it. Almost nobody thinks to point that same discipline at their own sales conversations, which is strange, because that is where the money sits.

So that is what I built. Every conversation has a next date attached to it. If I said I would follow up, something is watching for that date whether or not I am. If someone replies, I know that day. If a thread goes quiet past a certain point, it comes back and asks me what I want to do about it. Nothing clever in any of it, no impressive model, just a boring set of rules that runs while I am not looking.

Here is why I would build this before almost anything else. Chasing a new audience is a bet on somebody you have not met. Following up is a near certainty with somebody who already told you they were interested. One of those has a known cost and the other is a guess.

One warning, because I got this part wrong first. A follow-up system only helps if it is honest about what happened. If it marks something done because a message went out, rather than because a human came back, you have built a machine whose only real skill is reassuring you. That is worse than nothing, because now you believe it. Sent is not answered. Make it prove the answer.

One move this week, no software required. Open the last thirty days of your email and find every conversation where you spoke last and the other person never came back. Do not build anything yet. Count them. That number is your leak, and you cannot fix a leak you have not measured.

Then take the most valuable one on that list and write to that person today. The system comes later, and it is much easier to build once you know what it is protecting.

Subject: The job you do by hand every week

Subtitle / preview: Pick one. Put a clock on it. That number is the whole argument, and it is the only one that counts.

Pick the one job you do by hand every week. Not the biggest one. The one that keeps coming back.

Now put a clock on it. Time it start to finish, the next time you sit down to do it. Do not estimate, because your estimate will be wrong in a specific direction. Work you do on autopilot never arrives as a bill. It arrives four minutes here and twenty there, spread so thin across a week that it never looks like the thing to fix.

That number is the whole argument. Not a statistic about AI. Not a case study from a company that looks nothing like yours. Your number, for your job, measured once.

Last week I wrote about the conversations that go cold while you are busy. This is the same problem wearing different clothes. Both are cheap to ignore in any single week and expensive over a year, and neither one ever announces itself.

I found my own version of this by admitting it in front of you a few weeks ago. My problem was never building the thing. It was getting anyone to look at it once it existed. What fixed it was not motivation. It was putting the job on a repeating schedule with a date attached, the same way I would have handled it if it belonged to somebody else.

Here is why I keep pointing people at a weekly job instead of a big one.

It repeats, so whatever you fix pays you back every week rather than once. You already know every step, which means there is nothing to design, and design is where most of these attempts die. And it is small enough to finish in one sitting. That last part matters more than it sounds. A half-built automation is worse than none, because now you have a thing you do not trust and still have to check.

Most owners do the opposite. They go looking for one tool that will handle everything, and everything is too big to describe, so the tool gets configured for a week and abandoned. One repeating job fits in a paragraph. That is the entire difference between the two approaches, and it has nothing to do with which tool you picked.

One warning, because this is the part people get backwards. Do not automate the job you hate. Hate is a feeling and it is a bad selector. The job you hate might happen twice a year. Automate the job that repeats, even if you do not mind doing it, because repetition is arithmetic and arithmetic is the only thing here that compounds.

So: one job, one clock, this week. If the number surprises you, you have found your first one. If it does not, you have spent four minutes and can stop reading me on this subject.

Subject: The AI thing you tried that did not work

Subtitle / preview: It gave you something generic and slightly wrong, so you closed the tab. That was a setup problem, and it is fixable in an afternoon.

You tried it. It gave you something generic and slightly wrong. You closed the tab and got on with your day, and somewhere in the back of your head it filed itself under "not for us".

I want to make the case that you were right to close the tab and wrong about the conclusion.

Here is what almost certainly happened. You asked it to do a piece of real work in your business, and you gave it none of the things you would have given a new hire on their first morning. It did not know what you sell, who buys it, what you charge, who you are not for, or how you talk. So it did the only thing left available to it, which was to write the average version of your industry. Generic and slightly wrong is exactly what that produces. It is not a sign of a weak tool. It is a sign of a tool with no information.

Think about how absurd the equivalent would be with a person. You would not hand a new hire a customer email on day one, tell them nothing about the business, and then conclude from the result that hiring does not work for companies like yours.

Four things fix most of it, and none of them are technical.

Tell it what you sell, in the words your customers use rather than the words on your website. Tell it who your good customer is and, more usefully, who is a bad fit. Your prices go in too, including what you will not discount. Then give it two or three examples of work you were happy with, so it has something to copy that sounds like you rather than like the industry.

Write that down once, keep it somewhere you can paste from, and put it in front of the tool before you ask for anything. That is the whole fix. It takes an afternoon at most and it is the difference between generic and useful.

Now the part most owners have not thought about, and it is the one costing money.

The same gap works in reverse. When somebody asks an AI assistant to recommend a business like yours, it is answering from whatever it can find about you. If your business is not written down anywhere it can reach, it guesses, and it often guesses somebody else. You are not being beaten there. You are not in the room.

Everything above you can do on your own this week, and you should. The one thing you cannot do from your own desk is see what the machines already say about you, because you cannot un-know your own business well enough to read your site the way a stranger's assistant reads it.

So I built a free scan that does it for you. It reads your site the way an AI assistant would and shows you what it can and cannot work out about your business. About a minute, nothing to install, no call: catalystworks.consulting/scan

Look at the gaps it finds. Those gaps are the same four things above. Fix them for the machines and you will have fixed them for the tool on your own desk at the same time, which is the part I did not expect when I started doing this.
