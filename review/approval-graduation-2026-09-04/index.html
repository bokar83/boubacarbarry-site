2. The five graduation candidates, ranked by evidence

Ranked strictly by strength of evidence, not by how much friction each removes. Each one names what would replace the ask.

#1 Single-use data scripts STRONGEST

Evidence: 31 approval cards, 0 rejections. Every one is a script named oneshot_* that writes a few rows and is never executed again. Examples from your own history: the Guinea DM rows, the Energy4Life recruiter call row, the WGU interview row, the Money Map re-rank you asked for yourself.

Why it is the safest first move: these scripts cannot modify the system. They run once, write data, and become dead files. A wrong one is undone by editing a row. There is no version of one of these that files down a guardrail.

Proposed rule: A branch whose only changes under orchestrator/ or scripts/ are new oneshot_* files does not require approval. It merges under the existing Tier C rules and is reported, not asked about. If the same branch touches anything else, the normal hold applies to the whole branch.

#2 Ordinary application-code fixes in those two directories STRONG

Evidence: 128 cards, 0 rejections, covering the full window. This is the big one — two thirds of everything you tap.

The distinction that makes it safe: today "self-modification" means a file's folder. It should mean what the file does. A fix to the morning board is not the system modifying its own guardrails; a change to the gate is. Today they are treated identically, which is why you are tapping approve on Money Map bug fixes.

What this is NOT: it is not loosening the self-modification gate. It is making that gate actually mean self-modification. The list of genuinely guardrail-carrying modules — the gate, approval handling, the spend and budget code, credentials, the sending paths, the deploy scripts, database migrations, the coordination primitives — stays fully held. On the traced record that is 27 of 190 cards.

Proposed rule: Split the self-modification hold into a named guardrail list and everything else. A branch touching a named guardrail module holds for your tap exactly as today. A branch touching only ordinary application code merges under the existing Tier C rules. The list is explicit and additive-only: an agent may add a module to it without asking; removing one is weakening a hard rule and always stops.

CAVEAT This is the candidate that needs the most care, because the boundary is the safety property. Getting the guardrail list right is the whole job. It should run through Karpathy and Council before it is built — not now, at the point somebody actually builds it.

#3 Absorb decisions STRONG

Evidence: 27 approved, 0 rejected, fifteen unbroken weeks, every one a real Telegram tap by you. Plus 3 of 3 on scout auto-mute cards.

The nuance: most absorb verdicts are "note it and move on" or "park it". Those produce a ledger row and change nothing else. The ones that matter install something. Only the second kind is worth your attention.

Proposed rule: An absorb verdict of park or archive-and-note files itself and appears in the digest. A verdict that installs a new skill, agent, or integration still cards you, because installing is a real change to how the system behaves.

#4 Additive hardening — hooks, guards, checks MODERATE

Evidence: 12 hook cards, 0 rejections, plus two entries in this week's ledger and your verbatim instruction yesterday. This is literally the question that started the conversation.

Why it is ranked below the first three: 12 is a thinner record than 128 or 31, and there is a live counter-example sitting in the repo — a destructive-command guard has been written, tested, and deliberately held since 2026-07-25 specifically because registering it changes every future session's behaviour and its coverage is known to be incomplete. That is a real reason to hold, and it argues for splitting this candidate rather than graduating it whole.

Proposed rule: A hook or guard that only warns gets registered without asking. A hook that blocks gets a card, because a bad blocking hook stops every session in the system and the person who has to notice is you.

#5 Technical open questions in a build spec ALREADY GRADUATED

Evidence: your direct correction on 2026-09-03 — "You didn't need me for any of those questions. Next time resolve them yourself and proceed." Already written as a standing rule.

Why it is on this list anyway: because it is rule number 30 in a pile of 30, none of which know about each other. The work here is not a new rule. It is folding the pile into one standing default, so the next incident does not produce rule 31.

Proposed action: Consolidate the 30 existing "don't ask" rules into a single standing autonomy default with one list of exceptions. Not a new permission — the same permissions, in one place, where they can actually be read.

3. What stays gated, and why a clean record is not enough

Some classes below have perfect approval records. They still do not graduate, and the reason is not the evidence. It is that the evidence is the wrong test for them.

The principle. A history of yes tells you the answer has been right so far. It does not tell you what a single wrong answer costs. Graduation is only safe where those two things point the same way — a class of decisions that has been right a hundred times and where being wrong once is recoverable. Where a single wrong call is unrecoverable, a hundred rights buy nothing.

The damage list — not eligible, ever

Sending to a real person, publishing in your name, money, destroying or migrating data, credentials and permissions, shared infrastructure that can take services down, weakening a hard rule, legal exposure. These are not gated because we are unsure how you would answer. They are gated because a wrong instance cannot be taken back. An email that is sent is sent. A dropped table is dropped. A leaked credential is leaked.

And the evidence supports this rather than undercutting it: the one rejection in the entire history was a credentials and shared-infrastructure item. On the one occasion the record shows you saying no, you said it about something on this list. That is not an argument for shortening it.

The self-modification gate — not eligible under this proposal

Changes to the gate itself, to approval handling, to the permission files, to secrets, to the constitutional documents. These stay behind your Telegram tap, and the reason is different from the reason above.

This class is self-referential. Every other gate is a system checking a change. This gate is the system checking a change to its own checking. If an agent can graduate this class, it can graduate its way out of every other class, and the whole structure below it becomes decorative. That is not hypothetical: on 2026-07-12 the policy that the gate does not merge changes to its own logic existed only as a note in a commit message, and the gate merged such a change anyway. Nobody was malicious. The rule simply had no teeth, so it did not hold.

You said it yourself yesterday, in the same breath as asking for this: "We still put gates in there. We still put checks so that there are no rogue agents." This is that gate. It is the one that makes the others mean anything.

The distinction I want you to hold on to, because it is the whole design. There is a real difference between "should we register a hardening hook" and "should we change the rule about who may approve things". Both are infrastructure. Both have a clean record. But the first is reversible in one commit and the second decides whether reversibility exists at all. The first graduates. The second never does.

The honest tension in your own request

You said "even the gate approval cards are things that we can loosen". Here is the part I do not want to soft-pedal: every one of the 217 cards in the ledger IS the self-modification gate. The merge gate you loosened in July already works — ordinary merges do not card you any more. What is left is exclusively this one narrow gate. So on a strict reading, the answer to "loosen the cards" is "we cannot, that gate is the one that must not move."

I think that reading is technically correct and practically wrong, and here is why. That gate is not firing on 217 acts of self-modification. It is firing on 31 throwaway data scripts, 27 Money Map fixes, 35 markdown-only merges, and 27 things that actually touch a guardrail. The problem is not the gate's strictness. It is the gate's aim. Nothing in candidates #1 through #3 weakens what the gate does to a real self-modification — they stop it from firing on things that were never self-modification in the first place.

That said, narrowing what counts as self-modification is a change to a hard rule, which puts it on the damage list, which means it is your call and not mine. That is why this page is a proposal and not a change.

4. The repeatable rule, so this is a mechanism and not a list

You did not ask for a list of five things. You asked for the system to learn from its own history. A list gets stale in a month; a rule keeps working. Here is the rule I would put in.

The eligibility test — all four, no exceptions

#TestWhy it is there
1Not on the damage list.Cost of one wrong call is unrecoverable. No amount of history changes that.
2Not self-referential. Does not change the gate, the approval path, the permission files, or a hard rule.A class that can graduate itself makes every other gate decorative.
3Reversible by a revert, with no side effect that survives the revert.If undoing the change does not undo the harm, it was never a low-stakes class.
4Ten or more recorded instances, all approved, none rejected, spanning at least 30 days.Ten in one week is a burst. Ten across a month is a pattern.

What un-graduates a class, automatically

  • One rejection, and the class is back on cards immediately — for its next ten instances, no discussion.
  • One incident traced to a change that merged under the graduation — same treatment, plus a written note on what the class boundary got wrong.
  • You say so. No justification required, no counter-argument from an agent. Un-graduating is always free.

That asymmetry is deliberate. Graduating takes ten clean instances and thirty days. Un-graduating takes one word from you. The system should be much quicker to hand something back than to take it.

Cadence

WhenWhat runsWhat lands on your plate

QuarterlyRe-check the graduated classes are still cleanNothing, unless something has drifted.

One deliberate design choice worth naming: the monthly review recommends, it does not auto-apply. A mechanism that graduates classes on its own is a mechanism that can graduate its way past the damage list, and no amount of careful coding makes that safe. The review does the counting and the arguing. You do the graduating. That keeps the volume of things reaching you falling while the thing you are deciding stays worth your time.
