Why the last one failed, measured not guessed

I opened the actual renders instead of re-reading the code. Here is what you were reacting to.

The rejected version, flat.

And this is it on a phone. This is the whole story. The banner is the pale strip at the top, and it has no edge at all. It does not end anywhere. Your eye never registers it as a thing, so it cannot advertise anything.

Five defects, in order of how much they mattered:

  1. No edge. Cream on LinkedIn's white card. Nothing to see.
  2. Type 40% too small. 56px on a 396px canvas, which is 13px once a phone shrinks it.
  3. A caption, not a statement. “The constraint slowing your HR team down.” has no verb in it. It does not claim anything.
  4. A book serif at a quiet weight, which is why it read like a pull-quote.
  5. Half the canvas was decoration sitting exactly where your profile photo covers it on a phone. Wasted twice.

What the research actually says

You asked me to go look. Five findings that changed the build:

  • Contrast against LinkedIn's white background is named, explicit guidance. That is defect 1 word for word. (viralbrain.ai)
  • Six words or fewer, read in three seconds. That is the billboard standard, and a profile banner is a billboard. LinkedIn-specific guidance says under 8 to 10. (trailheadmedia, lifa.st)
  • A single flat colour field can carry a whole banner on saturation alone. Zapier's Lane Scott Jones runs solid brand orange, no gradient, no photo, and it works. (zapier)
  • 62% of LinkedIn profile views happen on mobile. This is the number that decided my recommendation. (venngage)
  • Four of the five strongest named consultant banners use no portrait at all. They lean on type, colour and proof instead. That is a real argument against concept B, and I am giving it to you rather than burying it. (magier)

One honest gap: nobody publishes a hard rule for how big the type should be as a fraction of the canvas. I set it from the phone maths instead, which is checkable: a phone renders your banner at about 375 by 94, so anything under roughly 85px on the design canvas is unreadable there. The old one was 56px.

A. The slab
Every HR team has a bottleneck
I find yours.
Message me · boubacarbarry.com

Near-black, edge to edge. On your white profile card this is the highest-contrast object on the page. 11 words. Voice score 91.

The catch, and it is the reason this is not my pick. On a phone only the big line survives. “I find yours.” on its own says nothing. The line that carries the meaning, the bottleneck line, is the small one, and the small one is unreadable at this size. So on most of your views this banner says almost nothing.

B. The split, with your face in it
Your HR team is stuck on something
I know where to look.
Message me · boubacarbarry.com

Two hard panels on a 7px amber seam. That visible edge is the most recognisable “this is an ad” move in layout. Your headshot is treated as duotone so it is part of the panel instead of a photo pasted on. 13 words. Voice score 93, after a rewrite.

Why the copy changed. The first version said “Name it. Remove it.” and I cut it. Your own About says you find it then fix it. “Remove” claims more than you say about yourself, and if the bigger word is not provably true it is not a bigger word, it is a wrong one.

Two honest arguments against this one. First, your profile photo sits directly underneath with the same face in it, 150px away. Two portraits of you that close can read as a lot of you. Second, the research: four of the five strongest consultant banners I found use no portrait at all.

On a phone the portrait is almost entirely covered by your avatar, which costs nothing (it is replaced by the same face), but it does mean the portrait is a desktop-only feature.

C. The billboard — my recommendation
Your HR bottleneck has a name.
Message me · boubacarbarry.com

One saturated field, no panels, no photo, and type big enough that it stops being text and becomes the image. Six words. Voice score 95, the cleanest of the three.

Why this one wins. It is the only concept whose big line is a complete thought on its own. On a phone, where 62% of your views happen, A says “I find yours” and B says “I know where to look”, and neither means anything without the small line nobody can read. C says “Your HR bottleneck has a name” and that works alone, at any size.

That is it on a phone. Compare it to the first screenshot on this page.

It also makes no claim about you that you would have to defend. It is a statement about the reader's situation, and the promise is in what it implies rather than in a boast.

And on desktop. The banner is now an object with edges.

The link question, straight

You said: either we advertise my link there or something else. My answer is yes, put a link on it, and it should be boubacarbarry.com. Three reasons.

  • It is your name. Nobody has to memorise a new string. It is already spelled out directly under the banner. catalystworks.consulting is 24 characters and an unusual ending, which is a lot to retype off an image.
  • It matches the audience. Your LinkedIn is deliberately pointed at corporate and HR buyers. That site is titled for AI strategy consulting; the Catalyst Works site is your founder and small-business track, which is a different audience by design.
  • It does not go stale. A workshop link dies the day the workshop does. Your own domain does not.

The caveat I am not going to hide. A LinkedIn banner is not clickable, and on a phone the URL renders at about 6px. It is unreadable there. So the link is a desktop-only element, and on most of your views it does nothing.

That is an argument for keeping it (it costs nothing and helps the desktop reader who wants to look before reaching out), not against. But if you would rather have those pixels back, say so and I will pull it and let “Message me” stand alone. Practitioner guidance does say a booking link converts better than a general site link, so a third option is pointing it at your calendar instead.
