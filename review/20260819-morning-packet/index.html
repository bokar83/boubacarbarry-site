I caught myself writing a correction into a comment on somebody else's post.

A polite one. Helpful-sounding, even. The thing they missed, the nuance they got slightly wrong, all delivered in a reasonable tone.

I read it back before posting and heard what it actually was. Me, scoring a point, with a helpful voice on top of it.

So I gave myself a rule. Add the thing they left out, and only when it makes their point bigger. Everything else stays in my drafts.

The difference shows up in one word. A comment that opens with "actually" is a comment about me. A comment that opens with "the part people miss here" is a comment about them.

I spent a day working that rule through other people's posts instead of writing my own. Ten of them. It cost me about what one post would have cost. My post would have reached my audience. The comments reached theirs.

The person whose work I am reading should see an ally, not somebody who showed up in their thread to win an exchange.

I used to think flexibility was generous. Here is everything I could do for you, pick whichever one fits.

It reads as desperate. I did not see that until I heard myself doing it.

A menu tells the other person you do not know which of those things they need. Which means you have not diagnosed anything. Which means the list is really a request for them to do the diagnosis for you. On a first call, with somebody who has never thought about their own problem in those terms, that is work you just handed them.

So I changed the shape of the first conversation. I bring no offer to it. I bring one question about the thing I suspect hurts, and then I stay quiet for longer than is comfortable.

Something like: review season is coming, how is your team handling it, are people already underwater.

If nothing hurts, that is a real answer, the call is short, and nobody wasted anything.

If something does hurt, they describe it in their own words. Their words are better than my list. Then I offer one thing. The one thing.

Arriving with one says I already know what you need. Arriving with five says I am hoping one of them lands.

The hardest part is the silence after the question. I am still working on that one.

People keep applying to more jobs for a reason that has almost nothing to do with whether it works. You can count it.

Twelve applications on a Tuesday is a number. It goes up. In a week where nothing else is moving, a number that goes up is worth a great deal to a person.

I stopped playing that game, and the first thing I noticed was the silence where the counting used to be. Nothing to tally, no confirmations landing, no way to say I did eleven of something today.

What replaced it does not produce a number for weeks. Be specific enough, in public, often enough, that when somebody sits down with a problem, a person comes to mind before a posting does.

That is the whole trade. You give up the scoreboard to get the outcome, and there is a long stretch in the middle where you have neither one and have to hold your nerve anyway.

The counting is the trap. It feels like progress because it is measurable. It is measurable because it is easy. And the things that are easy to count are usually the things everybody else is doing too.

I would rather three people know exactly what I do than a thousand skim my resume and forget it.

Which game are you playing right now, and be honest with yourself about why.

Earlier in my career I got handed a hiring process that everybody hated and nobody owned.

Not broken in a dramatic way. Broken in the ordinary way. Too many steps, most of them added by somebody solving a real problem years earlier, none of them ever removed. Every hiring manager had a private workaround. The official process and the actual process had stopped being the same thing long before I arrived.

We ran it as a continuous improvement effort. Got the managers who actually did the work into a room. Mapped what really happened instead of what the document said. Then cut.

Here is the lesson I still use, and it is the unglamorous one. Almost nothing needed to be invented. The gain came from removal.

I think about that constantly now, because AI arrives into companies as an accumulation story. Add a tool. Add an assistant. Add a layer. The part nobody says out loud is that most processes are slow because of steps that should not exist, and putting AI on top of those steps only makes the wrong thing happen faster.
