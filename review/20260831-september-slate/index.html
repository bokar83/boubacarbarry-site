                                                      You Cannot Fire Workers To Replace Them With AI #funfact

                                                      Beehiiv 
                                                      Subject: You cannot fire your way to an AI win

The assumption I keep seeing is that AI lets you cut people and pocket the difference. That math rarely survives contact with reality.

Here is what actually happens. A team hands a function to a model. The model handles most cases cleanly. The rest need judgment, context, and the memory of the person who just got let go. Now your operations lead is doing the work the model could not, with none of the original context, and customers are feeling the gap.

I run my own business with AI doing a lot of what I used to do by hand, so I know how it actually goes. Nobody got cut to make my setup exist. The work AI does for me is work I could not do at all before. Outreach at the volume of a small team. Back-office tasks that used to eat my evenings. Checks that catch mistakes before they cost me a weekend.

The expansion story is more honest than the replacement story. AI lets one operator hold ground that used to need a team. It does not let a team shrink by the same headcount and still produce the same output. The arithmetic is not symmetrical, and that is the whole point.

Why does the replacement story keep getting told? It sells consulting, and it gives an executive a clean number for a slide. Fire a group, save the salary, deploy the model, call it transformation. What the slide leaves out is the long tail the model cannot handle, the team quietly absorbing it, and the customer experience slipping while the savings and the costs sit on two different ledgers.

AI is not a cheaper version of headcount. It is a different kind of capacity. The operators who treat it that way keep their people and grow their reach at the same time. The ones who treat it as headcount math end up paying for the model, paying the consultant, and paying their team overtime to cover what the model dropped.

The framing is the strategy. Everything after it is consequence.

                                                                      Three rounds of the wrong fix

                                                                      LinkedInShort post 
                                                                      I spent three rounds fixing the wrong thing.

Something on my site looked broken on a phone. Squeezed, cut off, the way a page looks when it was obviously built by a guy holding a laptop. Three separate times I went in, found the piece that looked wrong, changed it, and looked again. Three separate times it was still broken.

The fix was always going to be one line. The three rounds were the price of looking in the wrong place.

                                                                                  Building an agentic AI strategy that pays off - without risking business failure

                                                                                  Beehiiv 
                                                                                  Most agentic AI strategies fail in the same place. The pilot ships. The demo wows the room. Six weeks later, nobody on the team can tell you what the agents are actually doing in production. The roadmap quietly drops the word agentic.

I have run that exact loop on my own systems and rebuilt it three times. Here is what survives the second month.

## Start with one outcome, not a tool

On agentsHQ the first outcome I held the system to was simple: every link I drop in a chat becomes a triaged candidate I can decide on from my phone in under ten seconds. Everything else (the classifier, the dispatcher, the scout adapters, the dashboard, the Telegram button with the approve and reject inline keyboard) existed to serve that one job. When a piece did not, I cut it.

Outcome-first is the only filter that survives the second month. Tool-first projects collapse when the model gets cheaper, faster, or different. Outcome-first projects swap tools and keep going.

## Build the kill-switch before you build the agent

The first thing I write for any new autonomous loop is the halt path. A flag file in version control. Commit it, push it, and every cron sees the flag on the next tick and skips with a Telegram alert. Reversible in one command. No console access required. No on-call page in the middle of a Sunday dinner.

The kill-switch is not a fallback. It is the permission slip that lets me trust the rest of the system to run while I sleep.

## Put a verdict-router in front of every write

Agents that can edit anything will eventually edit the wrong thing. The router I shipped this month classifies every file change into a tier before it lands.

- Tier A (memory, audits, decisions, playbooks): append-only. Block on the wrong shape of edit.
- Tier B (code, HTML, voiced content): advisory. Log and notice.
- Tier C (config, settings, SOP files): notice only, with a strict-mode flag for the paranoid case.

The boundary lives in the pre-commit hook, not in a human review. Reviews are too slow and too sparse to be the boundary. The hook fires on every commit, no exceptions.

## Log every outbound action with an approver column

I shipped a publish_events Postgres table that captures every send from every agent. Timestamp, channel, audience, payload, who approved it, the gate that let it through. Without that log, governance is a story you tell after an incident. With it, governance is a query you can run before one.

When something goes wrong (and it will), the first move is not a panicked code-read. It is a select statement.

## Build the reconstruction path before you build the speed

Speed without reproducibility is debt with extra steps. Before I let agentsHQ run unattended overnight, I wrote a branch-guard daemon that polls the VPS every five minutes and Telegrams me if the production HEAD drifts off main. It has caught one real drift event so far. The cost of that daemon was an afternoon. The cost of not having it would have been a Monday morning explaining a phantom deploy.

## What this adds up to

Agentic strategy is not a model selection problem. It is a boundary engineering problem. The agents are the smallest piece. The kill-switch, the verdict-router, the publish-events log, the smoke-test gate, the branch-guard daemon, the reply-pause gate inside the sequence engine that catches the lead who already replied: those are the system.

Build them in that order. Ship the smallest version that works. Then let the agent run.

If your agentic pilot does not have a kill-switch you can fire from your phone, you do not have a pilot. You have a prayer.

                                                                                                                Leading agents vs managing: the operator's case (LI long-form)

                                                                                                                LinkedIn 
                                                                                                                I stopped managing my AI agents. I started leading them. The difference is the whole game.

Managing an agent means you stay in the loop on every action. You approve each step. You catch each mistake. It works until you are running more than one. Then you are the bottleneck, and the system moves only as fast as you can read.

Leading an agent means you set the rules once, build the rails, and let the system carry them forward without you in the middle of every decision.

Three things I built that taught me the difference.

First, a scout that mutes itself. I run scouts that scan sources for signal worth keeping. One was returning nothing useful, hundreds of times. A manager checks the logs, notices the waste, shuts it off by hand. Instead I taught the system one rule: if a scout produces 50 results and zero are worth keeping, mute it and tell me. I taught that rule once. It enforces it now without me. That is leading.

Second, memory that compounds. Early on, every session started cold. The agents relearned the same context every time. Exhausting to watch, expensive to run. So I built a memory layer that persists across sessions and ports between working copies. A lesson learned on Tuesday is still there on Friday, on every branch. A managed system forgets. A led system remembers.

Third, reversibility before autonomy. This is the one most people get backwards. They think autonomy is dangerous, so they hold the reins tight and approve everything. I did the opposite. I built a single kill-switch that halts every autonomous job in one move, plus a guard that watches for drift and alerts me the moment something wanders. Once I could undo anything instantly, I could let the agents do far more on their own. Control is not the opposite of freedom. It is the precondition for it.

Here is the pattern under all three. A manager spends attention. A leader spends it once, on the rule, and gets it back every time the rule fires after that.

This is true of people too. The best operators I know do not hover. They build the conditions where good decisions happen without them in the room. AI just made the lesson impossible to ignore, because the cost of managing instead of leading shows up in your bill and your calendar the same week.

I am still learning where the line sits. Some decisions I will never hand off. But every month I find another one I used to make by hand that the system now makes better, because I taught it the rule instead of repeating the work.

If you are building in the same direction, leading agents instead of babysitting them, I would like to compare notes. This is early for everyone, and the people figuring it out are not the ones talking loudest about it.
