The real risk in this conversation, and how to handle it

The risk is not your experience. It is the title jump. Your record reads HRBP, Senior HRM, HRBP. This role reads Vice President. A recruiter screening for a VP seat is asking himself one question: can I defend this candidate to the hiring leader as an executive?

Do not let him ask it silently. Answer it before he has to. Somewhere in the first ten minutes, ideally right after your opening, put it on the table yourself:

"I want to name the obvious thing early. My titles have been business partner titles. What sat under those titles was executive-band work. Co-leading the restructuring of a global engineering organization. Redesigning the org structure and building the multi-year workforce plan for it. Partnering with senior executive band leaders on talent strategy and succession. I have been doing the work, and I would rather you hear that from me than wonder about it."

Why this works: it converts a doubt into a demonstration of judgment. Executives name the hard thing first. Candidates hope nobody notices.

What not to do: do not apologize for it, do not inflate a title, and do not say you ran HR for a Fortune 100. You did not, and that specific claim is a hard no.

The smaller risk: the gap

You have been running your own business since 2025. That is an answer, not a gap. Say it as a choice you made, in one sentence, and move on. Lingering on it makes it look like something it is not.

Questions to ask Ryan

Ask three. Lead with the first, because it gets you the job description you could not find and it hands him the useful part of the work.

  1. "Walk me through the role as you understand it. What does this VP actually own?" Your highest-value question. You genuinely do not have the posting. He will fill in scope, region and reporting line in one answer.
  2. "Is this a new role or a backfill, and what is driving the hire right now?" Post-demerger, with a new Americas president and a new CFO, the answer tells you whether you are being hired to build something or to steady something. Very different jobs.
  3. "Who does this role report to, and what does the HR team under it look like?" Team size, structure, and whether the Chief People Officer or the Americas president owns it.
  4. "How much of this job is at the sites versus at headquarters?" A great question for you specifically. The answer either plays straight to your strength or tells you the role is more corporate than it looked.
  5. "What does success look like in the first year, from the hiring leader's point of view?"
  6. "What is the process from here, and what is your timeline?" Always ask this, always last. And ask what range the role sits in if he has not already said.

Do not ask about remote work, vacation or benefits on a first recruiter screen. Do not ask anything the website already answers.
