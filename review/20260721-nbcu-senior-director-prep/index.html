Why each beat is there ›
  • "yes, I would like to proceed." Answers her actual question in the first line. She asked you to confirm fit. Confirm it, then earn the rest.
  • The work-I-enjoy-and-am-good-at line. Names the exact pillars of the description back to her, transformation, org design, workforce planning, real technology in HR, so she sees you read it closely and it maps to what you actually do.
  • The governance sentence. The proof you read the description instead of skimming it. It names the specific thing that separates this role from every other HR transformation posting, and it carries a mild opinion, which reads as a peer talking, not a candidate agreeing.
  • "confident I can bring strong experience... while learning plenty." Peer posture with humility. You dropped "I have led that kind of work before" on purpose, so it stays confident without tipping into a resume claim.
  • The built-system paragraph, on its own. Standing apart from the rest, it reads as proof, not an aside. It is the one thing on the page that is not on your profile.
  • The close. Acknowledges the availability you already sent without resending it, and puts the next move in her hands without begging for it.

Questions you ask her

Renata is talent acquisition, not the hiring manager. Ask her what she actually knows, and ask it the way someone already doing the job would.

"Is this a new role or a backfill? And if it is new, what happened that made them create it?"
The best question in the set. The answer tells you whether you would be writing the roadmap or inheriting somebody else's failed one.
"Who does it report to, and who controls the transformation budget?"
If those are two different people, that is the real job. Ask it early enough that the answer shapes everything after.
"What is already in flight? Is there an HR technology migration running right now that this role would land in the middle of?"
Signals you know these roles are usually created mid-migration, and gives you the platform answer before you have to volunteer one.
"The description names Technology, Finance and Legal as partners. Are those partnerships already established, or would building them be part of the first year?"
Nobody asks this. It is the difference between a role with a mandate and a role with a title.
"What does success look like at twelve months, in their words, not the job description's?"
If she cannot answer, that is information. If she can, it is the framework for everything you say in the next conversation.
"How much of this is roadmap and governance, and how much is hands-on delivery?"
You are strong on both, which is unusual. Asking makes you look like you are calibrating, not hoping.
"Remote eligible: what does that actually look like for this team? How often would they want me in New York?"
Practical, and you need the real answer, not the posting's answer.
"Where are you in the process, and what is the timeline you are working to?"
Close with it. It reads as a peer managing a calendar, not a candidate hoping for news.

The compensation conversation

The rule: they posted a band, so you never name the first number ›

The range is public. $335,000 to $445,000 base, up to 30 percent bonus, up to $35,000 sign-on, plus RSUs. That is a $110,000 spread on base alone. Where you land in it is decided by scope and by whether they think you are the obvious hire, and both of those get settled long before anyone talks money.

If she asks for your number on this first call

Say this"The posted range works for me. Where I fit in it depends on scope, and I would rather understand the scope first. If it turns out this is the right seat, the number will not be the hard part."

If she pushes for a figure anyway

Say this"For a role at this scope, I am oriented to the upper half of the posted range. If the scope is bigger than the description suggests, we should talk about the top of it."

Anchor upper half, never a specific number, never below the midpoint. Recruiters record whatever number you say and it becomes your ceiling.

If she asks what you make now

Say this"I run my own operation, so the comparison would not be useful to either of us. What matters is what this role is worth, and you have already published that."

True, clean, and it closes the topic without sounding defensive.

Non-negotiable

Never say you would take less. Never signal flexibility downward on the first call. Never volunteer that a number sounds high. The band is theirs, they published it, and you are inside it.

What NOT to say

✕

"I ran HR and operations." You did not. You were in HR, you helped run things within HR, and you had operations-adjacent exposure. Never upgrade it. It will not survive a reference check and it is the single fastest way to lose a process like this.

✕

"HR executive" or anything that implies a CHRO seat. HRBP is the truth and it is a strong truth. Do not inflate it.

✕

"Fifteen years at a Fortune 100." Ten at GE. Fifteen in HR total. Two different numbers, two different scopes. Merging them is a false claim and you have caught this one before.

✕

Anything that frames this as a career change or a return to HR. You never left HR. You are an HR professional who also builds the systems everyone else is still evaluating. That is one continuous line, not a pivot, and never a "getting back into" story.

✕

Any hint that you would be abandoning or winding down a business. Do not raise your consulting work unprompted. If she asks, keep it short: you run your own operation, this role is where you want the next decade, and the two are not in tension. Do not narrate a transition plan she did not ask for.

✕

Any internal codename for your system. Ever. Describe it by what it does.

✕

SuccessFactors you do not have. Your Workday and Oracle experience is real, so state it with full confidence. But do not stretch it into SuccessFactors or overstate the Oracle depth. Platform claims are the one thing a large HR org verifies in a single follow-up question.

✕

Opening with the credential. Not "I spent ten years in HR at a Fortune 100." Lead with the substance of what you are saying and let the credibility show through it. It is on your profile already.

✕

Apologizing for a gap. No "I don't have X, but..." Name it in one sentence, offer what you have instead, move on. Over-explaining a gap makes it three times bigger than it is.

✕

Selling too hard. She came to you. If you find yourself doing most of the talking on this first call, you have lost the posture. Ask, listen, answer with one strong story, and let her sell.
