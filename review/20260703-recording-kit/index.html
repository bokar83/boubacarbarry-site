Read once before you press record

Truth guardrails. If a take breaks one of these, re-take it.

  • No track record you do not have yet. July 30 is the first real room. True: "I run my own business on this." Not yet true: "I've taught rooms of owners." Fine and true: "helping people adopt new tools was part of my HR work."
  • No income claims, no invented numbers. Only real numbers allowed: the $47 / $97 seat price, July 30, and anything literally on screen.
  • Never imply AI writes your posts. If a writing workflow shows up, the true line is: AI does the first pass on repetitive business writing, you edit and decide. Never show or say AI drafts your public content.
  • HR is the seat, not "trainer." About ten years in HR at a Fortune 100, where helping people adopt new tools was part of the work. Never "training team," never "corporate HR."
  • Zero real client or prospect data on screen or on a call. Sample or volunteer business only. Lists shown as counts or tiles, never row-level.
  • No internal codenames spoken or shown. Say "my system," "my agents," "the agent that finds the businesses I should be talking to." If a surface shows a codename, crop it or skip it.
  • No hype patterns ("game-changer," "on autopilot," "six figures") except when quoting the ads you position against, framed as theirs.
  • Honest state only. If an agent is paused, say so. "A couple are paused right now because I didn't need them this month" is the credibility, not a weakness.

Setup and gear (one time, before the first take)

Night before
  • Freeze the demo machine: no pending updates, restart it, open the exact tabs, leave it.
  • Load your business brain file, and type one real decision into a fresh chat (feeds Demos 1 and 3).
  • Pick the one real thing Demo 5 will show (the Monday visibility scan output, or the content queue). Confirm it has a genuine, recent result to point at.
  • Charge everything. Use the Anker charger for the laptop, the stock one is flaky.
  • Open this kit on a second device (phone or tablet) so the script is never on your recording screen.
Morning, before take one
  • Laptop: Do Not Disturb / Focus on. Every messaging and mail app closed, not minimized. Calendar popups off.
  • Browser: fresh or clean window, bookmarks bar hidden, no extra tabs. Test-type one letter in the URL bar so autofill can't surface a client name.
  • Desktop clean. No files with client names visible. No keys or env files near an open window.
  • Scan every visible tile for codenames, client names, or real emails. Crop, rename, or skip any surface that shows one.
  • Camera at eye level. One window with decent light in front of you, not behind.
  • Screen recording on. Each demo's "on screen" item already open in a tab.
  • Shoot one 20-second test clip. Review framing and audio before the real takes.
Recording discipline
  • Shoot in segments, never one continuous take. Each demo and each teaching segment is its own file.
  • Say the segment name out loud at the top of each take (a cheap slate that saves the edit).
  • One clean take per demo. Fumble? Restart that one. Do not fix it in the edit.
  • Keep each demo under the length shown. Shorter and real beats longer and polished.
  • Parachute rule: if something live misbehaves twice, keep the best take and move on. The edit hides a re-take, the calendar does not hide a lost morning.
  • Save all six raw before you touch any of them.

Part B · The teaching session

T

How I run my business solo with an AI system I built

20-35 min
Email-gated replayLead magnet (mid-funnel)Backbone for a later live webinar

What it is: one recorded walkthrough, filmed solo, published free behind an email opt-in. Not a live webinar (that needs a registration page and a runway you do not have by Sunday). Record it now, run it live later off the same footage.

The angle: how one person runs a company's worth of work with an AI system. Not a lecture on prompts. You take one real thing you built, the AI phone assistant, and walk the whole move: the idea, directing a set of AI agents to research and build it, putting it live, testing it, and fixing what broke on the first try.

Who it is for: business owners who did not grow up with this technology, national, not local. The Gen X operator and the older owner who runs a real business, is not anti-AI, and is tired of being taught by a 25-year-old who has never made payroll. Second lane, same footage: HR people who want to up-skill in AI (HR is your seat).

Why you and no one else: you lived the full analog to digital to AI run, and you built a real working system you run your business on. Most people teaching this either grew up with the tech or have never shipped with it. You did both. That is the bridge nobody else stands on.

Segment flow (record each as its own file, say the segment name at the top)

0 · Cold open, the hook ~45s

On screen: you at the desk, or the phone assistant's number ready to dial. No intro music, no "hey guys."
Hook line: "I did not grow up with this technology. I still spent one afternoon directing a set of AI agents to research, build, and ship a working AI phone assistant, and I want to show you every move."

1 · Who I am, why me 2-3 min

  • The analog to digital to AI run, in your own words. You made the hard transition and you built the real thing.
  • About ten years in HR at a Fortune 100, where helping people adopt new tools was part of the work. So you have watched tech get sold as magic and land as homework.
  • Set the promise: no theory, no hype about robots taking over. One real build, start to finish, seams showing.

2 · The idea 1-2 min

  • What you decided to build and why: a phone line for your business that answers, holds a real conversation, and does not need you awake.
  • Keep it plain. The point is that it started as a normal owner problem, not a tech project.

3 · Briefing the system 4-6 min

  • How you brief the system: you describe what you want, and the work gets split across agents that research and plan before anything is built.
  • Show the shape of the brief, not the internals. Outcomes and direction, yes. The prompt text and orchestration, no.
  • The teaching point: you direct, the system does the legwork. You are the operator, not the typist.

4 · The build 4-6 min

  • Walk the build coming together: research to a plan, plan to a working thing. Narrate what each step is doing in plain terms.
  • Show progress and decisions, withhold the recipe. A viewer should feel how it is directed, not be able to rebuild it from the screen.

5 · Going live, and the first-try break 4-6 min this is the credibility beat

  • Put it live. Then be honest: the first try hit a snag (the carrier issue), you diagnosed it and cleared it.
  • This is the most valuable segment. Real systems break on the first run. Showing the diagnosis is what separates you from a course ad.
  • The line to land: the judgment is the business. The tool did the legwork, you did the fix.

6 · The live proof 2-3 min

On screen: call the line on speaker, one take, no edits inside the call. Let the greeting play in full. Ask one clean question. If you show the language switch, use a made-up name and number and say on camera they are made up.
Land it: "That line is answering whether I'm awake or not. That is one job off my plate, every day."

7 · The move, generalized 2-3 min

  • Turn it to the viewer: every business has one bottleneck costing the most, and the rest is noise. The job is to find that one and build the fix, with AI when it fits and without it when it does not.
  • The protection line: "I went from ChatGPT to Gemini to Claude and never lost a step, because I kept the principles, not the product."

8 · Close and soft CTA 1-2 min

  • What the email unlocks: this full session plus the real starter files you use, so the magnet overdelivers.
  • Two exit ramps, named lightly: the HR direction for the HR crowd, the fractional-operator work for the owners.
  • One soft CTA, said once. No countdown, no pressure. The July 30 room is the natural next step for anyone who wants to build a piece of their own.
