AI coach and in-field research: capture and pressure test

Your idea from 2026-08-03, written down properly, tested against the record and against a real five-voice Council, with the Money Map rows drawn up for your approval.

The Council, five angles

A real run, not a summary of one. Five voices answered your five questions independently, reviewed each other anonymously, and a chairman scored the convergence at 0.91 in one round. Three said kill, two said change. Nobody said keep.

The ContrarianKill

On research as a business

"It is not a business. It is a research project disguised as strategy." Demand is created by offers, not insights. Research without a monetization path is a luxury for a business with runway, and there is none here.

Closing line

"He is choosing curiosity over cash at the exact moment he must choose survival."

First PrinciplesKill

On whether interviews convert

Only to goodwill and a mailing list, unless each conversation ends with a direct invitation to buy something specific. Without that, it is content creation wearing the costume of business development.

On the corporate angle

It contradicts. The owner-operator has a business-level constraint and buys an outcome. The person inside a corporation has a career-level worry and buys development. Different problem, different decision, different wallet. "This is a pivot, not an expansion."

The OutsiderKill

On "AI coach" as a price-commanding position

The market treats it as a free-to-enter label. To charge, one of these must be true: a proprietary method with visible outcomes, a closed cohort with real scarcity, or borrowed institutional authority. Right now, none are.

What the outside sees

"You're treating visibility as revenue, and research as progress, when the only thing that moves the needle now is a direct offer to a clear buyer with a deadline to say yes."

The ExpansionistChange

The upside everyone else missed

"You are trying to gather data to justify the sale. In your current cash environment, the sale must happen to generate the data." Flip the order. The research is not a prerequisite for income, it is the vehicle that qualifies a paid pilot.

The bigger play

Turn the interview into a paid diagnostic that pays for itself and produces the same data.

The ExecutorChange

On the weeks it costs

Four to six weeks minimum, displacing the ghostwriting window, the already-authorised HR lane, and any direct client outreach. "The opportunity cost is the entire quarter's revenue goal."

The smallest surviving version

Keep the conversations. Drop the words "research" and "book". Every conversation ends with an offer to look at their actual work.

Where I disagree with the chairman

The chairman's Monday action is wrong and you should not run it. It says to email your ghostwriting coach and ask him for a warm introduction to the fractional-HR podcast host. Your coach runs a fintech-founder ghostwriting program. Nothing in the record connects him to that HR community, and there is no reason he would know anyone in it.

Asking a coach you have gone dark on for 25 days to make an introduction he cannot make, in the same email where you are explaining the silence, spends the one relationship with a hard deadline on a favour that does not exist. The peer-review round invented that bridge. Send the reply that is already drafted on the program page instead.

What all five missed

The interviews and the contact sheet you owe your coach are the same physical motion. The blown assignment is "build a sheet of ten to fifteen named people and reach out." This idea is "reach out to ten to fifteen named people and ask them something." Different lists, one habit.

Five voices framed this as research competing with outreach for the same weeks. The more useful read is that you have one outreach muscle and it has produced zero sends in seventy-five days. A second list does not fix that. But an ask that is genuinely easy to send might. Asking someone you respect for twenty minutes of their time costs you almost nothing to send. Pitching them costs you a great deal. Your own board carries a five-asks-a-day lock precisely because the asks are the part you hide from. A format that makes the ask easy is not a small thing.

That is why my verdict is change and not kill.

My verdict, in one line

CHANGE. The conversations live, demoted from a research project to a sales motion with a research byproduct, gated behind Tuesday and carrying a priced door and a kill date. The book, the title change and the corporate-individual buyer all die today.

The idea, written down properly

Your words, unedited

"I want to reach out to my friends and peers and colleagues in the HR world but also in other worlds and see how they are currently using AI. I'm gonna start doing some research on it and add that to my book or write a book on it. By doing in-field research I think I wanna get some good data on what people actually need and create tools that they can use. At the end of the day I would love to come into a company and help them to improve how people use the AI within their organization but I really am focusing on people, individuals who need to learn how to use AI better. I'm gonna be their AI coach. Yes this is for SMBs but the other people I'm looking at are people who are in the corporate world who want to use AI but don't know how to use it within the corporate world. I've done it. I know how to do that."

The thesis, stated up front instead of buried

Almost everyone teaching AI right now has never had to make it survive inside an organisation. They teach the tool. They have never had to get a new tool past a policy owner, past legal, past procurement, past the manager who does not want it, past the person who has to keep doing the old way in parallel until the new way is trusted. You spent ten years inside a Fortune 100 doing exactly that work, and you now run your own business on a system you built yourself.

That is the asymmetry. Not that you know AI. That you know the walls it hits once it leaves the demo, and you have been on both sides of those walls. Everything else in this idea is a way of turning that claim into evidence.

The five moves inside it, and how they feed each other

MoveWhat it isWhat it feeds
1. In-field interviews Ask friends, peers and former colleagues in HR, and deliberately people outside HR, how they actually use AI in their work today. Feeds everything. It is the only move that generates new raw material rather than rearranging what you already have.
2. Data, not anecdotes The same questions every time, captured in the same fields, so twenty conversations compare against each other instead of sitting as twenty separate stories. Feeds the tools and the book. Without this discipline, moves three and four have nothing underneath them and become opinion.
3. Tools built from the data Build for the needs the conversations actually surface, rather than for the needs you can currently imagine. Feeds the coaching offer, which becomes something you deliver with rather than something you describe.
4. The book Either a new one about how people really use AI at work, or the material folded into the book already in progress. Feeds authority, which feeds the room, which feeds the interviews. A loop, but a slow one that pays last.
5. AI coach, the position underneath Working with individuals rather than with departments. Two rooms: small-business owners, and people inside larger organisations who want to use AI and cannot work out how to inside their own walls. This is what all four other moves are trying to earn the right to charge for.

The honest gap in the thesis

"I've done it, I know how to do that" is true and it is rare. It is also not yet evidence anyone outside this page can check. The Council was right that it is not a business model on its own. The part they missed is that the interviews are a reasonable way to convert it from a claim into evidence, because after twenty conversations you can say something specific about what actually happens to AI inside organisations that nobody selling prompt courses can say.

That is the strongest argument for doing this at all, and it only holds if the conversations actually happen and get written down. It collapses completely if this becomes a folder of recordings you never open.

One operational constraint on the asymmetry. The rule already on the books is that formal credentials go in one-to-one emails and nowhere else. So the corporate track record can carry a personal ask to a peer, and it cannot headline a public post, a bio or a landing page. If this becomes public copy, the same claim has to be carried by what you have built and what you found, not by where you worked.

What ships from five conversations

Not a tool. Not a chapter. One finding, written up in one page.

  • The one thing at least three of the five said in different words.
  • The one thing none of them said out loud but all of them worked around.
  • The single task every one of them still does by hand.

That page is worth something on its own. It is the post that tests whether anyone cares. It is the reason the next fifteen people say yes, because you can now say "I have asked five people and here is what came back, I would like to add you." Three messages and six questions. The messages have been through the voice gate and are clean on every hard rule: no em-dashes, no disclaiming the sale, opener states the fact with no scene-setting, no sign-off because your signature appends itself, and no credentials in the body.

The follow-up · this is the one that makes it a business 
Thanks for the twenty minutes. Two things you said have already come up in other conversations, which usually means it is not just you.

One more thing and then I will leave it alone. What you described with [the specific thing] is the work I do for people, and I would rather set it up on your real workflow than send you a list of tools you will never open.

An hour, on your actual work, [price], and everything we build is yours to keep.

Want me to send a time?

Either way I'll send you the findings from all twenty when they are done.
Pick the price before the first conversation, not after. The record already carries two precedents: $150 to $250 for a paid session in the June plan, and $500 for a pilot in the Council's version. Your call, but decide it in advance so you never improvise it under pressure.

The six questions, asked in this order, every time

  1. Walk me through the last time you used AI for something real at work. What was the task?
  2. What do you have open right now? Which tool, whose account, who pays for it?
  3. What did you try that did not work, and did you ever go back to it?
  4. Where does it break inside your organisation? Is it policy, approval, the data, or a person saying no?
  5. What are you still doing by hand that you think a machine should be doing by now?
  6. Who else should I be asking, and would you introduce me?

Question four is the one nobody else asks, and it is where your ten years inside a large organisation earns its keep. Question six is the one that makes the whole thing compound. Neither is optional.
