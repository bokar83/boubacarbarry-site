One home for the domains

Should everything sit with one registrar, and which one · 20 August 2026

TL;DR

Recommendation: consolidate every domain you own into one Porkbun account.

The problem being solved here is scatter and control, not calendar awareness. Your domains are spread across at least five different providers, which means there is no single place that answers three basic questions: what do we own, when does each one expire, and what is each one attached to. One registrar gives you that list in one screen, on one card, on one bill. Porkbun also has an open API, so the list can check itself instead of depending on anyone remembering to look.

The second reason matters more over the next year than the money does. As you take on more client work you will start holding domains on behalf of other people. The system for tracking what is owned, what it costs, and what it points at has to exist before that happens, not after. Building the habit now, on your own eighteen domains, is cheap. Building it later, while someone else's site is on the line, is not.

What you are accepting: Porkbun is a small independent company. Cloudflare is a far larger and more durable business. You are trading institutional size for the lowest verified renewal price, an open API with no approval gate, and the freedom to keep pointing your DNS wherever you want.

What we are actually deciding

One question, and it is a structural one: should all of the domains live with a single registrar, and if so, which one.

Everything else on this page exists to answer that. Price matters, but it is the tiebreaker, not the reason. The reason is that a portfolio split across five providers cannot be seen in one place, cannot be checked by one script, and cannot be handed to anyone else without a tour of five different logins.

Why now rather than later

This is client-readiness infrastructure. The moment you are doing work for other companies, domains stop being a personal admin item and become something you hold on someone else's behalf. That comes with a duty to know exactly what is registered, under whose name, expiring when, pointing where, and paid on which card. A habit and a system built now, while the only portfolio at stake is your own, transfers cleanly to client work. A habit built under pressure, after the first client domain is already in your account, does not.

There is a second, quieter benefit. A single registrar with an open API makes the whole portfolio queryable. One list, generated on demand, showing every domain, its expiry, its auto renew state, and its nameservers. That artefact is worth more than the price difference, and it is only possible once everything is in one place.

That is what scatter costs, and no amount of attention fixes it while the portfolio lives in five places.

The options

Option A. Porkbun. Move everything into one Porkbun account. Cheapest verified renewal of the five, free WHOIS privacy, free open API with no approval step, and your DNS stays wherever you point it.

Option B. Cloudflare Registrar. Cheapest in principle, because they sell at cost with no markup. But it forces every domain onto Cloudflare nameservers permanently, and they publish no per-domain dollar price anywhere public, so you commit before you know the number.

Option C. Namecheap. Consolidate onto the provider holding the largest share today. No new vendor to learn. But the renewal prices are high, and the API needs an approval step plus an IP allowlist before it will answer a single question about your own portfolio.

Option D. Hostinger. Consolidate onto your hosting provider, which you already pay and already have wired up. Convenient, and the most expensive per year of the four registrars priced below. It also ties your domains to your host, which is the thing that makes changing hosts painful later.

Option E. GoDaddy. Consolidate onto GoDaddy. Their standard list price is the highest of everyone here, and the first step would be establishing whether you still have an account with domains in it at all. Named as a real option because domains may already be sitting there.

Option F. Stay split. Do nothing, keep five providers. On the list only as the baseline the others have to beat.

The Namecheap gap, in their own numbers

A .com is advertised at $11.28 and renews at $18.48. A .org is advertised at $8.48 and renews at $18.98. That is more than double on the .org. The first year price is the hook. The renewal is the real price, and the renewal is what you pay for the next decade.

Their fee disclaimer, word for word: "ICANN (the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers) charges a mandatory annual fee of $0.20 for each domain registration, renewal or transfer. This will be added to the listed price for some domains, at the time of purchase."

GoDaddy, since domains may already be there

GoDaddy is on this page as a real option, not a straw man, because the 2024 support ticket means domains may still be sitting in that account today.

What I verified today, and how

Their pricing pages refuse automated fetches, so this was read out of a real browser session on their own live domain search results page. On godaddy.com/domainsearch/find, searching an available name today returned a standard list price of $22.99 per year for .com and $23.99 per year for .org. The promotional offers alongside them were $0.01 for the first year on .com, explicitly labelled "1st yr only with 3 yr term", and $9.99 for the first year on .org.

What I could not verify, stated plainly: GoDaddy does not print an explicit "renews at" figure anywhere on that page. The $22.99 and $23.99 are their standard non-promotional list prices, which is what a renewal bills against, but the exact renewal line item is UNKNOWN until it is read inside a logged-in account. It will not be lower than list.

On that basis GoDaddy is the most expensive of the five at standard price, which matches its reputation. That reputation was checked rather than repeated.

The privacy claim, corrected

GoDaddy is widely said to charge for WHOIS privacy. On their own page today that is not what it says. Domain Privacy is listed under "Everything You Need, Free with a GoDaddy Domain", described as limiting the contact information shown in their public WHOIS directory. So privacy is free, and the page says so. Worth recording, because it is the kind of thing everyone repeats and nobody re-reads.

The action item this section produces: log into the GoDaddy account and look. That is the only thing that closes the question of whether four domains transferred out in 2024 or are still sitting there quietly renewing. It is a five minute job and it should happen before any transfer plan is finalised.

The Cloudflare question, stated plainly

Cloudflare sells domains at wholesale with no markup. Their words: "Cloudflare Registrar will never charge you more than what we pay to the registry for your domain." Privacy is free. On price alone it wins.

The catch is not small, and it is not reversible while you stay with them. From their own documentation: "All domains acquired via Cloudflare Registrar use Cloudflare nameservers... You will not be able to change to another DNS provider's nameservers while using Cloudflare Registrar." And from their FAQ, asked whether you can use other nameservers: "No, all domains on Cloudflare Registrar use Cloudflare nameservers."

Their transfer guide is equally direct: "Before you can transfer your registration, your domain must be active on Cloudflare." So you cannot even start the move until DNS is already switched over.

Some of your domains currently point at Hostinger hosting. Choosing Cloudflare means rebuilding every one of those DNS zones by hand first, getting every record right, and living with Cloudflare DNS permanently after. If you ever want different nameservers, the only exit is transferring the domain away to another registrar entirely. That is a real project with real downtime risk, in exchange for a saving of a few dollars per domain that they will not quote you in advance.

What is irreversible

Once a domain transfers, it is locked at the new registrar for 60 days. You cannot undo it, change your mind, or move it again until that window closes. Cloudflare states the requirement as: "Your domain was registered at least 60 days ago and has not been transferred in the last 60 days." Porkbun says the same: "a domain must stay at one registrar for 60 days before it can be transferred to another."

Changing the registrant name, organisation, or email address triggers its own separate 60 day lock. Cloudflare's documentation: "You have not changed your registrant name, organization, or email address in the last 60 days," and notes that "changes to these fields trigger a 60-day transfer lock. Some registrars let you opt out of this lock during the change, but not all do." So do not tidy up contact details right before a move. Do the transfers first, then fix contacts.

The other irreversible thing on this page is not a transfer at all. Four expired Squarespace domains have no recorded decision. Once a lapsed domain leaves redemption and drops, it is gone and anyone can take it. If any of those four are worth keeping, that is a decision with a clock on it, separate from and faster than the consolidation itself.

The good news on transfers

You do not lose paid time. Porkbun: "You won't lose any existing months/years of registration, and we add a year to your registration at the moment of transfer (in most cases)." So a transfer is effectively buying a year at the new registrar's price rather than throwing away the year already paid for.

Why Porkbun and not the other four
  • Beats Namecheap on money by about $7.40 per .com per year and $7.14 per .org, every year, with no loss of function.
  • Beats Cloudflare on freedom. Cloudflare may be a dollar or two cheaper, but it will not tell you how much cheaper before you commit, and it takes your nameservers as the price of entry. You have live sites on Hostinger hosting. Do not hand DNS to a registrar that will not give it back.
  • Beats Hostinger on both. Hostinger is expensive at renewal, and keeping domains at your host is exactly what makes leaving a host painful later. Separating the registrar from the host is good practice on its own terms.
  • Beats GoDaddy on price by roughly half, at $11.08 against a $22.99 standard list price on .com.
  • No promo game. The .com price to register and the price to renew are the same number, so it can be budgeted once and never surprises you.
  • The API is open. No approval gate, no IP allowlist, sandbox keys for safe testing. That is what turns "what do we own and when does it expire" into a query instead of a chore, which is the entire point of consolidating.

If you say yes, the order of operations
  1. Log into GoDaddy and settle whether any domains are still there. Nothing else is final until that is known.
  2. Decide on the four expired Squarespace domains with no recorded decision. Keep or let go, but decide, because that clock is already running.
  3. Open one Porkbun account. Turn on two factor with a hardware key or an authenticator app before anything else.
  4. Put a card on file and confirm it is live.
  5. Do not touch registrant contact details on any domain yet. Changing them starts a separate 60 day lock.
  6. Move two domains first, ideally ones with no live site, and watch them land. Confirm the DNS records survived.
  7. Once those two are clean, move the rest in batches. Copy every DNS record before starting each one.
  8. Turn on auto renew on every domain as it arrives.
  9. Wire a weekly automated check against the API that lists every domain, its expiry, its auto renew state and its nameservers, and makes noise when anything is inside 45 days or auto renew is off. This is the artefact that carries over to client domains.
  10. After everything has settled and the 60 day locks have passed, clean up contact details.
