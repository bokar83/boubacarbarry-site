Method: How This Is Structured (Not a List)

Most competitive reports are a list of names with one-line descriptions. This is not that. It borrows three disciplines from graph-based intelligence work:

  1. A fixed schema. Every entity gets a type before it gets a name. Types are locked before research starts, so the map stays consistent instead of turning into free-text notes.
  2. Nodes and edges, not paragraphs. Every entity is a node. Every relationship between two entities (acquired, competes with, covers, founded) is an edge, tagged with a type and a date where known. This is what makes the map re-runnable: next quarter, only the edges that changed need touching.
  3. A dedup pass. The same organization shows up under multiple names in public sources (a company renamed, an analyst's old firm vs. new firm). Those get merged into one node with an aliases list, so the map doesn't double-count the same player.

Below is the schema actually used for this map.

SCHEMA (node types)
- vendor            : a company selling an AI/HR product
- consulting_firm    : a firm selling AI/HR advisory or implementation services
- analyst_firm       : a research/analyst organization covering this market
- person             : a named individual (analyst, founder, thought leader)
- event              : a recurring industry event/conference

SCHEMA (edge types)
- acquired_by        : vendor -> vendor/acquirer, with a date
- founded            : person -> firm
- covers             : analyst_firm/person -> vendor or market segment
- competes_with       : vendor -> vendor (same buyer, same job-to-be-done)
- speaks_at          : person -> event
- sold_to            : firm -> firm (business sale, distinct from acquisition of a product)

The Map: How the Buyer Actually Sees It

This is the part a client pays for: not the list above, but the shape of the market once you group it by what an HR leader is actually trying to decide.

Decision 1: "Do I buy a suite or point solutions?" Workday, SAP, and Oracle are absorbing point solutions into their suites (Workday bought HiredScore, Evisort, Paradox, and Sana; ServiceNow bought Moveworks). That is the single biggest structural move in this market right now. The AI feature you'd have bought standalone eighteen months ago is now a line item inside a suite you already pay for. A mid-market buyer needs to know this before signing a new point-solution contract.

Decision 2: "Which point solutions still make sense standalone?" Talent acquisition (Phenom, Greenhouse, Beamery) and people analytics (Visier, One Model, Eightfold) are the two categories where independents are still winning distinct deals, because the suite vendors' offerings in these categories are newer and less mature.

Decision 3: "Who do I trust to tell me the truth about all this?" Three analyst voices actually cover this space in depth. Josh Bersin (broadest, most cited), George LaRocque and WorkTech (vendor and investor-facing, curates the HR Tech Conference investor report), and Madeline Laurano and Aptitude Research (narrower, TA and employee-experience focused, publishes usage-rate data like the finding that 74% of companies are now using AI in HR). A buyer who only reads vendor marketing never sees this layer.

Decision 4: "Do I need a platform, or do I need a translator?" This is the gap boutique consultants (MidMarket AI, ThirdEye Data, Algoscale) exist to fill. None of them sell a platform. They sell the judgment layer between a confusing vendor landscape and a company that just wants AI to work. This is also, functionally, the exact positioning Catalyst Works already occupies, just not yet mapped against this specific vendor landscape.
