The week my own system went quiet and I didn't notice

I run my business on a system I built myself. It handles the plumbing, the scheduling, the moving parts, while I go sell. For about two weeks, part of it went silent. And here is the part that stung: I did not notice until I looked. The machine was doing exactly what I told it to. A quality gate I put in place to stop bad posts was blocking every post, because the scoring behind it was measuring on the wrong scale. The gate was working. The judgment feeding it was wrong. I have spent fifteen years around how large organizations adopt technology. The failure was never the tool. It was the humans who stopped watching what the tool was actually doing and trusted the dashboard instead of the real output. I built the same trap for myself. A green light on the inside, a dark room on the outside. If you have handed a workflow to AI and it has "been running fine," go look at the output, not the status. The gap between "the system says it worked" and "the customer saw it" is where trust quietly leaks out. What is one automated thing in your business you have not actually looked at the output of this month?

LinkedIn · Lane: The room · Slot: Wed · PASS

The question owners actually ask about AI

I keep preparing to defend AI. The owners in the room keep asking something else entirely. I walk in ready for "will this replace my people." What I get is quieter and harder: "I already pay for three tools nobody uses. How do I know this is not a fourth?" That is not a technology question. That is a trust question wearing a technology costume. For years my seat was inside a Fortune 100, watching how decisions to adopt anything actually got made. It was rarely about the feature. It was about whether the person championing it had been burned before. So now I stop preparing the demo and start with the burn. Which tool did you buy that you regret, and what did it promise? Once that is on the table, the room changes. We are not talking about AI anymore. We are talking about how this owner decides to trust something new with real money. You cannot sell adoption to someone still paying for the last thing they were sold. If you are bringing AI into a team this year, ask the burn question first. The answer tells you everything about how to introduce the next thing.

My mother worked at the government telecom in Conakry. She taught me the thing AI keeps getting wrong.

My mother worked for the government telecom company in Conakry. Growing up in Guinea, I watched how a system that technically worked could still fail every person standing in line for it. The line moved. The forms got stamped. On paper, the process ran. And people still walked away without the thing they came for, because nobody in the system was measuring the human on the other end. I think about that every time someone shows me an AI rollout with a beautiful adoption metric. Usage is up. Logins are up. The dashboard is green. And the actual work the tool was supposed to change looks exactly the same as it did before. A stamped form is not a served person. A logged-in user is not a changed workflow. The two worlds I grew up between taught me to distrust the paper version of success and go look at the line. It is the single most useful instinct I bring into an AI conversation, and it has nothing to do with AI. Before you celebrate an adoption number, go find one person the tool was supposed to help and ask if their day is actually different.
