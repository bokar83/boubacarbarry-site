Post 1 — "Stop fixing, start diagnosing" REUSED

Source: docs/personal/book-source-material.md, section XIX, captured 2026-06-28 (a real three-rounds-of-failed-fixes-then-RCA incident). Stripped of the specific website/codename detail and reframed as a general lesson for a career/business-problem audience, per the no-internal-codenames rule.

Opening technique: jump straight into the scene, no time-anchor at all. Voice score: 93/100 · CTQ (Sankofa Council) score: 92/100 · Bookmarkability: 10/12

Three fixes in, and the problem was still there.

I was running a technical project, and every attempt targeted whatever looked broken. Each one failed differently. By the fourth round I stopped patching and asked a different question: what actually caused this, not what looks wrong.

Turned out the real problem sat one level up from where I kept looking. I kept fixing the part that was visibly ugly. The part that was actually broken was quiet and one layer removed.

Here's what I took from it. When you're on your third attempt at the same fix and it still isn't right, the problem usually isn't your execution. It's your diagnosis. You're solving the wrong problem well.

I see this constantly in HR and in AI adoption too. A team says their onboarding process is broken, fixes the paperwork, and nothing changes, because the actual break happened three steps earlier, in how the role got defined in the first place. Or a leader buys a new AI tool because a report is slow, when the actual issue is that nobody owns the report.

The fix, once you find the right layer, is usually smaller than the effort spent looking for it. One changed step. One reassigned owner. One clarified definition.

If you're stuck on something and you've already tried the obvious fix twice, stop fixing for a minute. Ask what's one level up from what you're staring at. That's usually where the answer is.

Post 2 — "The tool isn't the skill" REUSED

Source: docs/personal/book-source-material.md, section IX, captured 2026-06-21 ("the Boubacar superpower" — evaluating a tool built for one context by asking what the underlying method unlocks elsewhere). Reframed for a career-pivot/MBA audience evaluating whether a skill transfers.

Opening technique: lesson/insight stated first, then the story that proves it. Voice score: 92/100 · CTQ (Sankofa Council) score: 92/100 · Bookmarkability: 9/12

Never judge a tool by the industry it was built for. I forget that more than I'd like to admit, and I caught myself forgetting it again while building my own AI systems.

I was reviewing a tool built for a completely unrelated industry to solve someone else's problem. My first instinct was to dismiss it: wrong tool, wrong industry, not for me. Then I asked a better question. Forget the tool. What is the underlying method actually doing, and could that method solve a completely different problem I already have?

That one shift changed how I evaluate almost everything now, from technology to talent moves to which skill to build next.

Most people, when they see something that doesn't obviously apply to them, stop right there. Wrong industry, wrong background, not relevant. I used to do the same thing before I noticed the pattern in my own decisions.

The people who make the best career pivots do the opposite. They ask what the underlying skill or method actually is, strip away the label, and check if it ports somewhere else entirely. Someone who ran operations for a manufacturing plant and someone who ran a marketing funnel are doing more of the same underlying work than either one assumes.

This matters most right now, with so many people rethinking their careers around AI. The tool will keep changing every six months. The transferable skill underneath it, the ability to see what a method actually does and where else it applies, does not expire.

If you're evaluating a pivot, an MBA, or a new skill to build, start there. Not "is this exactly what I've done before" but "what is this actually teaching me how to do."

Post 3 — "The work nobody assigned me" NET-NEW

Source: Built from verified facts in memory/reference_boubacar_canonical_profile.md (the personal AI-tooling work done informally inside a large organization, and the 6-8hr-to-10min dashboard fact). No employer named, no tenure count stated, per the "never hand the reader your credentials inside a post" rule — framed as "when I was in the corporate world" instead.

Opening technique: a before/after contradiction, no time-anchor. Voice score: 92/100 · CTQ (Sankofa Council) score: 93/100 · Bookmarkability: 9/12

The dashboard took ten minutes. The report it replaced used to eat six to eight hours.

Back when I was in the corporate world, I watched good people get stuck inside processes that had outlived their purpose. Different sites, different countries, one thread running through all of it.

Somewhere in there I started building AI tools for myself, quietly, just to solve my own workload problem. That dashboard was one of them. I shared it informally with colleagues. Nobody assigned it to me. I built it because the old way was slow and I could see a faster one.

That's the moment I look back on now. Not a dramatic exit story, just a quiet realization that I was already doing the work I eventually left to do full time.

I left to build that work on my own terms: an operator who helps companies put AI to work, without the confusion and the overwhelm that usually comes with it.

The lesson I'd give anyone sitting inside a large organization right now, wondering if they should make a similar move: pay attention to the thing you're already doing that nobody assigned you. That unassigned work you can't stop yourself from doing is usually the clearest signal of what you should be building next.

Post 4 — "Fix the map before the tool" NET-NEW

Source: Built from the verified Kaizen / hiring-process fact in the canonical profile (part of the team that led it, never "I led it"; roughly 60-day to under-30-day cycle). Employer not named per the specific attribution rule on this result.

Opening technique: a physical/sensory detail from the room, no time-anchor. Voice score: 94/100 · CTQ (Sankofa Council) score: 93/100 · Bookmarkability: 10/12

Whiteboards covered in process maps, and a full week spent figuring out why the same steps kept showing up no matter who touched the file.

That's where I spent time on a Kaizen event focused on fixing our hiring process. Global reach, affecting thousands of hiring managers, and a hiring cycle that used to average around sixty days.

I wasn't the leader of that Kaizen. I was part of the team that led it, on a global HR transformation initiative, one voice among several who cared enough to spend a week in a room mapping every step of how a job requisition actually became a hire.

What struck me wasn't the AI or the automation we eventually added. It was how many steps existed only because nobody had asked, in years, whether they still needed to be there. Approvals waiting on people who'd left the company. Forms built for a system that had been replaced twice. Handoffs that existed because two teams had never actually talked to each other.

We got that cycle down from around sixty days to under thirty. Not because we found a magic tool. Because we finally looked at the whole thing at once instead of patching the part that was loudest that week.

I think about this every time someone tells me their team needs "an AI solution" for a slow process. Sometimes that's true. More often, the process has ten steps that made sense years ago and none that make sense now, and no one has looked at the whole map in years. Fix the map first. The tool gets a lot easier to pick after that.

Post 5 — "Presence isn't a soft skill" NET-NEW

Source: Built from the verified on-site/PPE/walk-the-floor facts in the canonical profile (section 7-8, the HOW-vs-WHAT rule his own resumes systematically hide). Reframed for the executive-coaching audience as a presence/visibility leadership lesson.

Opening technique: a direct question, no time-anchor. Voice score: 92/100 · CTQ (Sankofa Council) score: 91/100 · Bookmarkability: 9/12

How much of your week is spent where the work actually happens, versus where it gets summarized?

I ask that because my HR job title made it sound like I sat in an office reviewing policy. That was almost never where I actually spent my time.

I was on manufacturing floors, in repair shops, and near power plants regularly, following safety protocol and working alongside the people I supported. I still keep a set of personal protective equipment in my car out of habit, because you never know when you'll end up back on a site. At one large manufacturing location I covered, I made a point of walking the floor multiple times a day, not because anyone required it, but because I believe you cannot support people well from behind a desk you never leave.

That habit taught me something I now bring into every coaching conversation. The people closest to a problem almost always see it more clearly than the people reviewing a report about it. A dashboard tells you what happened. A walk across the floor tells you why.

If you're moving into a leadership role, or coaching someone who is, here's the test I use: track that ratio for a week. Both sides matter. But if all your time goes to the summary, you'll eventually manage a version of reality that stopped being true weeks ago.

Presence isn't a soft skill. It's how you keep your information honest.
