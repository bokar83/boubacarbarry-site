Stop Fixing, Start Diagnosing
The question I ask before I ask anything else in a coaching session
Three fixes in, and the problem was still there.

I was running a technical project, and every attempt targeted whatever looked broken. Each one failed differently. By the fourth round I stopped patching and asked a different question: what actually caused this, not what looks wrong.

Turned out the real problem sat one level up from where I kept looking. I kept fixing the part that was visibly ugly. The part that was actually broken was quiet and one layer removed.

That is the same move I make in a coaching conversation now. When someone brings me a stuck spot, I do not hand them a fix. I ask what is one level up from what they are staring at. Most people can answer that question themselves once someone makes them sit with it.

I see the pattern everywhere in HR and in AI adoption. A team says their onboarding process is broken, fixes the paperwork, and nothing changes, because the actual break happened three steps earlier, in how the role got defined. A leader buys a new AI tool because a report is slow, when the real issue is that nobody owns the report.

The fix, once you find the right layer, is usually smaller than the effort spent looking for it. One changed step. One reassigned owner. One clarified question asked out loud.

If you are stuck on something and you have already tried the obvious fix twice, stop fixing for a minute. Ask what is one level up from what you are staring at. That is usually where the answer, and the real coaching, lives.

The Tool Isn't the Skill
Why I never let a career pivot get decided by what someone already knows how to use
Never judge a tool by the industry it was built for. I forget that more than I would like to admit, and I caught myself forgetting it again while building my own AI systems.

I was reviewing a tool built for a completely unrelated industry, meant to solve someone else's problem. My first instinct was to dismiss it: wrong tool, wrong industry, not for me. Then I asked a better question. Forget the tool. What is the underlying method actually doing, and could that method solve a problem I already have?

That one shift changed how I evaluate almost everything now, technology, talent moves, and which skill to build next.

It is also the exact question I ask a coaching client who thinks their background does not transfer. I will not tell them what their skill is. I ask them what the underlying method behind their old job actually did, and where else that method could work. Someone who ran operations for a manufacturing plant and someone who ran a marketing funnel are doing more of the same underlying work than either one assumes, once you strip away the label.

This is exactly what matters when someone is rethinking a career pivot because of AI. The tool will keep changing every six months. The transferable skill underneath it, the ability to see what a method actually does and where else it applies, does not expire.

If you are evaluating a pivot, an MBA, or a new skill to build, start where I start with a client. Not "is this exactly what I have done before," but "what is this actually teaching me how to do."

The Work Nobody Assigned Me
What I built before anyone called it a job, and what that says about the next move
The dashboard took ten minutes. The report it replaced used to eat six to eight hours.

Back when I was in the corporate world, I watched good people get stuck inside processes that had outlived their purpose. Different sites, different countries, one thread running through all of it.

Somewhere in there I started building AI tools for myself, quietly, just to solve my own workload problem. That dashboard was one of them. I shared it informally with colleagues. Nobody assigned it to me. I built it because the old way was slow and I could see a faster one.

Around the same time, people started asking me a different kind of question. Not about the dashboard. About how to think through a stuck decision, an AI purchase they were not sure about, a team that would not move. I was already doing that work too, informally, unassigned, the same way I had built the tools.

That is the moment I look back on now. Not a dramatic exit story, just a quiet realization that I was already doing the work I eventually left to do full time: helping people put AI to work without the confusion and the overwhelm, using the same questions I always asked, out loud now instead of on the side.

The lesson I would give anyone sitting inside a large organization, wondering if they should make a similar move: pay attention to the thing you are already doing that nobody assigned you. That unassigned work is usually the clearest signal of what you should be building, or coaching, next.

Fix the Map Before the Tool
What a week of whiteboards taught me about the AI conversations I run now
Whiteboards covered in process maps, and a full week spent figuring out why the same steps kept showing up no matter who touched the file.

That is where I spent time on a Kaizen event focused on fixing a hiring process. Global reach, affecting thousands of hiring managers, and a hiring cycle that used to average around sixty days.

I was not the leader of that Kaizen. I was part of the team that led it, on a global HR transformation initiative, one voice among several who cared enough to spend a week in a room mapping every step of how a job requisition actually became a hire.

What struck me was not the AI or the automation we eventually added. It was how many steps existed only because nobody had asked, in years, whether they still needed to be there. Approvals waiting on people who had left the company. Forms built for a system replaced twice. Handoffs that existed because two teams had never actually talked to each other.

We got that cycle down from around sixty days to under thirty. Not because we found a magic tool. Because we finally looked at the whole thing at once instead of patching the part that was loudest that week.

I run every coaching conversation about AI the same way now. I will not start with the tool. I start by asking what the actual map looks like, step by step, before anyone touches a piece of software.

I think about this every time a leader tells me their team needs an AI solution for a slow process. Sometimes that is true. More often, the process has ten steps that made sense years ago and none that make sense now, and no one has looked at the whole map in years. Fix the map first. The tool gets a lot easier to pick after that.

Presence Isn't a Soft Skill
The ratio I ask every new leader I coach to track for one week
How much of your week is spent where the work actually happens, versus where it gets summarized?

I ask that because my HR job title made it sound like I sat in an office reviewing policy. That was almost never where I actually spent my time.

I was on manufacturing floors, in repair shops, and near power plants regularly, following safety protocol and working alongside the people I supported. I still keep a set of personal protective equipment in my car out of habit, because you never know when you'll end up back on a site. At one large manufacturing location I covered, I made a point of walking the floor multiple times a day, not because anyone required it, but because I believe you cannot support people well from behind a desk you never leave.

That habit taught me something I now bring into every coaching conversation. The people closest to a problem almost always see it more clearly than the people reviewing a report about it. A dashboard tells you what happened. A walk across the floor tells you why.

If you're moving into a leadership role, or coaching someone who is, here's the test I use: track that ratio for a week. Both sides matter. But if all your time goes to the summary, you'll eventually manage a version of reality that stopped being true weeks ago.

Presence isn't a soft skill. It's how you keep your information honest.
