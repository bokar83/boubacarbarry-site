Your pitch

The locked wording, the doors to open with, and what stays out · 2026-09-09

TL;DR

I help companies put AI to work without the confusion and the overwhelm. Most feel like they have to use AI right now, so I help them take a breath and get clear: do you even need it, where does it fit, and how do you make it work. Sometimes I step in and lead it myself, as their HR leader, their chief AI officer, or whatever the job needs. I know it works because I run my own business on AI, on a system I built myself. I’ve already crossed the bridge everyone’s still talking about.

That is the default, word for word. Everything below is how to open, who to open it on, and what stays out.

The pitch, locked
I help companies put AI to work without the confusion and the overwhelm. Most feel like they have to use AI right now, so I help them take a breath and get clear: do you even need it, where does it fit, and how do you make it work. Sometimes I step in and lead it myself, as their HR leader, their chief AI officer, or whatever the job needs. I know it works because I run my own business on AI, on a system I built myself. I’ve already crossed the bridge everyone’s still talking about.

Why it lands in that order.

  1. Their problem first. Confusion, overwhelm, the pressure to adopt AI right now.
  2. The value. Help them take a breath and get clear on IF, WHERE, HOW.
  3. How you plug in. Fractional, and the roles stay open ended. Never a fixed two item list. "or whatever the job needs" is the part that keeps the door open.
  4. Proof as the kicker, at the very end. You run your own business on AI, on a system you built yourself. The "system I built myself" detail IS the credibility.

Talking to a CHRO or an HR peer

I help companies put AI to work without getting burned, and I step in fractionally to lead it, sometimes as their chief HR officer, sometimes as their chief AI officer. You and I share the HR roots, but the reason this works is that I run my own business on AI now. I have already crossed the bridge everyone keeps talking about.

"You and I share the HR roots" is peer rapport with no number in it. A named tie you genuinely share with the person in front of you, a school, a company, a mutual, is rapport and not resume. That one is fine.

Pick the door, not the same line every time

His own instruction: "it may be different for each person, it doesn’t always have to be the same." Read the room, pick the door. Do not make any one of these the new line you use everywhere.

The test, plainly: if the opening line would be redundant to someone who clicked your profile first, cut it. Tenure, employers, titles and credentials fail that test by definition. They ARE the profile.

  1. Decide versus build. Almost nobody crosses the line between the person who decides and the person who builds. Executives commission systems and stay on the decision side. Engineers build them and stay on the build side. You taught yourself to code to understand the machine well enough to bend it to your own and your clients' needs, explicitly not to become a software engineer, mid career, with no title change and nobody asking you to. Best for: founders, operators, technical buyers, and anyone doing real diligence on you.
  2. Paid the cost of your own advice. You run your own business on a system you wrote yourself, so when you advise on AI you have already lived with the consequences of that advice. Best for: skeptical buyers, and anyone burned by an advisor who never uses what they sell.
  3. Learned to understand, not to sell. You taught yourself to code to understand how the machine works, not to pad a resume. That inverts the usual motive and disarms people braced for a pitch. Best for: CHROs and HR peers who have seen a hundred AI vendors.
  4. Readiness and proximity. You keep your PPE in the car from your GE years. Not a safety platitude, a readiness habit: you expect to be where the work happens, unannounced. Same instinct as walking the plant floor several times a day so people brought you problems directly instead of waiting for email. Best for: operators and site facing audiences. Texture inside a longer story, rarely a cold opener.
  5. Gen X chasm, crossed twice. Your generation already crossed one technology chasm, analog to digital, with no manual. You are crossing the AI one deliberately and can hand people the map. Best for: Gen X and older business owners who distrust 20 something AI gurus.
  6. Two continent bridge. Guinea and America, both lived, neither held as backstory to the other. Best for: global org conversations. Strongest as the answer to "how did you end up doing this", weakest as a cold opener.

What never goes in
  • No resume stuffing. Not "15 years", not "Fortune 100", not the MBA, not RBL, not GE, not the country count. Default is to omit background entirely. Include one only if it genuinely helps them understand you or moves the story forward.
  • Never say you ran HR or operations, or that you were an executive. You were IN HR at large companies and helped run things within HR. Operations adjacent only. The string "15 years running HR + operations inside Fortune 100 companies" is banned outright.
  • Never present your roles as a closed two item list. Always leave the tail open.
  • Prefer the qualitative version over the number if a background fact truly has to appear. "a Fortune 100 company", never "10 years at a Fortune 100 company".
  • Your only needed proof is that you run your own business on AI. Everything else is backup, brought up if they ask.
