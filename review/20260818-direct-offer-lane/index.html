The short version

2 touches tomorrow. One call, one email. That is the whole day.

One door, fractional HR and annual review season. One track, people who already know you. Everything else stays parked until the habit holds.

The first contact is diagnostic, never a pitch. Find out what hurts. The single offer comes after they name the pain in their own words.

Your frame, not mine: this is None to Run. Get around the track once before running the marathon.

The call, start to finish

Before you dial, thirty seconds. Two lines on screen and nothing else: the one specific thing you know about them, and the one solution you would offer if the pain matches. One. If two are on the screen you have not decided, and they will hear it.

Open.

[Name], Boubacar. Do you have ten minutes, or should I call you back? I will be straight with you. I am not here to pitch you. I want to understand how things actually run on your side. If there is nothing here for me, I will tell you that myself.

The questions, in this order.

  1. Walk me through what your week actually looks like right now.
  2. What is the part of it you would hand to someone else tomorrow if you could?
  3. How long has that been true?
  4. What have you already tried on it?
  5. What did that cost you, in time or money or people?
  6. If it stayed exactly like this through the end of the year, what happens?
  7. Who else feels this besides you?

While they talk. Count to three after every answer before you speak, the second half of an answer is where the truth is. Write their exact words. Solve nothing yet, the moment you solve they stop telling you things. If you are about to offer two options, stop, because two options is the sound of not having decided.

The pivot, only after they name the pain.

So the real problem is [their words, not yours]. Do I have that right? One thing I have done is [the single closest thing in the toolbox]. If that would help, I can do that for you.

Then stop talking. The silence after that sentence is theirs.

The toolbox, pick exactly one.

  • Review cycle: one thing I have done is plan a review cycle and run the reviews themselves.
  • Hiring: one thing I have done is simplify a hiring process so it stops living in one person's head.
  • AI: one thing I have done is take one workflow that eats a day a week and get it running end to end.

If nothing fits: "I do not think I am the right fit for that one, and I would rather say so." That buys more than a stretched yes.

Close. On a match, something short in writing by a named day, not a proposal deck. On no match, send nothing and do not invent a reason to follow up. Either way: "who else should be hearing what I just described?"

Log the objection verbatim the moment you hang up, before the next call.

Lead with the $2,500 sprint. It is under most Salt Lake signature thresholds, the urgency is real and not manufactured, and it converts into the $5,000 monthly six weeks later. Full review season as one flat number is $12,000, or $18,000 with the hiring loop rebuilt.

You do not discount the price. You shrink the scope. If you must move for a first client: half off the entry sprint in exchange for a written testimonial and a recorded debrief, named as a founding-client rate with the standard price shown beside it. Never discount the monthly retainer. Discount an entry fee once and you lose $1,250. Discount the retainer once and you lose the anchor permanently.

When they ask what you charge before anything is scoped:

Depends what we scope, so let me not guess. Most of my work sits between twenty-five hundred and ten thousand a month, and one day a week at five thousand is where most companies land. If you want something smaller to start, I have a fixed two-week piece at twenty-five hundred. Tell me what's actually broken and I'll tell you which one fits, or tell you it doesn't.

Three things that would be fatal: billing hourly, letting the $50 workshop become your reference price, and undercharging the first AI client when the published $5,000 anchor is an asset you have not spent yet.

Tripwires, and the one that matters

The money tripwire, 2026-10-17. If there is no paid work and no booked paid-scope conversation by day 60, the lane changes shape, not its copy. Named in advance so it is not a debate on the day: go in person only, or partner with someone who already holds the buyer relationships, or take a contract role to fund the runway. Not "send more".

Red team's hardest hit was that every other tripwire measured effort, so this could pass at day 60 with a full log and zero dollars. The rest, in order:

  1. Fewer than one real conversation per 25 touches by day 20 forces an offer rewrite, never more volume.
  2. Three consecutive days under the floor.
  3. Any day with fewer than three verified triggers or warm reasons.
  4. Weekly call count under five. Phone avoidance is the quiet killer.
  5. The block gets moved rather than shortened. A missed block is logged as missed, not rescheduled.

Beating the call fear, by design. Fifteen minutes and three dials, friendliest name first, nothing improvised, text or email first saying you will call at a set time, count dials and not outcomes, log the objection right after hanging up, and calls before email always. Email first is avoidance in a productive costume.
