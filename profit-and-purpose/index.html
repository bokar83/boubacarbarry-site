The Friar Who Paid for the Renaissance

Let me take you back about 500 years, because none of this is a new idea and I want you to see that.

In 1494, a Franciscan friar named Luca Pacioli published a mathematics book. Buried inside it was a section explaining how the merchants of Venice kept their books. Every transaction written down twice, once on each side of the ledger, so that nothing could go missing and nothing could be quietly stolen.

Double-entry bookkeeping. Possibly the most boring thing any man has ever put into print.

Now here is the part nobody tells you. This friar was not some hustle bro with a course to sell. He lived with Leonardo da Vinci and taught the man mathematics, and the two of them worked out of the same house for years.

Leonardo got the paintings. The friar got the ledger, and the ledger went on to run Europe for the next four hundred years.

The Medici ran a bank across the whole of it on ledgers like that. Florence funded the Renaissance on ledgers like that. Not on genius. Not on inspiration. On RECORDS. On the tedious, unglamorous, nobody-claps-for-it work of writing down who owes what and then actually following up on it.

Everybody remembers the art.

THE ART WAS PAID FOR BY THE BOOKKEEPING.

The Work Nobody Wants to Do

I told you recently that roughly 90% of our biggest sales month came from EXISTING CUSTOMERS. Not new leads. Not a viral post. Not a funnel. People who had already bought from us and came back for more.

Everybody nodded at that. Almost nobody did a single thing about it.

I know why.

Because retention is not exciting. Nobody ever built a personal brand on retention. There is no dopamine in it. It is a hundred small sh*tty little jobs that never make it to the top of anybody's list:

The buyer who purchased eight months ago and has not heard from you since.

The subscriber who quietly cancelled and you never even noticed.

The customer who got a life-changing result and was never once asked for a testimonial.

The person who replied to your email with a real question and got nothing back.

And here is the honest part, and this is the one thing today I am not going to insult you about. It is not that you don't care. It is that you are ONE PERSON, there are only so many hours in a week, and when it comes down to writing the piece or chasing the follow-up, you write the piece.

Every time. As you should.

And then the week is gone. And the hours that were left over were never the business's to take in the first place. They belong to a wife. To a son. To an hour on a Sunday that belongs to God and not to your inbox. To a father who would like a phone call and is never once going to ask you for one.

THAT IS A STAFFING PROBLEM.

It was never a motivation problem. Almost nobody in this game has a knowledge gap. They have a CAPACITY gap, and you cannot read your way out of one of those.

Point It at the Right Job

Here is the rule. Write it down.

NEVER LET IT WRITE. ALWAYS LET IT WORK.

The robot does not touch your voice, your story, your opinion or your name. Not one sentence. Not ever. It handles the work you were never going to get to anyway. Who bought what and when. Who used to open everything and has gone quiet. The follow-up that has been sitting undone for six months, which YOU send, in your own words.

Does one word of that sound like writing to you?

And somebody reading this is already getting ready to close the tab, and honestly, he has earned the right to be annoyed with me.

"You just spent a thousand words telling me my problem is that I have no hours. Now you want me to go build a back office. With what hours, exactly?"

Good. That is the correct objection, and most guys writing about AI will never answer it.

So here is the answer. You are not building a system this week and you are not wiring anything up to anything. You are going to do one single thing, it takes about twenty minutes, and it is finished before Saturday.

  1. Open wherever your sales actually live, export the buyer list, and sort it by purchase date. Find the ten oldest people who bought one time and never came back.
  2. Paste those ten into the machine and ask it for exactly one thing: what each person bought and how long ago. Not copy, not a subject line, not one word of persuasion. Just the facts.
  3. Write the ten emails yourself, every word of them, three sentences each. Name the thing they bought and ask one real question about how it went. Sell nothing.
  4. Send them before Saturday.

The machine did the filing and you did the talking, which is this whole article run at the smallest scale it comes in.

The profit was never the point. The profit is what buys back the hours, and those hours belong to the people who are still going to be sitting at your table long after every platform in this game is dead and forgotten.

The tool is three years old. The strategy is five hundred.

Rome Had A Word For This

There was an hour in the Roman morning that had its own name.

Salutatio.

Before the courts sat, before anybody did a day's work, the most powerful men in the city stood in their own front halls and received a line of people at the door.

Not prospects. Not leads. Clients. Men who were already attached to them.

They came every morning. They said good morning. They asked for a favour, a word with a magistrate, advice, sometimes just a basket of food. The patron gave what he gave. Then they walked with him down to the forum, and the size of the crowd walking behind a man WAS his power, out loud, in the street, where anybody could stand there and count it.

Now here is the part that should stop you.

THE DEAL WAS ALREADY DONE.

Nobody in that hall was being sold anything. The relationship had been made years earlier, sometimes generations earlier. That morning hour existed for one reason and one reason only: to keep the thing from dying.

A Roman with a thousand clients did not spend his mornings hunting a thousand strangers.

He went downstairs and opened the door.

→ He spent the first hour of every single day on people who owed him nothing new.

That is the whole lesson and I could honestly end the article right here.

Your buyer list is your front hall.

You have not opened the door in a year.

The Ninety Days. Four Moves.

This is the part you paid for, so I am going to be specific.

Not a funnel. Four moves, on four days, and then it starts again with the next buyer.

DAY ONE. MAKE THE THING WORK.

Not "thanks for your purchase". One message, from you, that walks him to the first part that actually does something for him. Open this. Do this one piece. Do it today. Nothing produces a refund faster than a man who bought something and never opened it, and you can kill most of that with one message on the first day.

DAY SEVEN. ASK THE ONE QUESTION.

"What was going on in your business the week you decided to buy this?"

That is the entire message. And what comes back is not feedback. It is your next twelve months of copy, written in the customer's own words, for free, by the exact person you were about to sit there and guess about.

DAY THIRTY. GO AND GET THE RESULT.

Not a testimonial. Nobody has a testimonial on day thirty and asking for one makes you look needy. Ask what changed. Ask for the small thing. "What is different now that was not different a month ago?" A few of them will hand you a sentence you could not have written with a gun to your head.

DAY NINETY. OPEN THE NEXT DOOR.

Ninety days is about when a man stops thinking of himself as somebody who bought a thing and starts thinking of himself as somebody who is building. That is the day he can hear the next offer. Not day two, when he is still a bit nervous about the money. Day ninety, when it worked.

Four messages. Four days. Per buyer.

The bottleneck was that nobody was counting.

That is your salutatio. Four names, eleven minutes, before the day starts.

Sample three · a second Wednesday, the history mode

THE MAN WHO COULD NOT MAKE STEEL

Andrew Carnegie wrote his own epitaph. It says nothing about steel.

Read the full pieceClose the piece 885 words

Andrew Carnegie did not know how to make steel.

Read that again, because it is one of the strangest facts in the history of American business. The man whose name sat on the largest steel company on earth could not have run a furnace to save his life. He was not an engineer. He was not a chemist. He could not have told you the temperature at which pig iron turns into something a man can build a bridge out of.

He was a telegraph boy from Dunfermline who came off a boat with nothing.

And at the end of it all he wrote his own epitaph. Out of an entire life he could have chosen any sentence he wanted to sit over him forever, and this is the one he picked:

Here lies a man who knew how to enlist in his service better men than himself.

Not a man who worked harder than everybody else.

Not a man who knew more than everybody else.

A man who knew how to get the work OUT of other people.

Now think about how the average creator online operates today.

Or better than that, think honestly about how YOU operated last Tuesday.

He writes the post, edits the video, answers the emails, chases the invoice, updates the spreadsheet, builds the landing page, fixes the link that broke on the landing page, and then somewhere in the wreckage of all that he tries to have one original thought worth publishing. Then he wonders why he has been sitting at exactly the same level for two years.

He is not lazy.

He is the precise opposite of lazy, and THAT is the problem.

Every one of these poor guys will tell you he is grinding. Grinding at what? Data entry?

→ He made himself the bottleneck in his own business and then called it work ethic.

Carnegie understood something that the modern solo operator flat out refuses to accept.

YOUR OUTPUT IS CAPPED BY WHAT YOU PERSONALLY TOUCH.

Not by how much you know. Not by how badly you want it. By the number of hours in a week and the number of jobs you refuse to put down.

Every job you keep is a job that will eventually stop you.

How many are you holding right now?

Here is what Carnegie actually did about it, because this is the part everybody skips.

He went and found Captain Bill Jones, who could run a steel mill better than Carnegie ever would, and paid him a salary reportedly equal to the President of the United States. He did not go and learn metallurgy. He bought the men who already had it, he got out of their way, and he spent his own hours on the two or three decisions that only he could make.

That was the price of admission for the entire industrial age. To stop being the bottleneck you needed capital, buildings, a payroll, and a town full of men willing to work in the heat.

Which is exactly why almost nobody ever got out of the trap at all.

That price collapsed.

You can hand off the unglamorous half of your business today without hiring one single human being.

Not the writing. Not the thinking. Not your voice. Nobody should ever touch those, and any man telling you to automate your personality is selling you the fastest available route to being forgotten by everybody who ever cared about you.

But the follow-up that has been sitting undone since March.

The buyer who paid you in January and has not heard one word from you since.

The subscriber who quietly cancelled while you were busy making content.

The customer who got a genuine result out of your work and was never once asked to say so out loud.

Those were always supposed to be somebody else's job.

And this is the one part of today I am not going to insult you about, because it was never a discipline problem. You are one man. When it comes down to writing the piece or chasing the follow-up, you write the piece, every single time, and you are right to.

THAT IS A STAFFING PROBLEM.

It was never a motivation problem.

So why are those jobs still yours?

Because you never had a payroll.

NOW YOU DO.

Carnegie's genius was never steel. It was knowing which work was his and refusing to touch the rest.

That is still the whole game.

Why it is built this way

This is the other mode you run. One figure from history carrying a business argument the whole way, no framework, no list. Caesar, Joe Girard, Alexander Pope. It is the register that makes the page read like it was written by somebody who actually reads, and it is the one most ghostwriters cannot do at all, because it needs the anchor to genuinely fit the argument rather than decorate it.

Carnegie fits because his own epitaph makes the argument for me, so the piece never has to reach. And Captain Bill Jones is the beat that makes it a Saturday rather than a nice story: Carnegie did not go and learn metallurgy, he bought the man who already had it and got out of his way. That is the mechanism, and it is the thing you never skip.

What I found

  • The white space is the page

    Most of your paragraphs are a single sentence, somewhere between thirty-five and fifty-five of them in a Wednesday piece. That one habit is the most recognisable thing about your writing and it is the first thing an imitator flattens without noticing.

  • The shout is a metronome

    Standalone all-caps lines, and the count moves with the piece. I counted anywhere from one to nine of them per article, with the middle at four, and about twenty-three capitalised words per thousand across everything. THAT IS WARFARE. YOU DON'T KNOW WHAT YOU DON'T KNOW. They mark time. They are not decoration and they are not volume.

  • The insult is affectionate and it always comes with a way out

    You go at the behaviour, never at the man's dignity, and you never leave him standing there. The line right after the insult is the escape route. That order is the trick, and it is why your readers take it from you and would take it from almost nobody else.

  • The profanity is masked

    You asterisk it more often than not. Written plain, it stops sounding like you.

  • The anchor is old

    Joe Girard in 1973. Salesmen out of an out-of-print book from the seventies. You reach back decades or centuries for authority, and across everything I read I did not find one instance of you citing a current internet name to prove a point.
