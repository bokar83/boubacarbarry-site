The writing

Five pieces, because the job is five jobs.

Your post did not describe a newsletter gig. It said banger articles Wednesday and Saturday, study what works, promote the page, interact with readers, possibly lead weekly discussions, and do whatever it takes. So this is not two newsletters.

Two full pieces for the Wednesday and Saturday slots. Then three short ones for the rest of it: the promotion, the reader who shows up in the comments, and the weekly discussion. The short ones exist because the newsletter is the part everybody applying will send you, and the other four jobs are the ones that actually decide whether this thing gets to fifty thousand.

Every piece is built from material you have already published. No invented numbers, no invented stories, nothing put in your mouth that you have not already said somewhere.

The Robot Cannot Be You

A few weeks ago I told you that information has never been cheaper. That is still true and it gets more true every single day.

Anyone can open their phone right now and get a marketing plan, a workout, a business model, and a summary of a book they are never going to read.

So what happens when every human being on earth is carrying the same infinite information machine in his pocket?

The information becomes worthless and the PERSON becomes everything.

Which is why handing your voice to a chatbot is the dumbest trade available to you in 2026. You are taking the one asset on earth that nobody can copy and swapping it for the one thing everybody already has for free.

→ Generic AI slop is not competing with good writing. It is competing with an ocean of other generic AI slop.

Your story is not in there. Your scars are not in there. That specific way you say a thing that makes a stranger stop scrolling is not in there.

A robot can produce a sentence.

IT CANNOT PRODUCE A REPUTATION.

But I Am Not Telling You to Throw It Out

Here is where most people writing about this get lazy, and that includes a few guys I genuinely respect.

They see the slop, they decide AI is for frauds, and they refuse to touch the thing at all.

That is just as dumb as the fool outsourcing his personality to it.

Both men are making the exact same mistake. They both think the only job AI can do is WRITE.

Why would that be the only job it can do?

And I already know what some of you low-IQ geniuses are thinking. "I tried it once and it wrote like a robot."

Not "what can AI write for me."

QUESTION TO READER: What is the work in your business that nobody sees, nobody enjoys, and nobody is doing?

Because THAT is the actual opportunity. And I would bet real money you already know what it is.

The Friar Who Paid for the Renaissance

Let me take you back about 500 years, because none of this is a new idea and I want you to see that.

In 1494, a Franciscan friar named Luca Pacioli published a mathematics book. Buried inside it was a section explaining how the merchants of Venice kept their books. Every transaction written down twice, once on each side of the ledger, so that nothing could go missing and nothing could be quietly stolen.

Double-entry bookkeeping. Possibly the most boring thing any man has ever put into print.

Now here is a fun fact for you. This friar was not some hustle bro with a course to sell. He lived with Leonardo da Vinci. He taught Leonardo mathematics. The two of them worked out of the same house.

Of those two men, which one do you think changed the way the whole world moves money?

It was not the painter.

The Medici ran a bank across the whole of Europe on ledgers like that. Florence funded the Renaissance on ledgers like that. Not on genius. Not on inspiration. On RECORDS. On the tedious, unglamorous, nobody-claps-for-it work of writing down who owes what and then actually following up on it.

Everybody remembers the art.

THE ART WAS PAID FOR BY THE BOOKKEEPING.

The Work Nobody Wants to Do

I told you recently that roughly 90% of our biggest sales month came from EXISTING CUSTOMERS. Not new leads. Not a viral post. Not a funnel. People who had already bought from us and came back for more.

Everybody nodded at that. Almost nobody did a single thing about it.

I know why.

Because retention is not exciting. Nobody ever built a personal brand on retention. There is no dopamine in it. It is a hundred small sh*tty little jobs that never make it to the top of anybody's list:

→ The buyer who purchased eight months ago and has not heard from you since.
→ The subscriber who quietly cancelled and you never even noticed.
→ The customer who got a life-changing result and was never once asked for a testimonial.
→ The person who replied to your email with a real question and got nothing back.

So why go hunting for another stranger when there are people sitting right there who already paid you once?

Every one of those is money. Every one of those is sitting inside your business right now, rotting.

And here is the honest part, and this is the one thing today I am not going to insult you about. It is not that you don't care. It is that you are ONE PERSON, there are only so many hours in a week, and when it comes down to writing the piece or chasing the follow-up, you write the piece.

Every time. As you should.

THAT IS A STAFFING PROBLEM.

It was never a motivation problem.

Twenty People in Dallas and One Problem

At the Mastermind we ran hot seats. Every person stood up in front of the room and explained what he was building and where he was stuck.

I went in expecting strategy. Offers. Positioning. Content.

What we actually found, over and over and over again, was a room full of seriously talented people whose businesses were leaking out of the back. Buyer lists nobody maintained. Follow-up nobody sent. Warm relationships going cold because there were not enough hours in the week.

Nobody in that room had a knowledge gap.

They had a CAPACITY gap.

Can you see the difference? Because that difference is worth a f*cking fortune.

Point It at the Right Job

Here is the rule. Write it down.

NEVER LET IT WRITE. ALWAYS LET IT WORK.

The robot does not touch your voice, your story, your opinion or your name. Not one sentence. Not ever.

The robot handles the work you were never going to get to anyway:

→ It knows who bought from you, when, and what they bought.
→ It notices when somebody who used to open everything has gone quiet.
→ It drafts the follow-up that has been sitting undone for six months, and YOU send it, in your own words.
→ It keeps the list of everybody who told you they got a result, so you can go back and ask them to say it publicly.

None of that requires the machine to be charming. It only requires it to be tireless.

Does one word of that sound like writing to you?

And that is the trade almost nobody is making. They handed the machine the one job that needs a soul, and kept for themselves the one job that needs a filing cabinet.

Flip it.

The One-Man Company Is Now a Real Company

Do you understand what this actually means? Because it is a lot bigger than a productivity tip.

For the entire history of building online, the ceiling on a solo operator was never talent. It was the back office. At some point you either hired people or you stopped growing. That was the deal, and everybody just accepted it.

That ceiling moved.

Pacioli's merchants needed clerks. The Medici needed a building full of them. You do not. You can run the operational half of a real business on your own now, with no payroll, no manager, and without turning into the kind of man who spends his entire week administrating instead of creating.

Which frees you up for the only two things that were ever going to make you rich in the first place:

Write like a human. Take care of the people who already bought.

The tool is three years old. The strategy is five hundred.

Sample two · the short history piece

THE MAN WHO HIRED BETTER MEN THAN HIMSELF

Andrew Carnegie could not make steel. That was never the point.

Read the full pieceClose the piece About 3 minutes

Andrew Carnegie did not know how to make steel.

Read that again, because it is one of the strangest facts in American business. The man whose name is on the steel industry could not have run a furnace to save his life. He was not an engineer. He was not a chemist. He was not a metallurgist.

He wrote his own epitaph, and this is what it says:

Here lies a man who knew how to enlist in his service better men than himself.

Not a man who worked harder than everybody else. Not a man who knew more than everybody else.

A man who knew how to get the work OUT of other people.

Now think about how the average creator online operates today.

Or better, think about how YOU operated last Tuesday.

He writes the post, edits the video, answers the emails, chases the invoice, updates the spreadsheet, builds the landing page, and somewhere in the middle of all that tries to have one original thought. Then he wonders why he has been stuck at the same level for two years.

He is not lazy. He is the exact opposite of lazy. THAT is the problem.

And every one of these poor guys will tell you he is working himself to the bone. At what? Data entry?

→ He made himself the bottleneck in his own business and then called it work ethic.

Carnegie understood something the modern solo operator flat out refuses to accept.

YOUR OUTPUT IS CAPPED BY WHAT YOU PERSONALLY TOUCH.

Every job you keep is a job that will eventually stop you.

Here is the difference, and it is the only reason I am writing this today.

Carnegie needed capital and a payroll to escape that trap. He needed buildings, managers, and thousands of men. For most of human history that was the price of admission, which is exactly why so few people ever got out of the trap at all.

That price collapsed.

You can hand off the unglamorous half of your business today without hiring one single person. Not the writing. Not the thinking. Not your voice. Nobody should ever touch those, and anybody telling you to automate your personality is selling you a fast route to being forgotten.

But the follow-up. The records. The reminders. The hundred small jobs that quietly decide whether a customer stays or leaves.

Those were always supposed to be somebody else's job.

Why are they still yours?

You just never had a payroll.

NOW YOU DO.

Carnegie's genius was knowing which work was his.

That is still the whole game.

Why it is built this way

This is the hardest one to fake and it is the reason it is here. Your move with a reader is not to be nice and it is not to be cruel. You insult the behaviour, then you immediately hand over something that costs you real effort and helps him, then you send him off with a command. Anybody can copy the insult. Almost nobody remembers to pay for it.

So the reply lands the joke, gives him a twenty minute job he can actually do tonight, and corrects the one line in his comment that was quietly holding him back. It ends on two words, which is how you end things.

Where I would tighten it with you. The comments are the one place a ghostwriter can genuinely damage you, because it is you talking to a paying customer in public. Your voice

How I built the voice, and what it still cannot tell me.

Not to explain you to yourself. To show you exactly what I was aiming at, so you can point at the parts I got wrong instead of having to describe your own voice from scratch. That is a much shorter conversation, and it is the one I would want to have in our first week.

What I actually read

Twelve full Profit & Purpose issues from March to August, the promotional emails around them, the community broadcasts you send from the store, the price-raise sequence, the hiring post itself, and what I could reach of your timeline. Roughly twenty two thousand words of you, read end to end rather than skimmed for phrases.

Then I stopped reading and started counting, because the things that make a voice recognisable are mostly mechanical and mostly invisible to the person doing them.

The eight things I was aiming at

  • You open on the enemy, not on yourself

    The community emails open warm, My friends. The Substack essays usually skip the greeting entirely and open cold on a type of person doing it wrong. The warm opener belongs to the announcements. Using the wrong one is the fastest way to sound like an imitation.

  • Short. Then shorter. Then one that runs

    Almost every line stands alone with white space around it. Then one long sprawling sentence breaks the run and you snap straight back to two words. A draft of yours written in normal three-sentence paragraphs is not yours, whatever the words say.

  • Capitals are the shout, not the punctuation

    Two or three standalone shouted lines a piece, always on the line carrying the argument, plus single words capitalised mid-sentence. Exclamation marks stay rare. Arrows mark a consequence, and labelled interjections do the turns: REALITY:, QUESTION TO READER:.

  • You swear at ideas, never at the reader, and you mask it

    About once a piece, asterisked four times out of five, never in an opener and never in a closer, and usually with a wink right after. Writing the word out plainly would be off, even when the word is right.

  • You punch at a type, never at a person

    Dorks, noobs, hustle bros, mental midgets, low-IQ geniuses. In everything I read you have never named and attacked a real human being. The insult is always followed by the way out, which is the part imitators drop.

  • The mechanism is the non-negotiable beat

    You never say a thing fails without explaining the machinery underneath it, and the explanation is always concrete: a parable, a named study, or your own exact numbers. It is why a piece about algorithms spends five paragraphs on Caesar. Anybody can copy the shouting. Almost nobody bothers with this.

  • You tell people not to buy

    Every hard sell carries an explicit exit. Please DO NOT become a paid subscriber. If not, no hard feelings. You never apply pressure, on the grounds that needing pressure means the product is the problem. A ghostwriter who missed that would wreck the paid pitch in one line.

  • Never an em-dash

    Not one, anywhere, in twenty two thousand words. No semicolons either, and no bold. Emphasis is capitals and white space. There are none of those anywhere above.

It only knows the newsletter properly. The Wednesday and Saturday register is the one I have enough of to be confident about. The promotional register I have second hand from emails. It reads two different men. The 2021 material and the 2026 newsletter are not the same voice. No profanity in the older work, epigraphs instead of jabs, a two-word command to close. And it has never been marked by you. A voice model built from the outside can be internally consistent and still be wrong in ways only you can see. Nothing here has been checked against your judgement. That is not a flaw I can engineer out. It is the reason the first thirty days below have you reviewing everything before it goes out.
