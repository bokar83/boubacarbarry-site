Sample one A full Wednesday, in your voice

The article.

AI IS NOT YOUR GHOSTWRITER. IT'S YOUR STAFF.

You are pointing the robot at the one job it is worst at.

Every week I watch another creator hand over the only thing that makes him valuable.

He feeds his story into a chatbot. He asks it for hooks. He asks it for a newsletter. Then he publishes whatever comes out and sits there refreshing the page wondering why nobody cares.

And then he tells me AI is coming for writers.

Wrong.

AI is not coming for your writing. It is coming for your ADMIN. And almost nobody in this game has figured that out, which is exactly why we are going to talk about it today.

Let's get into it.

The robot cannot be you

A few weeks ago I told you that information has never been cheaper. That is still true and it is only getting more true. Anyone can open their phone right now and get a marketing plan, a workout, a business model and a summary of a book they will never read.

So what happens when every single person has access to the same infinite information machine?

The information becomes worthless and the PERSON becomes everything.

Which is precisely why handing your voice to a chatbot is the dumbest trade available to you right now. You are taking the one asset that cannot be copied and swapping it for the one thing everybody already has for free.

→ Generic AI slop is not competing with good writing. It is competing with an ocean of other generic AI slop.

Your story is not in there. Your scars are not in there. The specific way you say a thing that makes a stranger stop scrolling is not in there.

A robot can produce a sentence. It cannot produce a REPUTATION.

But I am not telling you to throw it out

Here is where most people writing about this get lazy, including a lot of guys I respect.

They see slop, they decide AI is for frauds, and they refuse to touch it at all. That is just as dumb as the fool outsourcing his personality to it. Both men are making the same mistake. They both think the only job AI can do is WRITE.

I want to give you a better question.

Not "what can AI write for me."

QUESTION TO READER: What is the work in your business that nobody sees, nobody enjoys, and nobody is doing?

Because that is the actual opportunity. And I would bet money you already know what it is.

The work nobody wants to do

I told you recently that roughly 90% of our biggest sales month came from EXISTING CUSTOMERS. Not new leads. Not a viral post. Not a funnel. People who had already bought from us and came back.

Everybody nodded at that. Almost nobody did anything about it.

I know why.

Because retention is not exciting. Nobody built a personal brand on retention. There is no dopamine in it. It is a hundred small unglamorous jobs that never make it to the top of anyone's list:

→ The buyer who purchased eight months ago and never heard from you again.
→ The subscriber who quietly cancelled and you never noticed.
→ The customer who got an incredible result and was never asked for a testimonial.
→ The person who replied to your email with a real question and got nothing back.

Every one of those is money. Every one of those is sitting in your business right now, rotting.

And here is the honest part. It is not that you don't care. It is that you are ONE PERSON and there are only so many hours, and when it comes down to writing the piece or chasing the follow-up, you write the piece. Every time. As you should.

That is a staffing problem. It has never been a motivation problem.

In Dallas we found the same thing twenty times

At the Mastermind we ran hot seats. Every person stood up in front of the room and explained what he was building and where he was stuck.

I expected to hear about strategy. Offers. Positioning. Content.

What we actually found, over and over, was a room full of talented people whose businesses were leaking out of the back. Buyer lists nobody maintained. Follow-up nobody sent. Warm relationships going cold because there were not enough hours in the week.

Nobody in that room had a knowledge gap.

They had a CAPACITY gap.

There is a difference and the difference is worth a fortune.

So point it at the right job

Here is the rule, and I want you to write it down.

Never let it write. Always let it work.

The robot does not touch your voice, your story, your opinion or your name. Not one sentence. Ever.

The robot handles the work you were never going to get to anyway:

→ It knows who bought from you, when, and what they bought.
→ It notices when somebody who used to open everything has gone quiet.
→ It drafts the follow-up that has been sitting undone for six months, and YOU send it in your own words.
→ It keeps the list of people who told you they got a result, so you can go back and ask.

None of that requires the robot to be charming. It only requires it to be tireless.

And that is the trade nobody is making. They gave the machine the one job that needs a soul, and kept for themselves the one job that needs a filing cabinet.

Flip it.

The one-person company is now a real company

Understand what this actually means, because it is bigger than a productivity tip.

For most of the history of building online, the ceiling on a solo operator was not talent. It was the back office. At some point you either hired people or you stopped growing.

That ceiling just moved.

You can now run the operational side of a real business by yourself, without a payroll, without a manager, and without becoming the kind of person who spends his whole week administrating instead of creating.

Which frees you up to do the only two things that were ever going to make you rich anyway:

Write like a human. Take care of the people who already bought.

The tool is new. The strategy is 200 years old.

Sample two Shorter, in your history mode

The one with the dead man in it.

Different mode, to show range. This is the move you make when you put Caesar or Joe Girard in the middle of a business argument.

THE MAN WHO HIRED BETTER MEN THAN HIMSELF

Andrew Carnegie could not make steel. That was never the point.

Andrew Carnegie did not know how to make steel.

Read that again, because it is one of the strangest facts in American business. The man whose name is on the steel industry could not have run a furnace to save his life. He was not an engineer. He was not a chemist. He was not a metallurgist.

He wrote his own epitaph, and this is what it says:

"Here lies a man who knew how to enlist in his service better men than himself."

Not a man who worked harder than everyone. Not a man who knew more than everyone.

A man who knew how to get the work OUT of other people.

Now think about how the average creator online operates today.

He does everything. He writes the post, edits the video, answers the emails, chases the invoice, updates the spreadsheet, builds the landing page, and somewhere in there tries to have an original thought. Then he wonders why he has been stuck at the same level for two years.

He is not lazy. He is the opposite of lazy. That is the problem.

→ He has made himself the bottleneck in his own business and called it work ethic.

Carnegie understood something the modern solo operator refuses to accept: your output is capped by what you personally touch. Every job you keep is a job that will eventually stop you.

Here is the difference, and it is the only reason I am writing this today.

Carnegie needed capital and a payroll to escape that trap. He needed buildings and managers and thousands of men. For most of history, that was the price of admission, and it is exactly why so few people ever got out of the trap.

That price has collapsed.

You can now hand off the unglamorous half of your business without hiring a single person. Not the writing. Not the thinking. Not your voice. Nobody should ever touch those, and anybody telling you to automate your personality is selling you a fast route to being forgotten.

But the follow-up, the records, the reminders, the hundred small jobs that quietly decide whether a customer stays or leaves? Those were always meant to be somebody else's job.

You just never had a payroll.

Now you do.

Carnegie's genius was knowing which work was his.

That is still the whole game.

Go find out which work is yours.

Then put down everything else.

- Dino Anthony

Your voice

What I hear when I read you.

Not to tell you about yourself. To show you what I was aiming at, so you can tell me where I missed.

  • You open on the enemy, not on yourself

    The community emails open warm, My friends. The Substack pieces usually skip the greeting and open cold on a type of person doing it wrong.

  • Short. Then shorter. Then one that runs

    One to three sentences, and the single-line paragraph is the emphasis tool. Then one sprawling sentence breaks the run, and you snap back.

  • Capitals are the shout

    Six to ten a piece, always on the line carrying the argument. Arrows mark a consequence. Labelled interjections do the turns: REALITY:, QUESTION TO READER:. Typos stay in. You ship fast and you do not polish.

  • You swear at ideas, never at the reader

    Always censored, never in a closing, and usually with a wink right after it. The sign-offs go the other way entirely.

  • You punch at a type, never at a person

    Dorks, noobs, hustle bros, mental midgets. In everything I read you have never named and attacked a real human being.

  • Two registers, and this is the useful part

    The 2021 material reads like a different man from the 2026 newsletter: no profanity in sixty-eight thousand characters, epigraphs instead of jabs, and a two-word command to close. Knowing both is what lets somebody hit the right one on purpose.

  • You tell people not to buy

    And you never apply pressure, on the grounds that needing pressure means the product is the problem. A ghostwriter who missed that would wreck the paid pitch in one line.

  • Never an em-dash

    Never a numbered listicle headline. Never hustle, grind, or game-changer. There are none of those anywhere above.

First 30 days

What happens if you hand me the keys.

Read every voice transcript, workshop recording and product in the library and build an idea bank with the raw quote attached to each entry.
